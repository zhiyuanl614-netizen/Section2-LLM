To deal with the heavy operational expenditures of the ﬁfth-generation (5G) telecom service providers
(TSPs), powering 5G base stations (BSs) with renewable energy (RE) and stimulating a good interaction
between the RE-BS and the smart grid is recognized as an effective and practical solution. However, the
existing researches on the interaction between RE-BSs and the smart grid mainly study from the
perspective of the TSPs, ignoring the operational constraints of the grid. On the other hand, with the
rapid development of integrated energy systems, the integrated operation of the power and water dis-
tribution system attracts growing attention. And recently, water-cooling has been introduced as a
promising energy-saving technique for 5G-BSs, but few efforts have been spent investigating the
interaction between the BSs, the power, and the water distribution system. This paper considers the
water-cooled 5G-RE-BSs enabled by an integrated electricity-water distribution system (IEWDS), which
is managed by the integrated electricity-water service provider (IEWSP). Coordination mechanisms
between the TSP and the IEWSP are investigated, and the optimal model with two coordinated operation
modes of the IEWDS, incorporating operation constraints of both the IEWDS and the 5G-BS, is proposed.
Case studies on two IEWDS sample systems validated the beneﬁts of coordination and the effectiveness
of the proposed coordination methods.

1. Introduction
1.1. Research motivation
Energy and water support the sustainable development of hu-
man society. These two resources are highly interconnected and
mutually dependent since the production of energy requires large
volumes of water while the water withdrawal, treatment, and
distribution are usually energy-intensive processes. For example,
the statistics show that the cooling water demand of thermal po-
wer plants in the United States exceeds 40% of the total social water
intake [1], and the power consumption of the entire Chinese social
water cycle exceeds 19% of the total power consumption in the
country. Globally, approximately 75% of all industrial water with-
drawals were used for energy production in 2014 [2].
Over the past decades, the strong interdependencies between
energy and water have received much attention and developed the
concept of water-energy nexus [3]. Numerous researches on water-
energy nexus have been carried out from a macro perspective to
study such as the life-cycle water use for energy supply, the energy
use for water infrastructure, and the water/energy management or
saving techniques based on the long-term statistics or at the na-
tional level [3e6]. The recent years have seen signiﬁcant efforts
worldwide to develop integrated energy systems (IESs) [7]. The
potential synergies between electricity and water are sought to
achieve a more efﬁcient, ﬂexible, and decarbonized energy system,
which therefore suggests planning and operating power systems
and water systems in a more integrated perspective.
On the other hand, ﬁfth-generation (5G) communication has
become a hot topic in recent years. Compared with the fourth-
generation (4G), 5G promises signiﬁcant performance improve-
ments in increased data rate, reduced end-to-end latency, and
improved coverage, connectivity, security, and reliability, etc. [8,9].
Accordingly, 5G is expected to enable many emerging technologies
such as the Internet of Things (IoT), Device to Device (D2D) com-
munications, augmented/virtual reality (AR/VR), self-driving cars,

and Internet of Vehicles (IoVs) [8,10]. However, these performance
improvements bring about the obvious issues of signiﬁcantly
increased energy demand and operational expenditures, which
challenges 5G telecom service providers (TSPs) greatly and may
become the major deployment obstacle of 5G systems. Evidence
suggests that base stations (BSs) are the most energy-intensive
components of a wireless network. Nearly 80% of the energy
required by the networks is consumed at BSs [11]. The 5G
communication uses a high-frequency millimeter-wave (mmWave)
to carry data. Due to the strong absorption effect of the atmosphere
on mmWaves and the fact that mmWaves are easily blocked by
walls, foliage and even peoples' bodies, the coverage of a 5G-BS is
much smaller than that of the previous 2G/3G/4G [10,12]. The
typical density of the BS for 4G is around 8e10 BS/km2, while that
of 5G is anticipated to be 40e50 BS/km2 [12]. Besides, 5G-BSs are
generally equipped with some new high-energy-demand compo-
nents such as mmWave transceivers, fast data converters, inte-
grated
multiple-input-multiple-output
(MIMO)
antennas,
and
high-power ampliﬁers [13]. It is estimated the energy consump-
tion of each 5G-BS grows up to twice or more than that of a 4G-BS
[13]. Therefore, the total energy consumption of 5G-BSs might be 9
times or more than that of 4G to provide the same service coverage
[10]. A survey estimated that the high-density BSs and the addi-
tional equipment required by 5G will increase the TSPs’ total
network energy consumption by up to 150%e170% by 2026 [14].
To address the energy consumption issue, a variety of energy-
saving solutions are suggested to improve the energy efﬁciency
(EE) of 5G networks such as resource allocation, relaying tech-
niques, multiple radio access, cell breathing, adaptive MIMO
switching, BS sleeping, and so on [15,16]. Besides, powering BSs
with renewable energy sources such as photovoltaic (PV) panels
and wind turbines is considered a promising and practical energy-
harvesting solution to reduce the operational expenditure of TSPs
[13]. Statistics in Ref. [17] show that renewable-energy-powered
BSs (RE-BSs) have grown from 9000 in 2010e43,000 by mid-
2014. To deal with the intermittence and ﬂuctuations of renew-
able energies, RE-BSs are generally equipped with batteries or
connected to a power grid for reliable energy supply. In such cases,
the RE-BSs are powered by hybrid sources, which gives rise to the
problem of the power coordination or energy management of BSs.
Speciﬁcally, as conventional power grids evolve into the smart grids
with the main features of distributed generation, demand-side
management, and price signaling [13,18], more power coordina-
tion problems appear since the RE-BS connected to a smart grid
may act as a prosumer to participate in the energy trading and
demand-side response programs with the smart grid.
In addition to the above-mentioned energy-saving solutions,
water-cooling has been introduced to reduce the cooling costs of
BSs [19]. Compared with traditional air cooling, water-cooling has
the advantages of higher heat transfer coefﬁcients, low acoustic
noise, environmental friendliness, and a high possibility of heat
reuse [20]. Although water-cooling for BSs has not been widely
applied in commercial applications, it is gaining more and more
attraction. For example, in 2018, Nokia has cooperated with Finnish
telecom operator Elisa and power system provider Efore to deploy
the world's ﬁrst water-cooled 5G-BS in Helsinki, Finland [21].
Moreover, there are many successful applications of water-cooling
for data centers [22,23], which provide excellent references for the
design and operation of water-cooled BSs. In this work, cooling
water tanks are installed for BSs and supplied by the WDS to ach-
ieve circulative cooling and water demand regulation.
With the RE sources, batteries, and water tank installed, the 5G-
BS is essentially a small-scale integrated water-energy system. It
acts as a distributed energy-prosumer to the power grid and a
ﬂexible water load to the WDS. Accordingly, the interdependencies
among the 5G-BS, the power grid, and the WDS are necessary to be
recognized, and this is the motivation behind the current work.
1.2. Literature review
1.2.1. Coordinated operation of power systems and water systems
Researches on the coordinated operation of power systems and
water systems are brieﬂy reviewed as follows.
An optimal power-water ﬂow model of an integrated electricity-
water distribution system (IEWDS) considering network con-
straints of both power distribution systems (PDSs) and water dis-
tribution systems (WDSs), and the pump coupling is proposed in
Ref. [24]. In Ref. [25], an optimal model for operating an IEWDS is
proposed to minimize the operation cost of the integrated
electricity-water service provider (IEWSP) and enhance the wind
power accommodation. An analytical framework based on time-
series optimal power and water ﬂow simulation is developed in
Ref. [26] to analyze the interdependencies between power systems
and WDSs. In Ref. [27], the ﬂexibility offered by WDSs to the power
systems is studied, and a day-ahead power system dispatch model
integrating the energy regulating capacity of WDSs is proposed.
Based on this, the authors of [27] further propose a coordinated
operation model for the IEWDS incorporating the ﬂexibility pro-
vided by water treatment and desalination plants in Ref. [28]. A
dispatch method for a combined electricity-water supply system is
proposed and applied to smart communities in Ref. [29], whereas
the dispatch model with WDS constraints proposed in Ref. [30]
studies the optimal operation of islanded water-energy microgrids.
In addition to the mentioned work [24e30] focusing on the linkage
between electricity and water, the interdependencies between the
water, thermal and electrical energy are studied with an island
electricity-water-heat microgrid model proposed in Ref. [31], the
IES model composed of electricity, heating, and water distribution
systems is presented to improve the wind power accommodation
in Ref. [32], and the coordinated operation for an integrated water
and energy system including electricity, gas, and water distribution
systems is investigated in Ref. [33].
Although existing studies have made some contributions to-
wards the coordinated operation of power systems and water
systems, 5G-BSs and their operation ﬂexibilities are not incorpo-
rated in coordinated operation modeling, and therefore in-
terdependencies among the 5G-BS, the power grid, and the WDS
are not addressed.
1.2.2. Interaction between RE-BSs and smart grids
Existing studies on the interaction between RE-BSs and smart
grids are brieﬂy reviewed in this section.
Smart gird enabled mobile networks are considered in Ref. [34]
where energy from the grid and RE sources in each BS are assumed
to be shared among the BSs, and an optimization problem for joint
BS operation and power distribution is formulated to minimize the
on-grid power consumption. In Ref. [35], RE-BSs are equipped with
batteries and allowed to sell energy to a smart grid. In Ref. [36], the
RE-BS with a dual-battery architecture is studied and the joint
scheme for user association and smart grid energy transfer is pro-
posed. In Refs. [37,38], the BS utilizes its harvested RE and stored
energy to provide ancillary services to a smart grid by responding
to the requests of increasing or decreasing the on-grid power.
Accordingly, energy management strategies for BS on/off switching
and activating/deactivating of radio resource blocks are proposed in
Refs. [37,38], respectively.
However, all the above studies in Refs. [34e38] investigated the
interaction between RE-BSs and smart grids mainly from the
perspective of communication networks or TSPs. They focus on the
optimal operation of BSs, and the smart grids are treated as ideal

1.3. Contributions
In this work, we consider the water-cooled 5G-BS with RE source
(abbreviated as 5G-BS below) embedded in an IEWDS. The novelty
of this work is that we highlight the interaction between the 5G-BS
and the IEWDS from the perspective of energy & water manage-
ment. To our best knowledge, such interaction has not been
investigated in the literature.
In contrast to the previous work in Refs. [34e38], we investigate
the interaction between the BS and the IEWDS from the perspective
of energy management rather than the communication service.
Although there are some recent studies on 5G in the ﬁeld of power
and energy system (PES) such as [12,39], they focus on the 5G
enabled PES or exploring the application potential of 5G techniques
in PES.
To address the energy & water interaction between the 5G-BS
and the IEWDS, the cooperation mechanisms between the TSP and
the IEWSP are developed, and the corresponding optimal model,
incorporating detailed operation constraints of both the IEWDS and
the 5G-BS, are formulated.
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
(1) An advanced water-cooled 5G-BS is modeled for energy & water
management. The water-cooled 5G-BS with RE sources, bat-
tery, and water storage is modeled as a small-scale integrated
electricity-water system from the perspective of energy &
water management.
(2) The energy & water interaction between the 5G-BS and the
IEWDS is studied and the coordination mechanisms are pro-
posed. Two possible win-win operation modes of the IEWDS
are developed to explore the coordination mechanisms be-
tween the TSP and the IEWSP. The ﬁrst mode focuses on the
coordination between the IEWSP and the TSP, and the second
one is a dual coordination mode, which features the coor-
dination between the IEWSP and the upper-level grid, and
the coordination between the IEWSP and the TSP.
(3) The optimal model to address the coordination mechanisms
between the TSP and the IEWSP is formulated. The coordina-
tion mechanisms are conducted by an optimal model with
different objectives and detailed operation constraints of
both the 5G-BS and the IEWDS. The SOCP relaxation and
piecewise linearization techniques are applied to reformu-
late the proposed model into a tractable mixed-integer sec-
ond-order cone programming (MISOCP) problem.
2. IEWDS and 5G-BS
2.1. Interdependent structure
The IEWDS embedded with 5G-BSs includes two service pro-
viders, i.e., the IEWSP and the TSP, as shown in Fig. 1. The IEWSP
owns the PDS, the WDS, electrical pumps, PV stations, and wind
farms, and is responsible for their operation and maintenance. The
TSP has the ownership of all the 5G-BSs in the IEWDS as well as the
operation and maintenance authority.
The 5G-BS is mainly composed of the communication devices,
the RE source, the battery, and the water-cooling system (WCS)
along with the controllable water tank [19,22], as shown in Fig.1(a).
The water tank can be installed inside the BS to provide an on-site
water supply, or outside the BS to supply water to multiple BSs
within an area. Note that the 5G-BS are supposed to be able to feed
surplus electricity back to the PDS, and a similar bidirectional water
ﬂow between the 5G-BS and the WDS is assumed.
The power demands of the 5G-BS include the transmitting po-
wer of the communication devices, the ﬁxed power demand of the
BS, and the power supplied to the WCS. The power demands can be
supplied by the purchased power from the IEWSP, the RE source,
and the BS battery. The transmitting power is the power required to
provide services to the telecom users of the BS. Based on the test
results of [40], the transmitting power of 5G-BSs is nearly propor-
tional to the communication load. The ﬁxed power is the basic
power needed to operate a 5G-BS, which does not vary with the
communication load. The power supplied to the WCS is used to
circulate cooling water and cool the energy-intensive 5G devices of
the BS.
As shown in Fig. 1(b), the IEWSP purchases electricity and water
from the upper-level power system and the reservoir/water plant
to satisfy the power and water demand throughout the IEWDS,
respectively. Electrical pumps are the main coupling facilities be-
tween the PDS and the WDS, which consume power to boost the
hydraulic pressure of the entire or partial WDS. In Fig. 1(b), the

2.2. Coordination mechanisms
When the IEWSP and the TSP operate independently, the BS
battery is only used for the emergency power supply of the telecom
equipment and the BS tank is responsible for replenishing the
water loss during the cooling water circulation. Considering that
power emergency rarely occurs and water loss is generally negli-
gible, these two kinds of storage equipment are almost idle in the
normal operation of the IEWDS.
To make full use of the BS batteries and water tanks and to
create synergies between the operations of the IEWDS and 5G-BSs,
two coordination mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 2., are developed in
this work. The ﬁrst mechanism (Fig. 2(a)) focuses on the coordi-
nation between the IEWSP and the TSP; and the second one
(Fig. 2(b)) is a dual coordination mode, which features the coordi-
nation between the IEWSP and the upper-level grid, and the co-
ordination between the IEWSP and the TSP.
2.2.1. Mechanism 1
As shown in Fig. 2(a), Mechanism 1 assumes that the control
authority of the BS battery and BS tank is transferred from the TSP
to the IEWSP, i.e., BS batteries and BS tanks are dispatchable re-
sources to the IEWSP. Under this mechanism, the TSP provides the
BS battery and BS tank to the IEWSP in exchange for service
compensation, and the IEWSP has the potential to lower its oper-
ation cost by utilizing these dispatchable resources. Therefore, a
win-win situation may be achieved.
2.2.2. Mechanism 2
Mechanism 2 is a dual coordination mode, as shown in Fig. 2(b).
Besides the coordination between the IEWSP and the TSP, which is
similar to Mechanism 1, the coordination between the IEWSP and
the upper-level grid is expected. The upper-level grid sends the
daily tie-line power curve to the IEWDS to inform its desired tie-
line power, and the IEWSP tries to operate the PDS, the WDS, and
the BS batteries and tanks coordinately to follow this desired curve.
By doing this, the IEWSP is compensated by low-priced electricity
from the upper-level grid. On the contrary, the IEWSP will be
charged a penalty for the deviation in the actual tie-line power
curve from the desired one.
3. Problem formulation
In this section, the above two coordinated mechanisms will be
formulated as two optimal problems. The IEWSP is the decision-
maker for both problems. Different objectives are designed for
the two mechanisms, while the same constraints, including all the
operational constraints of the PDS, the WDS, the coupling facilities
between the PDS and the WDS, and 5G-BSs, are satisﬁed.

5.2. Two coordinated operation modes
The four cases as shown in Table 3 are developed to illustrate the
two proposed cooperation mechanisms between the TSP and the
IEWSP.
5.2.1. Operation-mode 1
The effectiveness of operation-mode 1 is investigated in this
subsection by comparing the results of Case 1 and Case 2. In Case 1,
the IEWSP solves an optimal operation problem to minimize its
operation cost without considering the TSP's service. It is supposed
that the BS battery is only used for the emergency power supply of
the 5G-BS and does not participate in the daily dispatch. The 5G-BS
mainly obtains power from the PDS and its on-site RE source. In
Case 2, the IEWDS and the 5G-BSs are operated in a coordinated
way. The TSP provides the BS battery and the regional BS water tank
as dispatchable resources to the IEWSP, and the corresponding
operation costs for the dispatchable resources are compensated by
the IEWSP with a contracted compensation coefﬁcient.
The daily operation costs of the IEWSP and the compensations
to the TSP in Cases 1 and 2 are shown in Table 4. It can be observed
that both the IEWSP and the TSP can beneﬁt from the coordinated
operation. Compared with Case 1, the IEWSP's operation cost is
reduced by $103.08, and the TSP receives an additional compen-
sation of $184.94 in Case 2.
The tie-line powers in Case 1 and Case 2 over the 24 h are
depicted in Fig. 7. In Case 2, an obvious increase of tie-line power
during the valley price period (22:00e8:00), as well as a decrease
during the peak price period (8:00e22:00), can be observed. This is
because in Case 2, the battery and water tanks are dispatchable to
the IEWSP owing to the coordinated operation. The IEWSP in Case 2
can choose to purchase power from the external grids or to
compensate the power supply by the TSP, and therefore achieve a
reduction in the electricity bill.
Besides, the proposed operation-mode 1 used in Case 2 is also
helpful to accommodate the surplus RE powers in the 5G-BS. There
is no other way for the TSP to accommodate its surplus RE powers
but to store in the BS batteries in Case 1, which results in the RE
curtailment of 627.7 kW. While in Case 2, the surplus RE powers are
allowed to transmit to the IEWDS, and it is found that all the RE
powers are accommodated and there is no RE curtailment in both
the IEWDS and the 5G-BSs.
To analyze the role of the BS battery and the regional BS water
tank throughout the day, more results are shown in Figs. 8e10. The
total charging/discharging power for all the BS batteries in Case 2 is

pump power in Case 1 and Case 2, as well as the total inﬂow/
outﬂow rate for all the BS water tanks in Case 2, are shown in Figs. 9

As shown in Fig. 8, the BS batteries are in discharging states
during the peak price period (8:00e22:00) and in charging states
during the valley price period (22:00e8:00). That is, the BS batte-
ries act as power sources to the IEWDS during 8:00e22:00 to
reduce the power purchased by the IEWSP from the external grid
with a high cost. For example, at time-period 19 when the power
load of the IEWDS is high (see Fig. A2(a)), it is found that the dis-
charging power of the BS batteries reaches the peak value of
0.45 MW, accounts for 12.82% of the tie-line power at the same
time. While after the time-period 22, the BS batteries begin to be
charged with low-cost electricity and guarantee that their energy
stored at the end of the day is not less than the initial value.
Meanwhile, compared with Case 1, the variable-speed pump of
the IEWDS in Case 2 can make a better speed-adjustment strategy
to reduce power consumption owing to the additional water stor-
age capacity provided by the BS tanks. For example, during the
time-period 2e3 and 22e24 (the valley price period), the pump
power in Case 2 increases (see Fig. 9), and the inﬂow rate of the BS
tanks increases accordingly (see Fig. 10), which indicates that the
pump in Case 2 pressurizes the WDS and charges the BS tanks with

pump in Case 1 has to consume much electricity to satisfy the high
water demand (see Fig. A2(a)); while in Case 2, since some water

6. Conclusion
This paper highlights the interaction between the 5G-BSs and
the IEWDS and investigates the possible win-win cooperation
mechanism between the TSP and IEWSP.
An optimal model for the coordinated operation with detailed
operation constraints of the 5G-BS and the IEWDS is proposed and
two modes for the operation of the IEWDS coordinating with the
TSP and the upper-level grid are further developed. The proposed
model is reformulated into a tractable MISOCP problem with the
techniques of SOCP relaxation and piecewise linearization.
Case studies are carried out with two test IEWDSs. Four cases
under the two operation modes are simulated and compared, as
well as sensitivity and adaptability studies are conducted. Simula-
tion results demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed
optimal model and its solution method, and more importantly,
recognized the mutual beneﬁts for both the TSP and the IEWSP
from the coordinated operation between the IEWDS and 5G-BSs.
