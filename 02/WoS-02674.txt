A B S T R A C T  
Increased renewable energy deployment in places like California have led to more frequent curtailment of these 
resources when their generation exceeds simultaneous demand for electricity, resulting in wasted opportunities 
for electric grid decarbonization. Public potable water distribution systems, which account for approximately 
1.2% of statewide electricity demand, may be able to shift the timing of their operations by filling their storage 
tanks when renewable energy is prevalent to help the California energy grid reduce renewables curtailment, grid 
emissions, and peak energy demand. We simulate the water demand, pumping, and energy demands of 702 
public water distribution systems serving 84% of Californian residents under eight different energy pricing and 
emissions minimization scenarios. We find that the potable water distribution sector in California has the flex­
ibility to shift its energy demands by up to 1071 GWh annually, reduce renewable energy curtailment by up to 
68%, reduce the net demand peak by up to 321 MW, and avoid 330,627mTCO2e of emissions. The scenarios that 
led to the greatest load shifting and emissions reductions included time-of-use energy rates that incentivize 
consumption during the middle of the day without a demand charge. The findings of this work not only highlight 
the potential of the water sector to help meet future energy and emissions goals, but also how the energy sector 
can best incentivize this shift.   
1. Introduction 
To prevent the worst of global climate change impacts, the United 
Nations have set goals to reduce carbon emissions by 45% by 2030 and 
to zero net emissions by 2050. In spite of these ambitious goals, coun­
tries around the world are falling short of their emissions targets (United 
Nations, 2021). The United States is currently the second largest 
greenhouse gas emitting country in the world, but states like California 
have taken on aggressive targets to reduce their emissions and increase 
their consumption of renewable resources (Ge et al., 2020). California 
aims to reach 100 percent renewable zero carbon energy by 2045. 
The ongoing transition from historically fossil fuel powered electric 
grids toward increased integration of renewable and low carbon energy 
sources has created new challenges for electric grid operation (Denholm 
and Hand, 2011). Some renewable energy sources are variable and 
intermittent, resulting in potential misalignment between the timing of 
renewable energy generation and electricity demand. The addition of 
more renewable energy sources in California each year have increased 
the frequency of occasions where anticipated generation exceeds the 
demand for electricity at that same time resulting in oversupply. To 
prevent over generation, excess energy is frequently curtailed by 
reducing output to match demand (USEIA, 2021). In 2020, California 
curtailed more than 1500 Gigawatt-hours (GWh) of solar and wind 
generation due to projected supply exceeding demand (CAISO, 2021a). 
Increased deployment of renewable energy in the future is likely to 
exacerbate this problem resulting in wasted opportunities for grid 
decarbonization. Without massive investments in energy storage or 
adjusting energy demands to match the renewable supply, California 
will not be able to reach their 100 percent renewable energy goal. 
As more countries world-wide strive to make aggressive carbon re­
ductions by increasing their reliance on renewable energy, mismatched 
generation and demand timing will likely limit the ability to integrate 
sufficient renewable resources to meet emissions goals. In order to 
minimize renewables curtailment, multiple solutions have been pro­
posed including storing energy that would otherwise have been cur­
tailed and demand response, which shifts demand to utilize renewable

resources when they are available (Zohrabian et al., 2021). One form of 
demand response, load shifting, moves energy load to alternate time 
periods and has emerged as a method to increase grid reliability and 
assimilation of renewable resources (Park et al., 2019; Wimmler et al., 
2017). Sectors that have existing flexible production schedules, batch 
processes or storage, such as plug-in electric vehicles and water pumping 
and storage, have great potential to perform demand response in the 
short term (Aghajanzadeh et al., 2015; Kernan et al., 2017; Park et al., 
2019; Van Triel and Lipman, 2020; Amaral Lopes et al., 2020; Zohrabian

In spite of identifying the promise of the water sector as a load shift 
resource, research in this space has been limited to single entity case 
studies and there have not yet been any attempts to quantify the broader 
scale of this opportunity or the challenges, such as in California as a 
whole using historical energy rates (Kernan et al., 2017; Oikonomou 
et al., 2018; Park et al., 2019; Amaral Lopes et al., 2020; Sowby, 2023). 
Mobilizing the water sector to shift when it consumes energy may 
require changing how energy utilities incentivize energy consumption 
(Zohrabian et al., 2021). Historically, energy pricing structures have 
been built for a fossil fuel based market. As alternative energy sources 
enter the mix, old incentives may no longer be achieving their intended 
goals. 
Utilizing a mass-balance model of water distribution system opera­
tions, paired with a pump scheduling algorithm, this study seeks to es­
timate the statewide potential for load shifting resources from 
California’s water distribution systems under different goals and pricing 
scenarios. The objective of this research is to estimate how much energy 
demand flexibility is realistically available from California’s water dis­
tribution, the impacts of this flexibility on grid emissions and renewable 
integration, and identify how the electricity sector could most effec­
tively incentivize beneficial load shifting through their rate structures. 
While using California as a large case study of many water utilities, 
this research demonstrates the potential demand flexibility of any water 
system that has water storage facilities and how the water sector in other 
states or countries could play a sizable role toward greening the energy 
sector and achieving global sustainable development goals. The findings 
of this research are useful to water suppliers aiming to reduce their 
energy costs and carbon footprints as well as to energy suppliers and 
energy rate designers seeking to improve grid reliability and integrate 
greater quantities of renewable resources on the grid. 
2. Background 
Water distribution systems are responsible for reliably delivering 
safe water to end users at an adequate pressure, accomplished through a 
combination of pumps, storage reservoirs, and gravity. Like an electrical 
system, supply and demand must always be balanced to maintain system 
pressure. Closed pressure zones, which do not contain any water storage, 
may require variable speed pumps which ramp their flows up and down 
to match the real-time water demands in that zone. Pumping too little 
water will drop the pressure, and pumping too much can over pressurize 
the system, leading to damage. For this reason, many parts of a water 
system are in open pressure zones, which contain storage facilities. In 
these open zones, pumping water at a rate greater than demand fills the 
water reservoir. Turning pumps off or operating below demand causes 
the water storage tanks to drain and provide pressure to the system. 
From the standpoint of the flexibility of pump energy timing, closed 
pressure zones have no flexibility; the only way to modify the load shape 
of these pumps would be to modify the timing of when end users 
consume water. In open pressure zones, the primary factor that de­
termines flexibility is the amount of water storage available relative to 
water demand and pumping capacity. With enough storage volume, 
pumps could be run to fill the reservoirs at one time of day and turned off 
at another time of day to meet demand using the water retained in the 
storage reservoirs. 
How well the flexibility within the water sector aligns with the needs 
of the electric grid depends on the electric rate structure. Electricity can 
be the single largest operating cost of a water distribution system and 
pumps will be operated to minimize these costs to the extent that they 
are still able to reliably supply safe water. Electric rates are often made 
up of two primary factors: a consumption charge (per kWh), and a de­
mand charge for peak consumption interval (per kW). Consumption 
charges account for the actual energy used and demand charges account 
for the generation capacity required to meet the peak demands of the 
system (Sherwood et al., 2016). High demand charges may encourage 
utilities to pump at constant rates to keep any one moment of demand at 
a minimum. Time-of-use (TOU) consumption charges, with variable 
pricing dependent on the time of the day, may encourage water utilities 
to shift when they consume energy to take advantage of low energy 
prices or avoid high costs at a different time (Faruqui and Sergici, 2013). 
Many electric utilities offer several different rate structures for a con­
sumer to select from, which may be comprised of different types of de­
mand and consumption rates. 
Due to the flexibility of water distribution systems and depending on 
the energy rate structures available to them, the water sector may be 
able to permanently change its electric load shape as well as perform 
dynamic load shifting (Menke, et al., 2016; Kernan et al., 2017). Modern 
fixed TOU energy rates provided by electric utilities typically encourage 
energy consumption either when renewables are abundant in the middle 
of the day and/or discourage consumption during peak energy demands 
in the early evening. The energy rate motivates users to alter when they 
use energy resulting in a permanent change to their energy load profile, 
known as load shaping (Faruqui and Sergici, 2013). Water utilities may 
also have the potential to participate in dynamic load shifting and 
respond to the daily needs of the electric grid if given access to 
real-time-pricing (RTP) (Kernan et al., 2017). RTP represents the actual 
cost of energy supply operations at any given time of day due to the 
combination of energy resources (fossil and renewable) on the grid at 
that moment. Cheaper prices typically coincide with a high prevalence 
of renewable resources on the grid and more expensive prices during 
peak energy demand periods because of expensive peaking plants being 
brought online to meet peak demand (Beck and Martinot, 2004). If there 
is an oversupply of energy available, grid operators will curtail renew­
able resources and sell energy at very low or even negative prices to 
balance the grid (CAISO, 2017). Water system operation is often fore­
casted and planned hours to days in advance, so unlocking the full po­
tential of dynamic load shifting would likely require sophisticated 
decision support software that could help operators co-optimize these 
dynamic prices with the other operational needs of the system. 
The water distribution sector is listed as one of the most promising 
short-term opportunities for mitigating oversupply of renewable energy 
for several reasons (Park et al., 2019). Water storage and pumping 
infrastructure is already built and integrated with SCADA control sys­
tems that could utilize software to make operational changes driven by 
price signals. Additionally, operators are relatively indifferent to when 
pumps operate as long as they are able to maintain system reliability, 
which is not the case in other sectors such as residential, where 
behavioral barriers can limit flexibility. However, risk aversion, hourly 
and seasonal variability in water demand, complex energy rate tariffs, 
and poor belief in the economic advantages are all challenges that have 
been identified as preventing the water sector from more fully partici­
pating in demand response programs (Sowby, 2021).

water to 84% of Californian residents, responsible for water quality, 
storage, and pressurization of water distribution systems. Simplified 
hydraulic models were created for each individual utility based on 
publicly reported data and assumptions from literature. The model was

3.2. Simplified hydraulic model 
Typical hydraulic models are used to represent detailed water dis­
tribution systems including pipe networks, pressure zones, storage fa­
cilities, pumping stations, and demand nodes. These models can predict 
hydraulic operation of a water system including pressures, energy con­
sumption, pumping rates, and hydraulic head throughout the modeled 
region (Robinson et al., 2012). In order to represent the potable water 
distribution systems of all utilities throughout California it was neces­
sary to make assumptions and simplifications to traditional hydraulic 
models. These assumptions include: 1) water demand and distribution 
are represented by a single demand center and single pressure zone, 2) 
water storage is represented by a single aggregated tank volume within 
the same pressure zone, 3) pipe networks are ignored, and 4) pumping is 
represented by a single aggregated pump station, which can operate at 
variable or continuous speeds. 
The simplified model for each utility is shown in Fig. 1, which 
highlights the primary components of the model as well as the static 
characteristics applied to each component such as total storage volume 
(Vmax) and maximum pump rate (Fmax). These characteristics were 
estimated for each utility using data such as individual utility water sales 
and water storage data extracted from multiple years of California 
electronic annual reports (eAR) (CA SWRCB, 2023). Additional details 
about the data used for each modeling component and the methods used 
to generate them can be found in the supplemental information (SI) 
section.

Equation (1). A pump scheduling algorithm was used to optimize the 
system such that costs were minimized, taking into account variable 
energy prices throughout the day and different pricing schemes. The 
algorithm allowed for changes in pump timing and rate to minimize the 
cost while constraining the solutions to satisfy the mass balance in

starting at the final time-step and evaluating all possible decisions in 
reverse until the current time-step is met. An initial penalty is provided 
for any scenario that would result in final storage levels less than the 
initial storage level (assumed as the average of Vmax and Vmin). The total 
performance of potential decisions was evaluated as the accumulation of

3.4. Scenarios 
To understand the energy demand flexibility of the water sector and 
evaluate optimum energy cost operations, multiple signals were 
assessed as unique scenarios. A baseline, that has a flat consumption rate 
and no demand charge will establish the flexibility of specific water 
systems and the overall water sector when introduced to more complex 
motivational price signals (scenarios 1–8). The potential for energy 
flexibility was calculated as the difference between the simulated energy 
demand in the baseline scenario and each of scenarios 1–8. Table 1 
summaries the various rates under each scenario for reference. Addi­
tional details about each scenario are available in the SI section. 
For real-time pricing (wholesale electricity prices) and emissions 
scenarios 7 and 8, which will vary for every hour of the year and by 
location, we chose to represent the diversity of the signals without 
substantially increasing the complexity of the input data by grouping the 
water systems into Northern and Southern California. These regions 
generally correspond to the service territories of Pacific Gas and Electric 
(PG&E) in the North and Southern California Edison (SCE) and San 
Diego Gas and Electric (SDG&E) in the South. Any water systems outside 
of these service territories were assigned to one of these regions based on 
their geographic proximity to the region. For RTP we utilized day-ahead 
market locational marginal pricing (LMP) data from the California In­
dependent System Operator in 2020. Prices for the northern region were 
represented by PG&E Demand Load aggregation point (DLAP) and pri­
ces for the southern region were represented by the average of SCE and 
SDG&E DLAPs. For our emissions optimization scenario, we utilized 
marginal operating emission rate data from California’s Self Generation 
Incentive Program (SGIP). Similarly to the LMP data, we used the PG&E 
SGIP data for our northern region, and an average of the SCE and SDG&E 
SGIP data for our southern region.

3.5. Model outputs and metrics 
Several metrics will be used to assess the system performance under 
the various scenarios as compared to the baseline including total energy 
consumption, emission intensity, net load shifted, and reduction of the 
net demand peak. These metrics assess the pricing signal’s ability to 
impact the electric grid when applied to Californian water distribution 
systems. 
3.5.1. Total energy consumption 
Assuming constant pumping efficiencies, it takes a certain amount of 
energy to deliver a given volume of water and we would expect each 
scenario to consume roughly the equivalent total quantity of energy over 
the same period. The optimization can result in water systems storing 
excess water at the end of some simulations in preparation for future 
time-steps. As a result, some combinations of water system character­
istics and energy price signals can result in select water systems poten­
tially ending the simulation with slightly more energy consumed by 
storing excess water in their storage tanks. This is accounted for by 
normalizing later results by this total energy consumption.

3.5.3. Average emission intensity, and avoided emissions 
Shifting the energy load may impact the average emission intensity 
of the water distribution sector given the variable GHG emissions of the 
electric grid at different times of the day due to the availability of 
renewable resources (Miller et al., 2022). Total GHG emissions and 
average emissions intensity will be calculated as in Equation (3) by 
multiplying the energy profiles under each scenario by emissions factors 
according to the appropriate hour and month and then summing the 
emissions and dividing by the total energy consumption. Average 
emissions intensities highlight the fraction of the grid-wide emissions 
that can be attributed to the energy consumption (which comes from a 
variety of sources) of the water distribution sector. The average GHG 
emissions factors by hour and month for California used in this analysis 
can be found in the SI section. 
We also considered marginal emissions intensities because they 
better represent the consequential impact of load shifting and avoided 
emissions. We calculated avoided emissions under each scenario by 
multiplying the change in hourly electricity demand (baseline minus 
scenarios 1–8) by hourly marginal emissions factors as in Equation (4). 
This accounts for the emissions intensity of the last unit of energy that 
would have been produced to meet demand. By reducing demand when 
marginal emissions intensities are high, the last units of energy are not 
required and those emissions are avoided. Avoided emissions account 
for the impact of shifting water distribution demands on the electric grid 
(which changes the last unit of produced energy), i.e. how the water 
sector can change overall grid emissions.

3.5.4. Net demand peak reduction 
Net demand peak is the maximum net energy demand (total energy 
demand minus wind and solar generation) in a single interval each day. 
This quantity represents the total nonrenewable capacity that was 
required that day and can dictate the number and size of fossil fuel 
generated power plants required to meet energy demand in California 
(CEC, 2021). Reducing the net energy demand peak may mean greater 
grid reliability as the grid can have greater capacity to handle demands 
and reduce the likelihood of rolling blackouts or insufficient energy 
supplies during high demand periods. A reduction in the net demand 
peak may also mean reducing grid emissions since peak demand is often 
served by less-efficient and more emitting ‘peaking power plants’ 
(Torriti, 2015). Regular peak energy demand has been shifting later in 
the day since 2011 with the summer total demand peak occurring on 
average around 6pm in 2020 and the net demand peak occurring after 
7pm (CEC, 2021). This metric of impact will be assessed at the statewide

perspective, in 2020 the annual California grid-wide instantaneous peak 
demand occurred in August at 47,121 MW with typical daily net demand 
peaks throughout the year around 30,000 MW (CAISO, 2021b).

4.1. Utility level 
At the individual utility level, we saw that the ability to load shift was 
largely limited by storage volume relative to water demand, the total

The ability for an individual utility to load shift is limited by their 
storage volume. In Fig. 4, during the relatively high costs of energy near 
7pm (panel ‘B’), the MNWD optimization avoided pumping (panel ‘C’) 
and used sizeable storage (panel ‘D’) to meet water demand until energy

were at their lowest for the week, reaching as low as $0/kWh. MNWD 
took advantage of Sunday’s low rates and used their storage to pump 
almost constantly throughout the afternoon until prices increased in the 
evening when they were able to shut off their pumps and meet water 
demand by draining their stored water. During this same period, the City 
of Dixon also increased their pumping, but could not take advantage of 
the entire low pricing period given their limited storage. Panel ‘D’ of 
Fig. 4 shows Dixon filing their tanks to capacity when energy prices are 
low and draining to their minimum when prices increased. We noted 
numerous utilities, shown as thin gray lines in Fig. 4, maxing out their 
tanks during low pricing periods indicating that their storage was 
limiting their ability to load shift. 
Total pumping capacity also plays a role in the ability of an indi­
vidual utility to load shift. MNWD has plenty of storage capacity, but at 
the global minimum price signal their pumps could not completely fill 
their tanks and instead they had to pump later in the week when prices 
were at a local low. We see this shown in Fig. 4 around hour 60, when 
prices reached $0/kWh and most utilities pumped at full capacity 
(provided they had storage), but most could not sufficiently fill their 
tanks during this time period and had to pump again around hour 75 
(early Monday morning) during a local low price, but outside the load- 
shifting window. 
Most utilities will shut off their pumps when facing relatively high 
price signals, but not all utilities have sufficient storage or pumping 
capacity to do this. In Fig. 4 we see the RTP signal peaking around hour 
19 (7pm) and for several utilities (thin gray lines) their pumping rate in 
panel ‘C’ remains high or even increases during this time in order to 
deliver adequate water volumes to meet demand. In cases where a water 
system has no appreciable storage, they are forced to pump to exactly 
meet demand and therefore demonstrate no capacity for load-shifting. 
Global minima and maxima prices matter, but local minima and 
maxima in rate structures can be especially impactful on utilities that 
have limited storage or pumping capacity. As seen in panels B and C of 
Fig. 4, both MNWD and Dixon pumped when local lows in the pricing 
scheme occurred. In the case of Dixon, they did not have adequate 
storage to meet demand for extended periods and were forced to pump 
whenever a local low energy price occurred even when a lower price 
would be available in a few hours. In the case of MNWD, which has 
excess storage, they could be more selective on when they pumped and 
could wait for lower pricing periods, but due to their pumping limita­
tions they could not fully take advantage of their storage capacity and 
the global minima and were still impacted by local minima prices. 
4.2. Statewide level 
Summing the energy demands for the 702 water distribution systems 
in our dataset resulted in an annual energy usage of approximately 3200 
GWh for distribution pumping, shown in Table 5, making up roughly 
1.2% of the 272,576 GWh total electrical energy consumed in California 
in 2020 (CEC, 2020). Since the water systems modeled represent 84% of 
the water distribution in California, we assume that the entire urban 
water distribution sector in California uses approximately 1.4% of the 
total statewide electricity consumption. 
The aggregated optimization results across the eight scenarios yields 
insight into the ability of the water sector as a whole to impact the 
California electric grid and statewide energy and emissions goals. 
Depending on the scenario, utilities behaved differently to minimize cost 
or minimize GHG emissions resulting in varying energy demand profiles, 
electric grid impacts, and load shifting potentials. 
4.2.1. Net load shifted 
Non-demand charge scenarios (5–8) resulted in significant shifts in 
energy loads into the curtailment window in aggregate over the entire 
year. Fig. 5 plots the annual daily average energy demand in California 
by the water distribution sector by hour of day under each optimization 
scenario. Marked on the figure as a shaded gray area is the curtailment 
window representing the hours when sizable quantities of energy are 
curtailed in California along with a dotted line representing the actual 
curtailment values for 2020. Scenarios with demand charges (1–4, solid 
lines) all resulted in minimal or negative load shifting and relatively flat 
hourly consumption rates so as to avoid being penalized for periods of 
high consumption. Scenarios 5–8, without demand charges (dashed 
lines), all resulted in positive load shifting and much more dynamic 
energy consumption over a 24-h period. These patterns held true for 
both the winter and summer periods as shown in panels ‘A’ and ‘B’ of 
Fig. 6. 
Pricing signals featuring their lowest pricing or emissions rates 
during the curtailment window with relatively high rates at all other 
times (scenarios 5&8) resulted in the greatest load-shifting. Scenario 8 
which minimized average GHG emissions resulted in the greatest annual 
net load shifted with the ability to shift approximately a third of its 
energy demand, approximately 1070 GWh, followed by scenario 5 with 
a modern TOU energy consumption rate without a demand charge. 
Scenarios 6 (RTP) and 7 (Marginal Emissions) also resulted in significant 
load-shifting, but to a lesser degree due to local low rates in the early 
morning outside the load-shifting window. 
4.2.1.1. Impact on curtailment. If we consider net load shifted as the 
potential to reduce curtailment of renewable energy, i.e. increase the 
capacity for integration of renewable energy, then statewide curtailment 
could be reduced by up to 68% under the optimized average emission 
scenario (scenario 8). In Fig. 5, California’s average daily renewable 
energy curtailment for 2020 is plotted by hour of day relative to the 
energy demand by the water sector under the various scenarios. In 2020 
the California Independent Service Operator curtailed a total of 1580 
GWh of solar and wind energy (CAISO 2021a). 
4.2.1.2. Seasonal impacts. The capacity for utilities to load-shift is 
greater in the winter than in the summer. Of the scenarios that were 
successful at load-shifting, with the exception of the average emission 
scenario 8 which performed similarly in summer and winter, their ca­
pacity to do so more than doubles in the winter months as shown in 
Table 3 and Fig. 6. One reason for this could be the different energy 
pricing schemes for summer and winter. For scenario 5 (modern TOU) 
this could be a result of a dip in energy prices during the load-shifting 
window in the winter which does not exist under the summer pricing 
scheme. For scenarios 6 and 7 (RTP and Marginal Emissions), their low 
prices are better aligned with the load-shifting window during the 
winter months whereas they skew earlier during the summer (see Fig. 2). 
The second reason for this is reduced water demand in the winter 
(largely due to reduced irrigation demands) paired with constant storage 
and pumping capacity allowing for greater flexibility to shift loads.

5. Discussion 
The results from this experiment support three major findings: 1) the 
water sector currently has the physical potential to play a role in sup­
porting a resilient and reduced carbon intensity electricity grid, 2) en­
ergy rate structures drive the water sectors capacity to influence the 
electric grid, and 3) in a changing energy market, energy rates must 
change to reflect modern goals. 
5.1. Water Sector’s potential as a grid resource 
Shifts to water distribution pumping will not solve California’s en­
ergy concerns by itself, but its potential impact is not negligible when 
compared to existing policies and incentives. The results of this work 
show that the water sector could shift up to one third of its total energy 
demand from undesirable periods into the middle of the day to promote 
increased integration of solar energy. Given that the water distribution 
sector accounts for 1.4% of the total electricity consumption in Cali­
fornia, this could mean potentially moving almost 0.5% (1275.6 GWh) 
of California’s total electricity demand into the middle of the day using 
already built infrastructure and no changes to the consumption patterns 
of individual water users. Additionally, our modeling suggests that the 
water sector could reduce the net demand peak by up to 321 MW or 
approximately a 1% reduction of the overall net demand peak of the grid 
(CAISO, 2021b). For perspective, electricity utility demand response 
programs combined statewide accounted for 3–4% of the grid adequacy

5.2. The role of energy rate structures in the water sector 
For the water sector to meet its potential as a grid resource, water 
utilities need to be appropriately incentivized. This means removing 
electricity demand charges, matching the TOU consumption charges and 
timings to grid goals, and being aware of water system limitations with 
regard to water storage capacity, pumping, and water demands. 
All scenarios with demand charges resulted in relatively flat energy 
consumption and low potential to load-shift or reduce peak demands. 
Demand charges discourage utilities from high levels of energy con­
sumption at any given time thus resulting in utilities maintaining a 
consistent level of consumption to minimize their overall demand. 
Removing demand charges allows utilities to respond to dynamic pric­
ing signals and be more responsive overall to the needs of the grid. 
Not every water utility can participate in load-shifting, but when 
faced with high energy prices (in the absence of a demand charge) the 
water sector as a whole was able to reduce pumping until prices dipped 
back down. If those pricing signals correspond to the load shifting 
window or a net demand peak (i.e. lowest prices between 9am and 4pm 
and highest between 5 and 9pm) utilities are able to perform significant 
amounts of load-shifting into the middle of the day and away from the 
early evening. To maximize this, the pricing signals need to be greater at 
all times of the day outside the load shifting window. Several of the rate 
structures we examined do this to an extent, but inconsistently with 
occasional low prices during periods of high carbon intensity and/or 
outside the load-shifting window. The modern TOU rate structure we 
examined did this during the winter, which is what allowed it to be so 
successful at load shifting during this period. 
Local minima in a TOU rate structure can have a significant impact 
on energy consumption in the water sector. Most water utilities cannot 
wait to pump until the daily minimum prices occur due to storage and 
pumping constraints. Even when incentivized to pump in the middle of 
the day, they were forced to also pump at alternative times, often in the 
early morning when local minimum prices occurred. The most obvious 
example of this is with scenarios 6 and 7 which have local minima 
around 3am. This resulted in less energy being shifted into the middle of 
the day, but greater avoided emissions. Timing of the local minima play 
a significant role in how water utilities respond to the pricing signals and 
their ability to meet electricity grid goals. 
In general, the modern TOU pricing structure (scenario 5) performed 
consistently at the top for all of the metrics we examined. Other sce­
narios, like the average emissions rate did well at shifting energy and 
avoiding emissions but performed poorly when considering grid ca­
pacity as related to the net demand peak. Depending on the goal, 
different energy rate structures performed the best. Table 5 summarizes 
the best and worst performing rate structures by the metrics used in this 
paper. 
5.3. Modern energy markets need modern rate structures 
Scenario 3, which has a legacy TOU consumption charge, resulted in 
the largest amount of load shifted out of the curtailment window. Legacy 
TOU consumption rates are still widely used by many electricity utilities 
and have their lowest prices during the night when energy demand is 
typically at its lowest. These rates were primarily designed to shift en­
ergy demand away from peak demand periods to limit the overall energy 
demand on the grid at any point in time. 
These rates made a lot of sense when the energy generated during the 
night and the day both came from the same fossil fuel-based sources and 
resulted in the same emission intensities, which is no longer true. These 
rates which used to make energy grids more resilient are breaking down 
in a new energy market. When applied to the water sector, legacy TOU 
rates resulted in grid impacts working counter to their original goals, 
demonstrating the need to remove and replace them with rates reflective 
of our changing energy market. 
5.4. Limitations 
While we believe that this study represents the best available esti­
mate to date of the demand flexibility potential of the California water 
sector, it has several important limitations. Water distribution systems 
are complex and this method simplifies utility operations in order to 
account for the 700+ public water systems in California. In the simpli­
fication of water systems, important operational constraints have been 
ignored, including water quality requirements, pressure requirements, 
and forecasting ability. 
Further research with more detail could improve the results and 
provide additional insight about the application of the principles dis­
cussed in this paper. Additionally, RTP and emissions rates are dynamic 
and would change in response to water system load shifting and pur­
chasing energy at alternative hours. If the entire water sector were to 
participate in the wholesale market (i.e. RTP) that may change the en­
ergy rates and this analysis. Likewise, more renewables being added to 
the grid as is expected to meet CA renewable energy goals, will influence 
the energy rates as well as the emissions intensities. 
For the purposes of our study we assumed a baseline energy rate 
tariff with flat energy consumption rates and no demand charges. In 
reality, water utilities are likely to be using a variety of energy rate 
tariffs, often multiple at a single utility. The purpose of assigning a 
baseline was to indicate the flexibility of the water sector, i.e. how much 
could the water distribution sector shift its energy demands when pre­
sented with different energy rate tariffs. A flat rate tariff was selected as 
the baseline because it results in a flat demand profile and operates in­
dependent of time (i.e. no matter how or when a utility consumed en­
ergy the total bill would be the same). Any of the scenarios could be used 
as a baseline and numbers recalculated using the values presented in this 
paper. 
Our optimization assumed perfect forecasting ability, which would 
not be present in the real world. Being able to perfectly forecast water 
demands and dynamic energy prices give a water system operator the 
ability to plan days ahead for low or high upcoming prices or water 
demands allowing for a minimization of cost while ensuring reliability. 
In reality, under some of the scenarios, water systems might not be 
aware of low energy prices until a few hours ahead, which will change 
how and their ability to shift their pumping demands. This limitation 
especially applies to dynamic energy tariffs such as wholesale (RTP) 
pricing and emissions minimization scenarios. For this reason, our es­
timate may be on the high side relative to a real-life operator who may 
be more cautious in the face of uncertainty.

5.5. Future exploration and what is needed 
From the findings of this research, there are several opportunities for 
extensions or additional exploration to be undertaken. A pilot applica­
tion of a handful of utilities with the ability to respond to dynamic en­
ergy prices such as RTP could be useful in validating the results and the 
flexibility of the water sector to perform load-shifting. Further, using 
what was uncovered in this research to create an ‘optimum’ rate struc­
ture for water utilities and applying the same or similar optimization 
process could help clarify the full potential, not limited to the currently 
available rate tariffs, of the water distribution network in California to 
respond to grid needs. Lastly, this research largely focused on the im­
pacts of load-shifting in the water sector on the electric grid, but did not 
explore the impacts to the distribution systems themselves such as costs, 
water quality, operational challenges, etc. Future research may consider 
what is occurring on the individual utility level and how that affects 
water distribution goals and operations. This research could consider 
questions such as which rates might be easiest for a utility to adopt, what 
operational controls are needed to optimize energy demands, and how a 
rate structure could be specifically designed for the water sector 
considering any sector specific limitations and challenges. 
This analysis focused on reducing curtailment and assigned a load- 
shifting window to 9am-4pm, but could have been shifted to examine 
another time period or address other needs of the electric grid. This 
study did not examine the impact of the various energy rate scenarios on 
grid concerns such as ramping of energy demands, which can occur 
leading up to the net demand peak. Additional analysis that examines 
how the water sector can be leveraged to meet other grid-related goals 
could be informative. 
6. Conclusion 
Potable distribution pumping in the water sector accounts for 
approximately 1.4% of the total electricity consumption in California 
annually and has the capacity to shift when it uses that energy to help 
integrate more renewable resources onto the grid, reduce curtailment, 
decrease GHG emissions, and reduce the net demand peak. We account 
for the operations of 702 public water distribution systems in California 
with simulated water demand, pumping, and energy demands under 
eight different energy pricing and emissions minimization scenarios. 
From this simulation, we find that the water sector has the flexibility to 
shift its energy demands by up to 1071 GWh annually, to reduce 
curtailment in California by up to 68%, to reduce its contribution to peak 
net demand by up to 321 MW, and/or avoid 330,627mTCO2e of emis­
sions depending on the energy rate tariff. 
The potential distribution system response depends on individual 
system’s combination of tank storage capacity, pumping capacity, and 
water demands as well as the applied energy tariff structure. Most sys­
tems had the ability to perform some load shifting with the greatest 
potential at systems with high storage volumes, low water demands, and 
high pumping capacity. Energy tariffs with demand charges resulted in 
minimal and in some cases negative load-shifting compared to the 
baseline. The tariffs without demand charges and TOU consumption 
charges that aligned low pricing with the load shifting window and 
higher prices outside the window, resulted in the most dynamic energy 
consumption, the greatest shifts in energy load, and the lowest GHG 
emissions. Depending on the objective, adjusting rate structures could 
encourage the water sector to shift when it demands energy such that it 
could better assist California in meeting future energy and emission 
targets.
