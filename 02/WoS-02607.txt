A B S T R A C T
In Japan, renewable energy is being rapidly introduced. Hence, surplus power and the required balancing
reserves will increase. In this study, to deal with this problem, we focus on a water distribution system (WDS),
especially water pumps, which can contribute to supply–demand balancing by shifting their operation. To
evaluate the current potential of water pumps in Japan, we modeled them and simulated their operation,
in which they can shift their pumping schedule to reduce surplus power and provide balancing reserves to
the power system, adhering to constraints such as the demand for water and the storage of water reservoirs.
We then developed a numerical model that simultaneously optimizes the operation of generators and water
pumps throughout Japan, and simulated their operation in 2040. The results showed that despite increasing
their power consumption, pumps shifted their pumping schedule to contribute to the entire power system and
reduced fossil generator fuel costs and renewable energy output curtailment.
1. Introduction
The introduction of variable renewable energy (VRE) power gener-
ation, such as photovoltaic (PV) and wind power (WP), is progressing
rapidly in Japan. This phenomenon was triggered by the introduction
of the Excess Electricity Purchasing Scheme for PVs launched in 2009
and the Feed-in Tariff program launched in 2012, under which electric
utilities purchase electricity generated by VRE power generation at a set
price. As of the end of FY2021, the amount of PV and WP installed was
69.35 GW and 4.58 GW, respectively (Agency for Natural Resources
and Energy, JAPAN, 2023). Compared to other countries, Japan has
a much higher rate of introducing PV systems than wind turbines.
Therefore, to make most effective use of VRE, it is necessary to utilize
daytime electricity generated by PV on sunny days, which exceeds
normal electricity demand.
In addition, the amount of electricity generated by VRE power
generators depends on weather conditions and is thus characterized by
unstable output. In the current power system, thermal power gener-
ation is mainly used to provide supply–demand adjustment power to
cope with output fluctuations of VRE generation and fluctuations in
electricity demand. The supply–demand adjustment capability in ther-
mal power generators includes a governor-free operation, also called
the primary control, in which the generator’s regulator automatically

changes the output in response to frequency fluctuations with a cycle
of up to a dozen seconds; a load frequency control (LFC), also called
the secondary control, in which instructions are sent from the feeder
control station to the power plants to control output in response to
frequency demand fluctuations with a cycle of several minutes to
several dozen minutes; and an economic load dispatching control, also
called the tertiary control, in which the economic load is distributed
to each power plant in response to frequency demand fluctuations
with a cycle of several dozen minutes or longer. As the introduction
of VRE generation increases, short-period fluctuations in VRE output
increase, meanwhile supply–demand adjustment capabilities of thermal
power generators decrease, because the number of operating thermal
power generators is decrease. As a result, there are growing concerns
about the shortage of supply–demand adjustment capability to short-
term fluctuations, especially in the LFC domain. In order to expand the
introduction of VRE, it is essential to secure LFC regulating reserves to
replace thermal power generators.
In order to effectively utilize the daytime surplus electricity, and
to secure additional LFC regulating reserves, demand response (DR)
using distributed energy resources (DER) on demand side, such as
stationary storage batteries, electric vehicles, residential heat pump
water heaters, and other demand side equipment, is expected. Many

studies have been conducted on the contribution of stationary storage
batteries (Guo et al., 2021), electric vehicles (Iwafune et al., 2019),
and heat pump water heaters (Omori et al., 2020) to power system
operation through DR. In this study, we focused on the DR using water
distribution systems (WDSs) as DER.
The WDSs in Japan require a large amount of electricity to operate
water purification facilities and pump water to high elevations, and
WDS projects account for approximately 1% of the nation’s electricity
consumption (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 2009). Water
pumps are used in the process of transporting water from rivers to
distribution reservoirs after converting it to purified water, and energy
consumption in this process accounts for a large portion of the total
energy consumption of WDSs.
Many water utilities in Japan that
consume large amounts of electricity have already introduced high-
efficiency equipment, inverter control of pumps, and storage batteries
for peak shaving, as energy conservation measures. In the future,
effective use of VRE will be required for further environmental and
energy conservation measures (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare,
2013).
In the process in which water pumps send water from the water
treatment plant to the distribution reservoirs, the operating timing of
the pumps can be changed because the distribution reservoirs are used
as energy storage devices. Therefore, By shifting the operating hours
of the pumps to daytime hours when PV generation is in surplus, it is
expected that VRE can be used more effectively. Furthermore, water
pumps can
quickly change the amount of water delivered through
inverter control.
Since power consumption can be quickly increased
or decreased, it is also possible to have a supply–demand adjustment
capability in the LFC domain for the power system operation.
From
these points, WDS has high potential to contribute to power system
operation by shifting the timing of electricity demand and providing
LFC regulating reserves.
Several studies have been conducted on the operational control and
modeling of the pumps in the WDS. As research into methods of saving
energy and reducing energy costs for water supply and distribution
systems, the optimization of determining the operation of water pumps
to minimize their one-day power consumption by modeling them as
mixed integer linear programming (MILP) was introduced (Arai et al.,
2012). In addition, a pump operation planning model for a large-scale
water distribution network (Hirano et al., 2017), minimizing the overall
operating cost of a real WDS with the flexible operation of water pumps
and in-house pumps at a water treatment plant, was proposed (Zim-
mermann et al., 2018). In addition, a model that considers the physical
constraints of the entire system was proposed (Fooladivanda and Tay-
lor, 2018). Various methods have also been proposed to contribute to
electricity supply and demand by controlling the operation of water
pumps in WDSs (Takahashi et al., 2016; Menke et al., 2016; Negishi and
Ikegami, 2019; Mkireb et al., 2019; Negishi and Ikegami, 2021). For
example, assuming a DR request to the WDS, an operation to minimize
the peak power of the grid was modeled (Takahashi et al., 2016).
In addition, assuming an incentive-type DR, a model was proposed
to minimize the net cost of the DR by subtracting the benefit of the
DR from the cost of electricity during pump operation (Menke et al.,
2016). Additionally, a method was proposed for modeling and optimiz-
ing the operation of water pumps that provide frequency regulating
power to the power system as MILP (Negishi and Ikegami, 2019). A
control method was also proposed to ensure the stable operation of
water pumps when there is uncertainty about water demand and the
provision of frequency regulating power (Mkireb et al., 2019; Negishi
and Ikegami, 2021). Furthermore, some studies have evaluated the
impact of changing the operation of water pumps for WDSs on the
system. In Refs. Imanaka and Uezu (2015) and Imanaka et al. (2016),
the impact of changing the operation of water pumps in a closed power
system on an actual island on energy conservation for the entire island
was demonstrated and evaluated.
Various research and optimization
models have been proposed to evaluate the efficiency of power con-
sumption of pumps in WDSs. However, these studies are characterized
by dealing with optimal operation for individual pumps but not for
a whole power system, dealing with the contribution through peak
cuts but not through the provision of short-term regulating reserves,
or dealing with small-scale grids but not large-scale multiple power
systems like Japan as a whole.
This study aimed to develop an optimization model that integrally
handles the DR operations using all of Japan’s WDSs and the operations
of 10 power systems in Japan, and to quantitatively evaluate the eco-
nomic and environmental contribution potential of the DR operations
using these WDSs. In this study, two types of DR operations were
considered: a shift in operating hours to utilize surplus daytime PV
generation, and an operation in which pump inverter control provides
short-term regulating reserved in the LFC domain. The first feature of
this research is that the effects of these two types of DR operations of
WDSs were evaluated using the Supply-Demand Analysis Model for op-
timizing generator operation across multiple power systems connected
by wide-area interconnection lines.
Japan is part of the Pacific Ring of Fire and is geographically
characterized by mountainous terrain,
so there are large differences
in elevation between regions. In addition, there is concentration and
dispersion of the population depending on the region, The geographical
characteristics of each region of Japan also affect the size of the pumps
and distribution reservoirs of water treatment plants that supply water
to that region. Therefore, it is not possible to evaluate the potential of
the DR operations in Japan as a whole unless the pump systems and
their operations in WDS in various regions of Japan are simulated. On
the other hand, the number of WDSs in Japan far exceeds the number
of the generators in all power systems. Therefore, handling all WDSs
individually increases the scale of the model, and is difficult from the
perspective of computational load.
In this study, we classified all WDSs in Japan into four clusters
according to their geographical characteristics, and the systems and the
operations of four WDSs that represent the clusters were incorporated
into the power system Supply-Demand Analysis Model. Using this
novel method, we succeeded in developing a model that can perform
optimization within an executable time range. The second feature of
this study is that a large number of WDSs throughout Japan were
clustered based on geographical characteristics, and their operations
were incorporated into the power supply–demand analysis model.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The analytical
methodology used in this study is described in
Section 2.
Section
3 describes the modeling of the WDSs used in the analysis for all of
Japan. Section 4 describes the computational model used. Section 5
describes the computational conditions and results of the case analysis
and discusses the potential impact of the Japanese WDS on the power

Section 6 presents conclusions based on the contents of this

The overall flow of analysis performed to evaluate the contribution
of water pumps to the Japanese power system is shown in Fig. 1.
First, we obtained the elevation difference and population density

geographic information system (GIS). The water supply areas of water
utilities throughout Japan are classified into four groups based on two
parameters: the difference in elevation within the water supply area
and the population density of the water supply population. We deter-
mined the water service providers that represent these four groups, and
determined the water supply unit configurations for the four groups.
We calculated the equivalent number of each of the four types of WDS

2.2. System representation
To simulate Japanese WDSs that considers the characteristics of the
topography and population distribution, we classified the water supply
areas of water suppliers nationwide into four groups from A to D using
two parameters: the difference in elevation within the water supply
area and the population density of the water supply population.
Firstly, as shown ‘‘System Representation’’ No. 1 in Fig. 1, we col-
lected the GIS data, which has the elevation difference and population
density of the water supply area of each water supplier nationwide from
the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport, and Tourism’s National
Land Information (i.e., data on waterwork-related facilities, elevation,
and slope 5th order mesh data) (Ministry of Land, Infrastructure,
Transport and Tourism, 2012, 2011).
Secondly, as shown ‘‘System Representation’’ No. 2 in Fig. 1, the
elevation difference and population density of the water supply area
for each water utility were calculated. The distribution of water service
providers based on the two parameters of elevation difference and
population density of water supply was divided into four groups using
the k-means++ method (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007), a clustering
method.
Next, as shown ‘‘System Representation’’ No. 3 in Fig. 1, the water
service providers and their water supply areas closest to the last center
of gravity of the clustering were designated as representative areas in
the groups. In this paper, we assumed a single system in which multiple
water pumps connected in parallel deliver water to a single distribution
reservoir, and water is distributed from the reservoir according to
the amount of water demand,
as shown in Fig. 2. In this system,
water from a water treatment plant is delivered to the distribution
reservoir by water delivery pumps, and water delivery pumps consume
electricity. In this paper, the water pump and distribution reservoir
are collectively referred to as the ‘‘water supply unit’’. From the wa-
ter supply facility data in the annual reports (Hitachi city enterprise
bureau, 2022; Hiroshima city waterworks bureau, 2022; Saitama city
waterworks bureau, 2022; Chiba prefecture waterworks bureau, 2022)
published by the water supply companies in the four representative
areas, the average values of the water pumping capacity, rated output,
pump head, number of pumps, and water volume in the distribution
reservoir per water treatment plant in each representative area were

2.3. Mathematical optimization models
In this study, three operational planning models that simulate the
operation of the WDSs, generators in the grid, and interconnection lines
among grids were formulated as MILPs and analyzed by optimization
calculations. All the following models perform optimization calculation
for 24 h with a one-hour resolution,
and the evaluation for one
year is performed by executing these one-day optimization 365 times.
These MILP models were optimized using Gurobi Optimizer 9.5, a
mathematical optimization solver.
2.3.1. Water pump model
Based on the WDS configuration determined in Section 2.2, pump
operation is determined by minimizing the one-day power consumption
of the pumps for each of the four types of water supply units in
each system as the objective function. Using this model that minimizes
power consumption, it is possible to calculate the power consumption
of water pumps without DR operations.
2.3.2. Supply-demand analysis model
The model, developed with reference to Negishi et al. (2022),
determines the operation of all generators in each power system and the
interconnection lines among power systems while considering power
flexibility through interconnection lines among power systems, with
the objective function of minimizing the fuel cost for one day. Since
WDS operation cannot be optimized with this model, the optimal
operation of the power system is calculated by adding the power
demand calculated by the Water Pump Model to other underlying
power demands.
2.3.3. Supply-demand analysis and water pump model
This model combines the Supply-Demand Analysis Model with the
Water Pump Model. The model determines the operation of various
generators, interconnection lines, and water pumps for each system in
Japan, using the minimization of the one-day fuel cost as the objective
function. In this model, the constraints are that DR operation can only
be performed within the controllable range of the pump, and also that
the demand for tap water can be supplied with sufficient margin. There-
fore, it will not have any negative impact on the water supply business
or the power system reliability. By incorporating WDS operation into
power system operation optimization, WDS operation hours can be
determined in a way that minimizes fuel costs for the power system as a
whole. Furthermore, when WDSs provide LFC regulating reserves, it is
possible to reduce the amount of LFC regulating reserves that existing
thermal power must provide, leading to more economically efficient
operational results.

2.4. Computational model analysis
To clarify the economic and environmental contributions of chang-
ing the operation method of water pumps, we divided the water pump
operation methods into three cases: Cases A, B, and C. The calculation
flow for each case is described as follows.
Case A In Case A, calculations were performed using the Water Pump
Model and the Supply-Demand Analysis Model. First, assuming
that the current water pumps in WDSs are being operated with
the aim of saving energy, the Water Pump Model calculates the
power consumption when the water pump operates at the lowest
power consumption. Next, the calculated power consumption of
the water pumps is added to other power demand data, and
using this as input data, calculations are performed using the
power system Supply-Demand Analysis Model. In this case, the
efficiency of the power system operations without water pump
DR operations was calculated. In this study, the DR effects were
analyzed using this as a comparison target.
Case B The calculation model used is the Supply-Demand Analysis
and Water Pump Model, which incorporates the Water Pump
Model into the Supply-Demand Analysis Model. In this model,
the operation of the water pumps is determined by minimizing
the fuel cost of the entire power system. If there is a surplus in
PV generation during daytime in sunny days, operating pumps
during the day allows for more effective use of VRE and reduces
fuel costs required for thermal power generators. Therefore, the
water pump operations can be expected to shift to reduce the
output curtailment of VRE generation.
By comparing Case B
with Case A, it is possible to clarify the DR effects of shifting
the demand time using the pump operation in WDSs.
Case C In Case C, regarding the constraints on providing LFC regu-
lating reserves in the same model as Case B, we assume that
the LFC regulating reserves can be provided by inverter control
from the water pump in WDSs without changing the number of
pumps in operation. By taking over the LFC regulating reserves
that used to be provided by thermal power generators, it is
possible to reduce the operation of thermal power generators
and contribute to reductions in fuel costs and CO2 emissions. By
comparing Case C with Case B, it is possible to clarify the DR
effects when adding a function to provide LFC regulating reserve
to the inverter of the water pumps in WDSs.

4. Mathematical models and used data
4.1. Water pump model
This model is a calculation model developed with reference to
Negishi and Ikegami (2019) and simulates the case where water pumps
are operated in such that they minimize their power consumption in the
form of an MILP. As described in Section 2.3.1, this model optimizes the
number of water pumps in operation and the amount of water delivered
at each time by considering the capacity of the distribution reservoirs,
the demand for water distribution, and the power consumption of the
water pumps when they are in operation for the operation of 10 water

4.3. Supply-demand analysis and water pump model
The Supply-Demand Analysis and Water Pump Model is a cal-
culation model that incorporates the Water Pump Model into the
Supply-Demand Analysis Model and further improves it so that the
water pumps can provide balancing reserves to the power system. In
this model, the constraints of the Water Pump Model are added to the
Supply-Demand Analysis Model, and the operation of the water pump
is optimized simultaneously with the optimization of the operation of
each generator and the electricity supply through the interconnection.
Because the objective function of the model is to minimize fuel costs, as
in the electricity supply and demand analysis model, the water pumps
are expected to be operated to minimize the fuel costs of the entire
power system, that is, to reduce the output curtailment of PV and WP
output and to provide regulating reserves to the power system.

4.4. Calculation conditions
Calculation conditions in the analyses are explained as follows. This
study analyzes 10 regions of Japan using a case study assuming the year
2040, when the introduction of VRE sources in Japan is expected to
progress and output curtailment of VRE generation will become more
pronounced than it is today. For all cases, two scenarios were prepared
with different installed VRE source capacities.
Scenario 1 This scenario of penetration of VRE generators is based on
the target values in the medium case (Ministry of the Environ-
ment, 2015). The target values of RE penetration in 2040 are set
in terms of total nationwide installed capacity, and these values
must be allocated among the regions to obtain input data for
the proposed model. Regarding the allocation method, shares
of each region in the total installed capacity were calculated
based on the data as of the end of 2016 for VRE sources other
than WP (Agency for natural Resources and Energy, 2022) and
with reference to Japan Wind Power Association data for WP
sources (Japan Wind Power Association, 2014).
Scenario 2 In this scenario, the installed capacity value is twice that in
Scenario 1 to examine the operation of the water pumps and the
resulting effects when additional renewable energy is installed.
The installed capacity of the VRE for every regional power system for
2040 is listed in Table 8. The optimization model for each case was
calculated using Gurobi Optimizer 9.5, a mathematical optimization
solver. In all cases, the parameter for the deviation of the cost function
value from the linear relaxation problem of integer programming was
set to 0.5%. As a result, in our model, there are a total of 72,048
decision variables, of which 27,744 are integer variables. Additionally,
the model includes 84,776 constraints.

6. Conclusions
In this study, to clarify the potential of water pumps in the WDS
to work with power systems in Japan, we first divided the current
Japanese WDS into four groups based on two parameters: population
density of the water supply population and elevation difference. Next,
we used optimization calculation models to simulate two cases: one

3435
Y. Akimoto et al.
case in which the water pumps are operated to minimize the power con-
sumption of the pumps as the current operation method, and another
case in which the operation timing of the pumps is changed to avoid
suppressing the output of the VRE generation and to provide balancing
reserves to the power system as necessary. In the optimization analysis,
supply–demand analysis was conducted assuming the year 2040, when
VRE generation is expected to be widely used, and the environmental
and economic effects of the optimal operation of the water pumps for
the power system were evaluated in terms of the amount of VRE output
curtailment, CO2 emissions, and annual fuel costs.
As a result, by optimizing the operation of the water pumps for
the power system, the power consumption of the water pumps was
higher than that of the current operation; however, the VRE output
curtailment, CO2 emissions, and annual fuel costs were reduced by 1%
or more. These results show that an operation method that prioritizes
the use of renewable energy, rather than an operation that minimizes
power consumption, results in more economical and environmentally
friendly operation. Furthermore, by controlling the power demand of
water pumps, which account for less than 1% of Japan’s total electricity
demand, the ability to reduce generation fuel costs and CO2 emissions
by more than 1% has a significant impact. In addition, providing the
system with balancing reserves from the water pumps resulted in an
additional effect. It is meaningful from an economic and environmental
perspective to proactively introduce DR operations that utilize water
pumps at WDSs.
However, from the operation of the pumps obtained through the
analysis, we observed that the capacity of the water reservoir con-
nected to the pumps reached its maximum capacity when the output
curtailment of VRE occurred, and the power consumption of the pumps
was reduced during that time. This suggests that further benefits can
be obtained by replacing water facilities with a larger water reservoir
capacity, if the location allows. This point requires further analysis.
