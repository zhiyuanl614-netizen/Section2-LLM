A B S T R A C T  
According to WorldVision, Eritrea has the least access to clean water in Africa. Therefore, this work presents a 
unique dual-technology power plant (DTPP) model for electricity and distilled water production as a potential 
solution to the clean water problem in Eritrea. The novel design of the DTPP consists of a solar chimney power 
plant (SCPP), operating in the daytime, and a cooling tower (CT), operating at nighttime, showing excellent 
performance improvement over the conventional SCPP. Based on the results, the DTPP produced ~509,715 kWh 
of electrical energy annually, which is almost double the amount of energy generated (238,273 kWh) by the 
traditional SCPP. Additionally, the DTPP produced 189,867 tons of distilled water annually, which is 37% more, 
when compared to the SCPP. Furthermore, the electrical power produced can also be used to produce additional 
clean water using other desalination techniques. It was concluded that the DTPP could be a viable solution for the 
clean water shortage in Eritrea. A valuable recommendation can be optimizing the DTPP design specifically for 
water production tailored to the needs of Eritrea.   
1. Introduction 
There is no doubt that energy and clean water demands have 
increased in the last decade due to continuous growth in the industry 
and world population [1]. Particularly, over the last decades between 
1995 and 2015, the global usage of energy has increased dramatically 
from 8588.9 million tonnes (Mtoe) to 13,147.3 Mtoe, respectively [2]. 
This issue has been addressed and faced by the extensive use of depleting 
fossil fuels such as natural gas, oil, and coal. However, fossil fuels are 
contributing to increasing greenhouse gasses (GHGs), causing environ­
mental problems such as global warming [3]. Yet, it is a serious chal­
lenge to manage the water and energy shortage with the current 
depletion of natural resources. Sustainable renewable sources of energy 
are suggested as favorable techniques for reducing GHGs concentration 
in the atmosphere [4]. The formulated governmental policies for regu­
lating emissions concentration into the atmosphere ensured the utili­
zation of clean energy resources [5]. Apparently, solar energy-based 
technologies are the most promising technologies for power generation 
among other renewable energy-based technologies. This because of their 
high potential and low negative impact on the environment, while 
providing clean energy [6]. One of the suggested promising technologies 
is the solar chimney power plant (SCPP). The SCPP can produce both 
electrical power and desalinated water simultaneously [7]. The first 
SCPP pilot plant was built in Spain in the 1980s, producing 100–200 MW 
of electricity [8,9]. The configuration of the SCPP consists of a chimney 
tower, turbine, and solar collector [10]. The base-collector heats the air 
between the system and the ground using solar radiation, changing the 
physical properties of the air, and increasing its enthalpy and mo­
mentum. As a result, the kinetic and thermal energy of the air increase, 
propelling it upward in the chimney and colliding with the blades of 
mechanical turbines to generate electricity [11]. Generated heat from 
the system can be harnessed for desalination and providing potable 
water giving a promising technology to address both water scarcity and 
energy demand [12]. Integrating the SCPP with seawater desalination 
was proposed for the first time by Niroomand et al. [13]. Various 
modifications and innovations to the conventional solar chimney (SC) 
were proposed over the years [13]. Maia et al. [14] studied the 
unsteady-state flow of air within the SC which was modeled and solved 
by analytical and numerical solutions. The numerical results were 
validated by comparing them with the experimental results. Further­
more, the study showed that the height and diameter of the chimney 
were the most important design parameters affecting the performance

the inlet of the chimney and making appropriate changes to the col­
lector, can improve the thermal performance of the system as proposed 
by Singh et al. [19]. The results showed an increase in the air velocity by 
2.70 folds, leading to a significant increase in power generation when 
compared to the conventional SCPP design. 
Interestingly, Haaf et al. [20] discussed the design of a solar chimney 
pilot plant in Manzanares. Using simple estimates for the basic principles 
of SCPP, they have concluded that the economic power generation of a 
scaled-up plant can be designed to produce up to 400 MW. This power 
can be used to make other processes viable such as hydrogen production 
by electrolysis as an environmentally safe source of energy [20]. This 
investigation was extended in another study conducted by Haaf [21] to 
figure out the efficiency of the collector, chimney, and turbine. The 
pressure losses in the chimney pipe and turbines were calculated and 
found that they are critical factors in the efficiency of the chimney [21]. 
A novel and sustainable Dual-Technology Power Plant (DTPP) design 
was suggested by Abdelsalam et al. [22]. The design was implemented in 
the city of Aqaba in Jordan and its performance was studied. The sug­
gested design takes advantage of the fact that the SC and the cooling 
tower (CT) have the same structure to offer a combined operation of 
both designs, ensuring that the design can run continuously, day and 
night, to generate electricity and distilled water. The results showed that 
the novel design is environmentally friendly, as it supports CO2 emission 
reduction. In addition, the design can produce electricity of approxi­
mately ~528 MWh per year and 138,300 m3 of freshwater given that the 
conventional design produces only 365 MWh of energy per year and 90, 
000 tons/year of freshwater [22]. The performance of this sustainable 
and novel design was further assessed by Abdelsalam et al. in the city of 
Doha, Qatar [23]. The results show that the design is promising, sus­
tainable, economically feasible, and offers an obvious reduction in GHGs 
emissions [23]. Despite the interest of scholars and the increasing 
number of published papers in the field of SCPP, thermal efficiency is 
still a challenge. Additionally, the large land requirement remains also 
as another challenge that needs to be addressed [24]. Therefore, vali­
dating the different suggested SCPP designs and their suitability for 
various weather conditions and geographical areas is critical. Various 
studies were conducted regarding studying the performance of the 
feasibility of the SCPP in different countries around the world analyzing 
its potentiality of reliable performance and economic feasibility. Table 1 
demonstrate location-based scientific efforts carried out from the 
experimental and numerical perspectives (see Table 2).

2. Materials and methods 
This section provides a detailed description of the proposed DTPP 
design, modes of operation as a SCPP and as a CT, followed by a sum­
mary of the mathematical model. 
2.1. DTPP description 
Fig. 1 illustrates the main design structure of the DTPP model. The 
structure is a combination of an SCPP and a cooling tower which are two 
different components that are usually constructed and operated sepa­
rately. A cross-sectional view of the DTPP with the physical dimensions 
is shown in Fig. 1. The DTPP includes three main modules: A collector, 
solar chimney, and turbine. The last two modules are common between 
the SCPP and the CT. This arrangement is critical to ensure cost reduc­
tion of the DTPP [45]. The SCPP operates in the presence of solar ra­
diation, hence it operates in the daytime only while the CT, potentially, 
can operate 24 h. Hence, the DTPP can produce electricity and distilled 
water 24/7, based on the weather conditions [38]. 
2.1.1. DTPP operating as an SCPP 
During the daytime, solar radiation heats the air between the col­
lector and the ground. Consequently, a density difference is created 
between the air in the system and the ambient, as shown in Fig. 1a. The 
accumulation of high-temperature air (low density) leads to increasing 
its pressure and creating a driving force that allows the air in the system 
to move upward the chimney. As the hot air moves upward, its velocity 
increases colliding with the blades of mechanical turbines and creating 
electrical energy. The colored arrows represent the temperature profile 
and flow of the air in the system. 
In addition, the system can be utilized further, as the collector of this 
innovative design helps in the desalination process once a seawater 
basin is added at the base of the chimney to humidify the air [36]. As the 
air moves up the chimney, it condensates at the inner walls of the 
chimney. The condensated water can then be transported outside the 
chimney through water pipes, as shown in Fig. 1a. The blue dots on the 
inner walls of the inner indicate condensed water. 
2.1.2. DTPP operating as a CT 
At nighttime, the dry air at the top of the chimney is cooled down by 
spraying a water mist that evaporates instantly, which results in 
increasing the density of the air. The operation of the CT is shown in 
Fig. 1b. This creates a density difference between the air inside the 
chimney and the ambient air causing a pressure column to drive the cold 
air downwards. As the air moves down the chimney, it exchanges energy 
with the turbine to generate electrical power. The blue arrows in Fig. 1b 
indicate cooler air than the outside air and the direction of airflow. The 
turbine is the key component in the design structure, as it must be 
bidirectional (clockwise or counterclockwise). This is important to 
support the system in operating either as an SCPP or as a CT.

The economic analysis of the DTPP was based on calculating the 
fixed capital costs (CapEx), operating costs (OPEX), and revenues. The 
CapEx calculation was based on the historical price of each component 
adjusted to the size of the design (i.e. online published information 
related to the process construction cost at similar capacities). After 
collecting the cost of the individual components (chimney column, base, 
turbine, and all other accessories), the CapEx of the proposed DTPP was 
calculated as per equation (25): 
Ci = TC + Sn
(25)  
where, Ci is the cost of the item, TC tabulated cost as reference size, S is 
the actual size and n is the exponent factor for equipment size.

3. Results and discussion 
A 24-h weather condition data on June 8th in Assab, Eritrea, is 
shown in Fig. 2. Solar radiation in this region begins at 6:00 a.m. with 
very low intensity, and increases progressively, until it reaches its peak 
at noon, and then it settles almost at zero after 6:00 p.m. The solar 
irradiation readings take the normal distribution curve during the 
period between sunrise and sunset. A value of 850 W/m2 was recorded 
as the average maximum value of solar irradiation during the period 
from 8:00 a.m. until 4:00 p.m. and continues for 8 h. This period of solar 
irradiation is the main source of operation for a conventional SCPP. 
However, the electrical power output will be zero for about 12 h at night 
for the conventional SCPP (no CT), this is due to the lack of solar 
irradiation. 
It was observed that the wind speed does not change much 
throughout the day, the values were between 0 and 6 m/s. The average 
value of the wind speed was noted around ~3 m/s. As for relative hu­
midity readings, it was observed that the difference was from 15% to 
62%. The average value of the relative humidity was about ~38.5%. It 
can be noticed that the SC is setting idle after the 12 h of solar radiation 
(i.e., it operates only in the daytime) which means 50% of the time the 
SCPP is not producing electricity. This impacts both the productivity and 
performance of SCPP as well as the economic aspects. Therefore, the CT 
was added in the DTPP design to operate in times of no solar radiation, 
hence, increasing the overall electrical power output. 
Fig. 3 describes the weather data for the entire year arranged season- 
by-season. From Fig. 3a, it can be observed that the average solar irra­
diation in the spring ranked the lowest amongst all seasons. However, 
the summer had the highest average solar irradiance, followed by the 
fall and then the winter. Similarly, the average ambient temperature 
almost followed the same trend as the average solar irradiance as shown 
in Fig. 3b. The figure shows that the summer season achieved first place 
for the highest average temperature, while the runner-up was for the 
spring season, then the fall, and the last place was for the winter season. 
However, the average values for the spring and fall are relatively very 
close. Interestingly, Fig. 3c shows that the average relative humidity 
increases in the winter reaching ∼73%, while decreases in the spring to 
67%. However, as expected the summer had the lowest average relative 
humidity with a value of approximately 57%. Furthermore, one can 
observe that the fall and spring had almost identical average relative 
humidity. Reviewing the average wind speed data, one can observe that 
the average wind speed varies quite a bit during the seasons; the highest 
value (10.40 m/s) was in the winter and the lowest (5.31 m/s) was in the 
summer as shown in Fig. 3d. Furthermore, the fall and spring average 
wind speed values fall in between. Based on the average data presented, 
one can expect that the summer will have the best performance of 
electrical power production. This is because the maximum solar irradi­
ance and temperature, and the lowest humidity occurred during the 
summer. In contrast, one will expect that the least electrical power 
production will occur in the winter because it has the lowest solar 
irradiance and temperature, as well as the highest humidity. 
For the wind speed effect on the performance, it is quite questionable 
as previous related work discussed it differently. Niroomand and 
Amidpour [47] stated that the air velocity increase might cause the air to 
spend a shorter time under the collector, in this case, it might cause the 
air to absorb less energy and consequently, the temperature difference 
between the air inside and outside would be less, resulting in less elec­
trical power generation. On the other hand, M´endez and Bicer [48] 
mentioned that high wind caused by high pressure differential will 
accordingly give high energy production and also increase the efficiency 
of the system. Regarding the production of freshwater, Zuo et al. [49] 
demonstrated that the, when the air flow is speeded up, the temperature 
difference between the glass cover (an additional glass surface inte­
grated in their system between the collector and seawater pool) and the 
seawater pool’s evaporating surface, is moderately increased. Hence, 
improving the freshwater productivity. On the contrary, P.I [50]. re­
ported that increasing the wind speed from 0 to 2.15 m/s will cause the 
productivity of freshwater to approximately increase by 11.5%, how­
ever, an increase of wind speed from 2.15 m/s to 8.81 m/s will increase 
the productivity by 1.5% only. In other words, low wind speed is ad­
vantageous for higher water production, while the enhancement in 
water production by high wind speed seems to be not very clear. As a 
result, the observed high or low wind speed belonged to a specific season 
cannot be used to expect a certain behavior or performance of the pro­
posed system, and it is better to expect the system performance based on 
the other parameters because of their stronger influences. 
The impact of the water pool radius on the temperature profile of the 
air in the system is shown in Fig. 4. The impact of collector radius on the 
temperature which is one of the crucial operational parameters of 
coming air is critical to examine. This is because the air temperature 
impacts both design objectives of DTPP- power generation and desalted 
water production. The temperature of the air increases once it enters the 
chimney as it contacts the solar glass. However, it cools down when 
approaching the water pool as it is saturated with water vapor. 
As it is shown in Fig. 4, the smaller the radius of the pool, the smaller 
the decrease in air temperature. The reason behind this is that a smaller 
pool radius leads to a lower surface area available for heat transfer be­
tween the air and the water pool. In contrast, a larger radius means a 
higher surface area available for heat transfer, hence lower developed 
ΔT, but more distilled water. This leads to a lower airflow; therefore, the 
mechanical energy to be transferred to the turbine will be lower. Hence, 
a lower electrical energy production. Therefore, distilled water pro­
duction and electricity generation are two conflicting objectives that 
need to be optimized. This agrees with the results found by Kiwan et al. 
[51], who showed that the air temperature and flow are inversely pro­
portional to the radius of the water pool. Consequently, it is also 
inversely proportional to power production. They reported that 
increasing the pool radius decreases the generated power from the SCPP. 
This is due to the increased density of the air as it contains more water 
due to seawater evaporation, hence reduction of the electrical power per 
Equation (24). Continuous increase of the radius causes excessive heat 
transfer; therefore, airflow has an upper limit on its temperature to

and CT, DTPP generates a 513.23 MWh in lumped-sum electrical power

seasonal electrical power generation from the CT is noticeable with a 
statistical range of 165 MWh. In contrast, the SCPP has a statistical range 
of only 13 MWh. The CT operation in this hybrid design depends mainly 
on the heat and mass transfer as the sprinkled water evaporates, leading 
to producing a cooling effect. From the heat transfer, the developed 
driving force of the temperature difference determines the airflow and 
the pressure difference for the operation of the turbine. Therefore, the 
winter months which are characterized by low temperatures, and high 
humidity, create a low-temperature difference (ΔT) for the air that is 
flowing, resulting in low heat transfer, hence low electrical power pro­
duction. While in the summer months, the humidity is lower, and the ΔT 
is higher, therefore, both the mass and heat transfer is enhanced. 
Consequently, enhancing the electrical power production. For the SCPP, 
the heating of air by the solar irradiation creates a pressure difference 
between the top and the bottom of the SCPP, causing an upper move­
ment of air to the top of the chimney and, consequently an interaction 
with the turbine generating electrical power. As a result, when this 
pressure difference is low, it is expected that the electrical power pro­
duction will also be low. This can be observed, when the solar 
irradiation and temperature are low, which are found to be in the winter 
months (Fig. 6).

the solar irradiation pattern found in Fig. 5. It should be mentioned that 
the reason for the low distilled water production in the winter is the low 
solar irradiation and low ambient temperature found in this season. 
However, regarding the wind speed, as mentioned earlier in the dis­

to consider it as one of the main parameters affecting the distilled water

3.2. Economic analysis and environmental impact 
The economic viability of any proposed model in the industry is as 
important as its environmental viability. Because one of the most 
important objectives of any project should be profitably for the stake­
holders [54]. The cost of the DTPP design that consists of a chimney, 
concert basin, glass base, and turbine was determined to be $3.25 
million. The economic analysis showed a payback period, DCFRR and 
ROR of 4.5 years, 25.6%, and 27.8%, respectively. The cost analysis 
suggests a very short payback period where the proposed DTPP can have 
a positive cash flow just after 4.5 years of construction and provide 
electricity and freshwater to nearby areas. Both the DCFRR and ROR also 
confirmed the profitability of the project during the service life which is 
considered profitable [53]. Based on the annual electrical energy pro­
duced by the DTPP in this study, the revenues can be calculated as 
follows:  
Revenue = US $0.367 × 513,230 = US $ 188,355                             
(29) 
The positive values of returns besides the ROR and DCFRR show and 
support the notion that the DTPP in Eritrea is economically viable. 
According to a study conducted over the countries of the Eastern 
Africa Power Pool and the potentially joining members, Eritrea seems to 
have one of the lowest capacity values for solar energy usage as a fuel. 
Additionally, the study showed that it is expected that by 2030 the en­
ergy requirements for Eritrea to reach 2.2 TWh [55]. As a result, the 
proposed design can be considered as a promising power plant to be 
implemented to increase the utilization of solar energy for electrical 
energy production. However, it should be ensured that the imple­
mentation of this power plant is environmentally friendly and supports 
the reduction of CO2 emissions. 
The annual CO2 emissions reduction for running the DTPP can be 
calculated using Equation (30). Based on the equivalent CO2 emissions 
generated using a coal-fired plant, the conversion factor is estimated to 
be 0.95 kg CO2 eq./kWh of electricity [56]. 
CO2mass (kg) = 0.95 × production of electrical energy (kWh)
(30)  
CO2mass (kg) = 0.95 × 513, 230(kWh) = 487, 568.5 
Hence, 487 tons of annual CO2 emissions can be reduced from 
running the DTPP. 
4. Conclusion 
In conclusion, the promising design of the DTPP was proposed 
aiming to overcome the critical challenge of clean water shortage in 
Eritrea. The performance of the design, depending on a specific location 
in Eritrea, was analyzed and discussed from various perspectives and the 
annual distilled water production and electrical energy production were 
determined. It was found that the DTPP design can annually produce 
about 190,863 tons of distilled water and 513,230 kWh of electrical 
energy, showing the best results in the summer months. Additionally, 
the DTPP can reduce CO2 emission by 487,568 kg and generate 
$188,355 of revenue annually. Hence, it can be said that the DTPP is a 
feasible solution for the clean water dilemma in Eritrea. However, 
further investigations should be carried out regarding improving the 
operation in the winter season as the high humidity and low solar 
irradiation of the winter months will affect the performance of the sys­
tem negatively. One recommendation can be integrating the DTPP with 
other technologies to enhance performance. For future work, the focus 
will be related to carrying out a detailed economic study of the proposed 
design customized specifically for Eritrea’s economy. Moreover, opti­
mizing the DTPP design specifically for water production, tailored to the 
needs of Eritrea. Future work may also include, assessing the perfor­
mance of the DTPP in other areas in Eritrea, perhaps several cities, to 
provide a comprehensive view of the DTPP’s performance at the county 
level.
