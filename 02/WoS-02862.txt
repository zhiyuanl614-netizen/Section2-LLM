A B S T R A C T
This research addresses the integrated management of Water Distribution Systems (WDS) and Power Distribution
Systems (PDS) to enhance operational efficiency and resilience during extreme scenarios. Traditionally, these
systems are operated independently, leading to sub-optimal performance. Alternatively, some studies have
proposed a joint operation of WDS and PDS, assuming that decisions are made simultaneously by a single
decision-maker, which is impractical. We propose a novel emergency control method that relies on sparse
communication between WDS and PDS operators, aiming to minimize load shedding (LS) in the PDS by stra-
tegically managing power demand in the interconnected WDS. The results show that the proposed coordinated
approach has similar performance to a full cooperation approach between the two systems while requiring only
minimal information exchange. Our approach demonstrates the potential of limited yet targeted information
exchange, fostering cross-sectoral collaboration to maintain service continuity under extreme conditions, and
underscores the critical role of integrated management in ensuring the resilience of interconnected infrastructure
systems.
1. Introduction
With the increasing demands on Power Distribution Systems (PDS)
and the challenges posed by integrating renewable energy sources,
ensuring system resilience and efficiency through smart grid manage-
ment has become a critical objective. The intermittent and uncertain
nature of renewable sources increases the vulnerability of PDSs to
extreme events, hazards, and instabilities, particularly due to rapid
changes in weather conditions. Therefore, developing robust strategies
to maintain PDS functionality across a broad spectrum of situations is
crucial. Nevertheless, the current trends in power supply management
pose substantial challenges in achieving this goal. Integration of
renewable energy sources and energy storage facilities impacts network
topology. Networks become more decentralized, thereby facilitating
better management of energy resources at the local level to improve
overall grid efficiency. As a result, portions of the power network
function as off-grids microgrids that can operate independently of the
main transmission grid [1]. While off-grid microgrids are robust to
failures in the transmission grid, their isolation can exacerbate the
challenges of maintaining system stability and meeting demand, espe-
cially under extreme conditions [2].
Extensive research has been conducted on enhancing PDS resilience
against such scenarios, including studies from a preventive perspective
such as system design [3–5] as well as corrective aspects focusing on
mitigating the extent and severity of extreme events [6–8]. A common
strategy for reducing the impact of these events is load shedding (LS)
which becomes necessary when the system’s capacity to meet demands
is compromised, leading to enforced power outages. LS is defined as
power demand not supplied [9]. Under such circumstances, PDS oper-
ators are tasked with making operational decisions to minimize the
impact on consumers in terms of LS [10–12]. A promising strategy for LS

to periods of lower demand to support the balance between supply and
demand. In this context, infrastructure systems that are interconnected
to PDS have the potential to serve DR, such as heating systems [13,14],
gas distribution systems [15], and Water Distribution Systems (WDS)
[16]. Recently there has been a growing interest in decision-making
processes that integrate PDS and WDS. WDS are infrastructure in
charge of the water supply for domestic, agricultural, and industrial uses
and typically include power intensive facilities (pumping stations,
desalination plants, etc.). As a result, WDS are among the largest power
consumers accounting for an estimated 7–8 % of the total globally
generated energy [17,18]. Moreover, WDS consists of inherent opera-
tional flexibility due to storage facilities that allow for operational ad-
justments and are typically used for cost minimization [19,20]. A set of
power-consuming water facilities controlled by the same operator can
offer significant potential to provide the required flexibility to both LS
reduction and support many other PDS applications [21]. At the same
time, several studies suggested joint optimization for WDS and PDS to
minimize operational costs [22–25] and investment costs [26,27].
Furthermore, the same WDS flexibility can serve other DR objectives.
For instance, [16] demonstrated how DR involving WDS can reduce
GHG emissions while not compromising on operational costs. Additional
applications of integrating WDS with PDS are frequency regulation [28]
and voltage regulation [29]. The flexibility inherent in WDS can also be
utilized to increase renewable energy source integration [30], acting as
flexible loads for PDS that can absorb excess power or adjust to inter-
mittent supply. The renewable surplus can also be effectively used by
storing energy indirectly through pumping to fill water tanks during
off-peak periods [31].
Due to the growing stress on both systems, interdependent resilience
is identified as a crucial aspect of PDS and WDS management where the
potential benefit is yet to be fully realized [32,33]. According to [34],
the resilience dependency between the systems is bi-directional where
each system can contribute to the other system’s resilience. [34] also
pointed out the challenges associated with integrated systems resilience
enhancement and suggested a method to quantify the combined resil-
ience. The quantification of multi-system resilience was the focus of
several studies that suggested different metrics for this purpose. A
common approach for quantifying resilience is according to the amount
of lost power and water [35]. A different approach, suggested by [36], is
to include metrics based on the quality of the power flow on top of the
loss of service. Power quality is estimated as voltage magnitude metric,
duration of power lines at their upper bounds, and thermoelectric metric
specific for the case where the WDS is used as a cooling cycle system. A
few studies suggested methods for resilience enhancement through
design and operational interventions. [37] presented a multi-objective
optimization model to minimize investment costs and expected loss of
power and water. A similar approach from an operational perspective
was presented in [38]. An emergency response is suggested to a PDS that
connects several microgrids. Once an emergency occurs, each microgrid
adjusts its operation to support the entire distribution system by mini-
mizing the total loss of power and water. Yang & Lo [39] introduced a
multi-objective approach aimed at simultaneously minimizing unsup-
plied loads and water, the tradeoff between the two objectives is dis-
cussed presenting that loss of power can be prioritized over loss of water
and vice versa.
The above studies presented fully collaborative decision-making
frameworks, meaning that decisions for both systems are made simul-
taneously by a single decision maker. A contrasting perspective involves
each utility making its own decision in response to the other utility’s
decision, without any cooperation. For instance, [35] presented an in-
dependent decision-making approach where the PDS operator de-
termines an emergency response policy to minimize LS, and then the
WDS operator adjusts pump scheduling according to the available power
to minimize water supply shortage. [40] presented a non-cooperative
game theory approach in which the two utilities act as independent
players in a Cournot competition to determine the optimal operation
dispatch. While the above studies present remarkable advancements in
utilizing the links between the two systems, there still exists a significant
knowledge gap: Cooperation-based approaches embody an ideal situa-
tion of complete knowledge sharing and cooperation, yet this is rarely
the case in real life and is thus impractical. Conversely, independent
approaches lack the benefits of collaboration and are therefore likely to
be sub-optimal. Consequently, a strategy that enables collaboration
through reasonable communication between PDS and WDS is essential
for achieving more resilient and efficient infrastructure systems.

approach to minimize LS under emergency scenarios with minimal in-

2. Methodology
2.1. Frameworks for integrated systems decision-making
To provide clarity and consistency throughout this paper, let us
introduce several terms that will be used throughout this paper to
distinguish
between
the
different
optimization
decision-making
strategies:
• Decoupled Optimization: In this approach, there is no communica-
tion between the two systems’ operators. One operator takes action
independently, and the second operator responds to these actions
subsequently, without prior coordination.
• Centralized Coupled Optimization: This approach involves a fully
cooperative strategy where decisions for both PDS and WDS are
made simultaneously by a single stakeholder, ensuring that all ob-
jectives and constraints regarding both systems are known and
considered in tandem.
• Coordinated Optimization: This new approach, introduced in the
current study, is based on sparse communication and information
exchange between the operators of PDS and WDS. It seeks to balance
the benefits of Centralized Coupled optimization, which represents
the optimal solution with the practicality of Decoupled optimization,
which is simple and does not depend on any collaboration, aiming to
optimize the resilience of both systems through selective information
sharing.
Fig. 1 depicts the overview of the proposed methodology. Emergency
events are generated randomly based on several properties that describe
the two systems’ states (see a detailed description of the random sce-
narios generation in the last part of the methodology section). Each
scenario is optimized with all the above decision-making strategies to
find optimal operation policies, minimizing the loads not served.
The method is designed to minimize LS in PDS under extreme sce-
narios with limited power resources. The method is designed for a local
microgrid is a medium voltage of 13.3 kV, but similar principles can be
applied to other types of networks. The first step is to develop an opti-
mization model with an objective function subject to physical and
operational constraints. The optimization modeling allows for either a
combined optimization of the WDS and PDS or separate optimization
problems for each system. While the PDS is optimized to reduce LS, the
WDS has two distinct objective functions. The first is for routine periods
where the objective is to minimize operational costs. The second
objective is for emergency scenarios where the WDS prioritizes sup-
porting the PDS. In the second case, the WDS minimizes weighted
pumping power consumption, where the weights are received from the
PDS operators to represent the time steps and locations (buses) where LS
reduction is most essential. The LS is calculated using a soft constraint
approach involving adding slack terms to the bus power balance con-
straints which permits some degree of constraints violation. The sum of
all violations is the PDS objective function. In the WDS problem, all
constraints are hard to ensure that water supply is not compromised to
mitigate power LS. Following are the mathematical formulations for the
three frameworks of integrated decision-making. The notation used in
the equations below is summarized in the Nomenclature table above.

2.3. WDS model
The WDS optimization is framed as a pump scheduling problem
subject to satisfying consumer demands. The hydraulic behavior of the
WDS, including flow-head relationships, is bypassed using an implicit
hydraulic formulation. This approach avoids the need to solve nonlinear
hydraulic equations during optimization by relying on pre-computed
system parameters and operational constraints that indirectly capture
the connectivity and performance of the network. Such simplification is
commonly employed in WDS optimization studies [42,43] to enhance
computational efficiency and scalability. The system operation is dis-
cretized to a finite number of hydraulic states, each representing a
different set of active pumps. Table 1 presents this setup, where every
row represents a set of active pumps such that a value of 1 indicates the
pump is active (and 0 otherwise). The decision variables in this formu-
lation are binary variables that determine whether a pump combination
is used or not. Each active pump is associated with nominal flow and
power values which are the results of the hydraulic conditions generated
by activating the corresponding set of pumps.

j are set to 1 if pump j is connected to bus i and 0 otherwise. As
mentioned above, two objective functions can be considered. The first is

2.6. Coordinated optimization
The essence of the proposed method lies in the design of a commu-
nication protocol between the water and power utilities, aimed at
optimizing a shared objective. The common objective is minimizing LS
under emergency scenarios while the operational costs of the WDS are in
lower priority and the power utility will be willing to pay the extra costs
of the WDS. Ideally, a perfect knowledge program would be imple-
mented, where all data and constraints from both systems are integrated
into a single optimization problem. However, due to the independent
management of water and power utilities in real-life systems, such
comprehensive integration is impractical. Instead, we propose a meth-
odology that accommodates limited information sharing to effectively
enhance the common objective. Based on the bilevel optimization (BO)
paradigm [44], the formulation includes a leader and a follower. Unlike
the classic BO setup, the follower gets the opportunity to respond to the
leader’s policy and ask for adoptions that serve the common objective.
The leader is the WDS which, unless received a notification from the
PDS, optimizes pump scheduling to minimize costs. The follower is the
PDS which adjusts the generation schedule to the WDS operation. Dur-
ing extreme scenarios such as when the PDS is disconnected from the
main grid and LS becomes inevitable, the following steps are taken: First,
the power operator solves an LS minimization problem while consid-
ering pumping loads as flexible loads. This step obtains an auxiliary
solution that identifies the optimal pumping schedule from the PDS
perspective. It is used solely as an auxiliary to support the next stages
and will not be implemented. This is because it is assumed that in
emergency scenarios water demand is a critical load, so the WDS can
adjust its operation to support the PDS objective of reducing LS, but
without compromising the satisfaction of water demand. Therefore,
when the PDS solves the first stage inner problem it ensures to allocate at
least the nominal power that the WDS typically uses. This prevents the
WDS from being limited in subsequent stages of the method due to the
auxiliary solution. In other words, pumping loads are effectively
assigned an infinite criticality index, which imposes a very high cost for
LS affecting these loads. This prioritization ensures that pumping loads
are treated as essential in the LS minimization process. It is noted that

2.7. Costs analysis
The response to PDS line outages, supported by operational adjust-
ments within the WDS, entails certain costs for the water utility. While
LS is minimized, the water utility incurs additional expenses due to al-
terations in the pumping schedule. To address this issue, it would be
beneficial to monetarily quantify the additional costs carried out by the
WDS due to the interventions for LS reductions. Furthermore, these costs
are compared with the Cost of Energy Not Served (CENS). This analysis
of the surplus cost of the water utility as a result of emergencies could
also facilitate the calculation of potential subsidies between the two
utilities, promoting a more collaborative financial relationship. The
additional costs for the WDS are calculated as a function of the differ-
ence in pumping costs by the decoupled and coordinated approaches
during the emergency and the costs associated with the missing volume
of tanks at the end of the emergency, as outlined in Eq. (21). This
equation consists of two components: the first calculates the cost dif-
ference during the emergency hours, reflecting the increased opera-
tional costs of the WDS; the second estimates the cost to replenish the
missing water volume at the end of the emergency, factoring in the
average specific power of the WDS pumps and applying the on-peak
tariff, under the assumption that the replenishment occurs during on-
peak hours.

2.8. Random emergency scenarios
A random scenarios generator was developed to support the evalu-
ation of the proposed methodology. The scenarios were generated by
drawing sets of random parameters that characterize each scenario.
These parameters include the start time of the scenario, its duration,
scaling factors (multipliers) of the water demand, power loads, and PV
availability profile. Additionally, the number of damaged power lines
and the index of damaged (failed) lines. Lastly, the state of tanks and
batteries at the start of the emergency, meaning the water volume and
energy stored in tanks and batteries respectively. These parameters
generate a range of scenarios different from each other in the level of
severity. For example, in a scenario that occurs during a peak con-
sumption day where water and power consumption are at their high
range, it will be more difficult to reduce LS since the systems have fewer
reserves. Similarly, some power lines are more critical than others,
charged batteries are desired at the beginning of an emergency, etc. In
this context, the amount of LS results from a scenario indicates its
severity. All the parameters were drawn from a uniform distribution
according to the configuration presented in Table 2.
3. Results
The proposed method was evaluated through a case study inte-
grating a small WDS pumping water from three wells with a modified
version of the 33-bus system benchmark. The case study is designed to
simulate a local energy microgrid capable of off-grid operation, the
microgrid voltage is 13.3 kV. By combining local renewable generators
with storage devices and emergency diesel generators, microgrids can
sustain off-grid operation for extended periods. This shift to microgrids
helps mitigate the instabilities in the main grid caused by the intermit-
tent nature of renewable energy. Additionally, it ensures a reliable and
high-quality power supply for consumers within these microgrids, who
are shielded from the fluctuations and disruptions affecting the main
grid [47].
Accordingly, the discussed microgrid is equipped with distributed
energy resources, including PV and energy storage systems as suggested
by [48]. To the modified 33-bus PDS we added a WDS that draws water
from three local wells. The WDS contains two pressure zones such that
the wells pump water into the lower pressure zone governed by tank t1
(3435 m3) and a booster pump lifts a portion of the water to the higher
elevation consumers governed by tank t2 (1235 m3). The well pumps
and the booster pumps both represent centrifugal pumps and are
modeled the same in the optimization models. The wells are equipped
with emergency diesel generators ensuring their functionality during
emergencies. These generators can also provide power to the PDS.
(Fig. 3)
To rigorously evaluate the ability of the proposed coordinated opti-
mization ability to reduce LS it was tested against a range of 1000
emergency scenarios based on the distributions presented in Table 2.
For each random scenario, LS was minimized by all three operational
strategies: decoupled, centralized coupled, and coordinated optimiza-
tion (the reader is referred to the definitions in the methodology sec-
tion). The effectiveness of the three strategies was statistically compared
as presented in Table 3. The values in the table represent the amount of
LS in kWh and the values within the parentheses show the deviation
from the centralized coupled approach which constitutes a benchmark.
The centralized coupled approach outperforms the other strategies
epitomizing the ideal of perfect knowledge and coordination between
WDS and PDS. The decoupled approach resulted in a 7.86 % increase in
LS compared to the centralized coupled approach. Remarkably, the

3.1. Edge scenarios analysis
Further analysis was conducted on specific scenarios to investigate
the factors influencing the LS reduction capacity. Two extreme scenarios
were chosen, the first one demonstrates a significant LS reduction
whereas in the second one, only a minor reduction is achieved.
Scenario 1 – significant LS reduction
The scenario starts at 19:00 and lasts 12 hours. Lines 6 and 26 of the
PDS are under outage. The tank volumes at the emergency start are 27 %
and 46 % of their total capacity, and the batteries charging states are
55 %, 42 %, 66 %, and 24 %. For these conditions, LS will be 4199 kWh,
2944 kWh, and 2942 kWh for the decoupled, coordinated, and
centralized coupled strategies respectively, exhibiting an almost 30 %
reduction in LS. The WDS response to a scenario can be reviewed
through the tank volume during the emergency as shown in Fig. 7, the
pump schedule and power depicted in Figs. S.1 and S.2 in Appendix A.
The emergency starts in the evening, right before the off-peak tariff
period. Uninformed of the emergency event, the decoupled solution is
such that the WDS pumps as much as possible during this time which can
be seen in Fig. 7 where the tanks are constantly filled during the
nighttime. With the PDS delivering the emergency signal, the WDS re-
duces pumping to the minimum possible without violating tank mini-
mum volumes. This result indicates that emergencies during off-peak
periods are more convenient. While from a pure PDS perspective, this
might seem intuitive, the proposed methodology sheds light on how
WDS has greater potential to support emergencies during off-peak
periods.
Another factor impacting the results is the water demand and power
load factors of 0.93 and 0.8, respectively. The lower consumption of
water and load reduces the systems’ strain allowing for a better supply-
demand balance. The tank volumes are quite empty with 27 % in the
main (lower) tank and 46 % in the upper (smaller) tank, and the battery
states are around 50 % charged. These initial conditions are not favor-
able for the objective of LS reduction, yet this scenario is one where WDS
reduces LS the most.
Scenario 2 – minor LS reduction
A second emergency scenario illustrates conditions where the WDS
contribution to the LS reduction is limited. This emergency starts at
06:00 and lasts 19 hours. Lines 14 and 25 of the PDS are under outage.
The tank volumes at the emergency start are 31 % and 53 % of their total
capacity, and the batteries charging states are 83 %, 66 %, 74 %, and
35 %. For these conditions, LS will be 11474 kWh, 14414 kWh, and
14406 kWh for the decoupled, coordinated, and centralized coupled
strategies respectively, indicating an LS reduction of 0.5 %. It is noted
that both centralized coupled and coordinated approaches only minorly
reduce LS, pointing out that the cause for the minor improvement is not
the method disadvantages but stems from the scenario itself which
contains critical conditions that prevent LS reduction. Fig. 8 presents the
tank volumes during the emergency, the pump schedules and the power
depicted in Figs. S.3 and S.4 in Appendix A.
This emergency scenario falls in the daytime when power and water
consumption are at their upper edge, so the systems have fewer re-
dundancies to decrease LS. The water demand factor is 1.09 and the PDS
loads factor is 1.11. These values indicate a peak consumption day,
adding another challenge on top of the emergency scenario. The two
water tanks start the emergency with 31 % and 53 % of their capacity.
This exceeds WDS operation standards where pumps are expected to
operate during the night and start the morning demand cycle when full.
This exception from common practical standards is one of the causes
that prevent the WDS from shifting pumping and enable further LS
reduction. Lastly, the PV availability is at its low edge, with a PV factor
of 0.8 which further limits the power generation capacity.
4. Conclusions
The integrated management of WDS and PDS was investigated
through a coordinated optimization framework, compared with decou-
pled and centralized coupled strategies aimed at minimizing LS during
emergencies. Previous studies on joint multi-system optimization often
assumed full transparency and cooperation between operators, which is
unrealistic. Alternatively, some studies suggested independent frame-
works where each utility optimizes its objectives independently, but this
is typically sub-optimal. The current study proposes a middle path of a
coordinated optimization approach. This approach is based on practical
communication between utilities, leading to better overall performance.
The coordinated strategy proved highly effective in managing emer-
gency conditions for the examined case study of a local microgrid con-
nected to a small WDS, consistently reducing LS compared to decoupled
decision-making across a range of simulated emergency scenarios. For
this
case
study,
the
coordinated
strategy’s
performance
closely
approached the optimal benchmark results of the centralized coupled
strategy. While the proposed method does not rely on assumptions that
limit its applicability to the specific case study, its effectiveness was
demonstrated only on a single configuration, with variations primarily
in the simulated scenarios. Therefore, future research should focus on
implementing and validating the method across different categories of
networks to further confirm its generalizability and robustness. Addi-
tionally, the study analyzes the factors that impact the severity of
emergency scenarios and the effectiveness of the proposed method in
reducing LS. It was found that WDS operational adjustments are more
effective in scenarios where some redundancy is available, such as
during nighttime scenarios. Future research should analyze different
