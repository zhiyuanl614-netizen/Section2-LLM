A B S T R A C T
Water distribution systems (WDSs) are designed to convey water from sources to consumers. 
Their operation is a main concern for engineers, researchers, and practitioners and is subject to 
demand, pressure, and quality constraints. Pumping stations require power to pump water and 
keep system pressure at a desired level. On the other hand, power is generally supplied through 
power grids (PGs), which require optimal operation while satisfying operational constraints, such 
as generation limits, power consumption, and voltage constraints. Since the two infrastructure 
systems are interconnected, decision-makers could benefit from a holistic approach that would 
allow solving the two operational optimization problems together as one conjunctive problem. 
This paper presents the full mathematical formulation of the conjunctive optimization problem, 
including a novel modelling approach for the operation of a variable speed pump, which does not 
include integer variables for pump status, thus allowing to solve the model as a non-linear pro­
gramming (NLP) problem. The formulation is applied to two illustrative case studies, and the 
results are compared to the optimal operation of the independent WDS. The inclusion of the PG in 
the optimization problem is observed clearly in the results and influences them quite signifi­
cantly. WDS operation is shown adjust to the PG constraints, and the application of the 
conjunctive model results in a cost reduction rate of more than 10 % for both case studies.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background
The challenges posed by significant population and urban growth, climate change and depletion of energy sources have placed a 
heavy burden on the design and management of infrastructure systems. As natural resources dwindle at an alarming rate, the need to 
address these challenges becomes even more urgent. Besides developing new and more efficient technologies and finding alternative 
resources to rely on, smart operation and management of existing resources and infrastructure systems are crucial for battling this 
global crisis. Water Distribution Systems (WDS) and power grids are among the most critical systems to serve the modern human 
population. Better management of those systems could be key to a better future on the planet [1].

WDSs form the bedrock of essential infrastructure, designed to ensure the secured conveyance of water from sources to consumers. 
These systems distribute water in varying quantities to different consumers, which requires an operational policy – a decision 
regarding the quantity of water that should be pumped or stored, and the amount of energy that should be injected into the system at 
every operational step. An optimal operation policy is one that leads to a desired outcome or objective value. Among various possible 
operational objectives, water utilities aim to minimize water loss [2], energy cost [3,4], and/or environmental impact [5]. WDS can be 
used to supply water continuously, or intermittently, depending on consumer demands [6]. This study, however, will consider only 
WDS that supply water in a continuous and uninterrupted manner.
In a similar manner, power grids are large-scale networks that connect power plants to power consumption centers. They are 
designed to satisfy varying power demands under different operational and technological constraints (e.g., maintaining a specific 
voltage). Often, the power grid is fed by several power sources. These sources are located at different ends of the grid and have different 
limitations on the amount of power and availability time periods. Power injected into the system can originate in different sources, 
such as fossil fuels, coal, wind, pumped hydro-storage, thermal or solar [7]. Due to all the reasons mentioned above, power grids 
require continuous operation, monitoring and balancing of supply and demand.
Owing to the interdependency between the water and the power systems, our research presents a novel approach to the conjunctive 
optimization of WDS and power grids, a topic that has begun to garner attention in the literature [8,9]. These studies have made 
significant contributions to the field; however, they do not account for the detailed modeling of energy flow within the power grid, 
particularly the physical relationships between voltages and power flow, which are crucial for ensuring the stability and efficiency of 
the integrated system. Our paper addresses this critical gap by incorporating detailed modelling of the AC power grid, including both 
active and reactive power as functions of complex voltages and the admittance matrix of the power system. This approach provides a 
deeper understanding of the joint operational challenges and regulatory mechanisms essential for the holistic optimization of inter­
connected water and power networks.
The model formulated in this paper is applied to two illustrative case studies that help demonstrate the developed methodology. 
Furthermore, a comparison between the optimal operations of the conjunctive problem and the decoupled water network is presented 
to highlight the benefits of the conjunctive operation.
1.2. Literature review
The research and study of the optimal operation of WDS date back to the 1970’s. Shamir [10] has addressed the issue of a changing 
load on the distribution system and proposed a methodology for optimal operation under those conditions. Following Shamir [10], a 
dynamic programming solution was developed by Sterling and Coulbeck [11]. Many different approaches were since then suggested, 
such as linear programming [12], non-linear heuristic methods [13], genetic algorithms [14] and analytical methods [15,16]. More 
recent efforts concentrate on developing methods to deal with the increased complexity of the optimization problem, partly stemming 
from the inclusion of water quality considerations. Those methods are highly innovative and include the use of metamodeling [17], 
machine learning and surrogate modelling [18] and automatic clustering [19]. Similarly, optimal operation of power grids is an active 
research topic. In this research field, the problem is known as the optimal power flow (OPF) problem [20]. In the OPF problem, the task 
is to seek the optimal amount of power to be generated subject to demand constraints and physical constraints of the power grid. An 
objective function, such as minimizing cost or losses throughout the grid, is used to derive the optimal solution [21]. Several methods 
have been used to solve the OPF problem, including linearization [22] and different relaxation methods [23], [24], among many others 
[25], [26], [27]. A major challenge that researchers and practitioners are considering is the integration of renewable energy sources in 
the power grid, such as solar and wind. By the end of 2019, installed capacity of wind power reached 651 GW worldwide [28]. The 
growing dominance of these renewable power sources poses challenges for the operation of power grids and is a research topic of high 
relevance.
In light of the above, a rich body of literature exists on the operation of WDSs and power grids. However, despite the two systems 
being strongly coupled, they are handled separately in most studies, and their relationship and impact on each other still constitute a 
research field that is in its infancy.
Drinking water and wastewater pumping account for 2 % of the power consumption in the United States, and power costs typically 
constitute 40 % of the total operational costs of WDS [29]. The potential benefits of exploring the relationship between those two 
systems are widely recognized, specifically in operation and management [30]. They include, among other benefits, gaining flexibility, 
sustainability, and reliability, while reducing operation costs. This advocates the need for analysis and examination of the relationship 
between those two critical infrastructure systems.
In recent years, several studies have been conducted to investigate the coupled optimization of power and water systems. Koh et al. 
[31] examined the relationship of a reservoir system with the power grid. By creating a hard-coupling modelling framework, they 
showed that the water system improves the integration of renewable energy sources in the power grid. Pereira-Cardenal et al. [32] 
optimized a joint regional water-power system, focusing on a regional irrigation system. These two studies considered water systems 
used for power generation and agricultural purposes, rather than drinking water distribution infrastructure. Zamzam et al. [33] have 
suggested an optimal operation problem with the objective of minimizing operation costs, which include the operation cost of the 
power system, the cost of using distributed energy sources, and the cost of operating the water network. The authors assumed given 
energy prices for the water operator and did not acknowledge that those prices are subject to the solution of the OPF problem. 
Moreover, the constraints regarding the WDS consider fixed flow directions, which is usually not the case in real systems. The con­
straints are then relaxed using successive convex approximations. The results presented in that paper do not include the operation of 
the considered storage inside the WDS, which underlines the focus on power systems and power grid perspective rather than on WDS

Stuhlmacher and Mathieu [34] formulated a problem with the objective of minimizing operation costs and maximizing revenue 
due to pump flexibility. In this discussed study, the number of timesteps in the simulation is 3, which is relatively low considering 
operational conditions tend to change more frequently. The authors also assumed that the pump flow is regulated by opening and 
closing valves connected to the pumps; this regulation is rarely employed in real-life systems, since it wastes valuable hydraulic head 
produced by the pumps.
Ayyagari et al. [35] have considered a similar co-optimization problem of water and power networks. Several aspects can 
distinguish our work from theirs. The objective considered was a minimization of active power losses along transmission lines in the PG 
and pump power in the WDS, as opposed to cost. Although those objectives are often used, operation cost is a far more common 
objective for operators. Additionally, this paper offers a wider analysis, presenting two case studies of different scales with sensitivity 
analysis scenarios, and a solution for the original non-linear problem formulation, as opposed to the employment of approximation 
methods. DallAnese et al. [36] have looked at an optimal power flow problem in a power distribution network containing renewable 
energy sources, but have not considered combining the connection to a water distribution system to their power network.
Oikonomou et al. [37] suggested revealing energy prices to the WDS operators one day in advance, to allow them the optimization 
of their operation, the solution of which will then be sent back to the power system operators. This approach is the common one used in 
practice for WDS operation and will be presented in this paper as a means for comparison to the solution of the conjunctive operation. 
Oikonomou and Parvania [38] added to the same WDS treatment and desalination plants and included the energy consumption of 
those plants as part of the cost calculation in their objective. In both studies, the only available power source is conventional gen­
erators. Majidi et al. [39] added a pumped-storage hydropower unit to the WDS as a sustainable energy source that can help reduce the 
power generated by conventional generators. The problem was formulated as a mixed-integer non-linear programming (MINLP) 
problem, and the solution method included the formulation of piecewise linear approximations of the non-linear constraints. The three 
mentioned studies presented three similar WDS layouts that differ by minor modifications. Several issues regarding this layout and 
design come to mind when examining them from a WDS analysis perspective. The system is branched, which does not represent well 
the complexity of a WDS. Furthermore, links connected in series meant to convey the same flow do not have identical diameters, which 
is not realistic and causes unnecessary increased head loss. In addition, pumps are connected in series, which will often require co­
ordination of their operation and is not preferred.
Putri et al. [8] proposed a predictive control algorithm for the interconnected water and power system, applying it to a real-world 
system. Their results pointed to the significant benefits of the approach - saving capital, reducing carbon footprint and allowing 
smoother operation of the system. Bhatraj et al. [9] presented an optimization model for both the design and operation of a renewable 
energy based microgrid coupled with water supply systems, demonstrating how clean energy could be utilized to increase the effi­
ciency of the water system, in terms of energy use. Although constituting as significant contributions to the field, those studies do not 
consider the detailed modelling of the power grid [8,9], specifically the inclusion of power flow equations. Nonetheless, the detailed 
modelling of those two networks is crucial for revealing their optimal operation.
This paper offers several contributions to the topic of conjunctive optimal operation of WDS and power grids. Firstly, all mentioned 
studies were conducted by power system experts, which resulted in inaccurate hydraulic modelling of the WDS and lacked the input of 
water distribution experts. This study is aimed at highlighting the WDS operation and the way in which it is affected by the coupling 
with the power grid, and therefore presents a more accurate modelling of the WDS, which allows flow directions to change throughout 
the operation, rather than using fixed flow directions, which is unrealistic. This addition is significant, as it dramatically enlarges the 
solution space. Secondly, this study offers a broad sensitivity analysis, which allows for a more thorough analysis of the trade-offs 
taking place inside the system. Lastly, the analysis dedicated to the results in this paper focuses not only on the way that the two 
systems improve the operation of one another, but also on the way they constrain one another - a perspective that was not applied in 
previous studies, to the best of our knowledge.
2. Methodology
This section is dedicated to mathematically formulating the full conjunctive optimal operation problem of a WDS coupled with a 
power grid. The full formulation is detailed below, and includes the decision variables, the objective function and constraints. The 
formulation is followed by a sub-section suggesting a novel modelling approach to overcome the need of binary variables to model the 
operation of variable speed pumps. Thus, the obtained problem is a non-linear programming (NLP) problem, containing only 
continuous variables, which allows the employment of a non-linear solver. The formulation was coded using YALMIP optimization 
modelling language [40], a MATLAB© toolbox that allows the formulation and solution of optimization problems. The solutions were 
obtained using IPOPT and FMINCON solvers.
2.1. Decision variables
Generally, an optimal operation problem requires the determination of several quantities, which then dictate the physical state of 
the entire system. For example, the determination of pump rotation speed and flow will dictate the flow distribution and nodal heads 
inside the WDS. However, the solution method chosen for dealing with the problem (a numerical non-linear solver) requires the 
calculation of derivatives using numerical approximations, which requires the inclusion of all calculated quantities as dependent 
decision variables for the determination of a progression direction in the search space.
Therefore, the decision variables for the conjunctive optimal operation problem include all decision variables from the two separate

2.3. Constraints
The nonlinear constraints of the power grid are presented below, which include the physical constraints and the mathematical 
relationships between the different variables and parameters in the system. The formulation is presented according to the bus injection 
model, which allows representing alternating current (AC) power flow equations in a quadratic and convenient form.
Power grids convey power using alternating current (AC). Alternating current is an electrical current that changes its magnitude 
and direction in a periodic manner, as opposed to direct current (DC), which flows in constant magnitude and direction. In the AC 
system, power, current, voltage and impedance are all mathematically represented by complex numbers. The real part of the power is 
referred to as active power, and the imaginary part is referred to as reactive power. Those two power components affect each other and 
must satisfy power flow equations, as will be described in this formulation. All buses in the power grid are connected through 
transmission lines, each having an impedance value. The admittance matrix defines the connectivity of the power grid and gives 
information about the total impedance connected between every two buses.

2.4. Overcoming the need for binary variables
Fig. 1 demonstrates different possible states the system could attain, according to the classic modelling of variable speed pumps, as 
described in Eq. (19).
The valve located between the pump and the network is aimed at correcting the model according to the desired hydraulic behavior 
of the system. At the event in which the pump is on, water will flow from the source in the direction of the network; the binary variable 
Zp,l will equal to 1; pump head gain will attain a positive value; and the valve will be open, so no significant change in total head will be 
observed along it, as shown in Fig. 1a.
At the event in which the pump is off, the source would be disconnected from the rest of the network, and the head gain along the 
pump will equal to zero. The binary variable Zp,l will equal to 0, and the free variable ΔHv, representing the valve head loss, will come 
into play. The valve will be closed, to represent a disconnection between the pump and the network. In such a case, the hydraulic head 
inside the network will be determined by the inner hydraulics of the water network, forcing ΔHv to acquire the difference in head (i.e., 
head loss) between the network and the source. If the hydraulic head in the network is greater than that at the source node, water will 
flow from the network in the direction of the source and will then be blocked by the valve (Fig. 1b). If the hydraulic head in the network 
is lesser than that at the source, water will flow from the source in the direction of the network and will then be blocked by the valve as 
well (Fig. 1c).
The claim presented here is that under several practical engineering assumptions, the same effect can be achieved without the use 
of the variables ΔHv and Zp,l, which constitute the classic hydraulic modelling of variable speed pumps operation. By using the 
expression of Eq. (13), Eq. (19) could be reformulated as:

In the scenario shown in Fig. 1b, the pump is off, and the hydraulic head inside the network is greater than that at the source. The

preferable to locate a pump at a point where the head at the discharge node could be lower than that at the source. The need to add 
pumps to a system stems from the requirement to increase the hydraulic head inside the network, meaning the discussed scenario is

3. Illustrative case studies
The conjunctive optimal operation problem was considered for two illustrative case studies. In both cases, the pumps in the WDS 
are supplied with power through a power grid containing two generators: a conventional one, and a sustainable (solar) one, which is 
available during a limited period of the day and bounded in the amount of power it is able to generate. Both case studies are relatively 
small scaled, due to the nature of the solution method and the limited capability of the non-linear solver used. The goal of these case 
studies is to illustrate how the inclusion of the power grid in the model affects the optimal operation of the WDS. For each of the case 
studies, the simulation period was set to 24 h, with a timestep length of 1 h.
The effort to obtain a solution included the use of two non-linear solvers – IPOPT and FMINCON, which use numerical approxi­
mations for evaluating derivatives. When optimizing the operation of the independent WDSs, both solvers were able to converge to the 
same solution, whereas when solving the conjunctive problems, only FMINCON was capable of achieving an optimal solution. This is

3.1. Illustrative case study I
Fig. 2a presents the layout of illustrative case study I. The system comprises of a water distribution system with one loop connecting 
three consumers, one pump and a storage tank. The demand multipliers for the consumers throughout the simulation period and the 
water distribution system attributes are presented in Fig. 3 and Table 1, respectively. The pump is supplied with power from bus 1 of 
the power grid, which includes 3 buses, 3 transmission lines, 3 capacitors, 2 generators at buses 1 and 2 and an external load at bus 3. 
All properties for the power grid are presented in Table 2. The generator at bus 1 is a conventional one, meaning it is available all day 
long and is not constrained in the amount of power which is able to produce. The generator at bus 2 is assumed to be a solar energy- 
based power generator. The power produced at this generator is only available during a limited period of time during the day (when 
solar energy is available), and the amount of power it is able to produce is limited. The daily solar energy availability profile is shown in 
Fig. 1b. The layout presented in Fig. 2, water demand profile presented in Fig. 3 and all properties in Tables 1 and 2 constitute the data 
used for the base run of this illustrative case study.
The goal of this relatively simple illustrative case study is to gain a general understanding about the mechanisms that are at play 
between the different components in the system. The simple structure of the system allows to analyse the results more intuitively and 
introduce different load scenarios and parameter modifications that can later be projected or tested on larger models.
After the formulation, an optimal solution was obtained using FMINCON solver. The solver was used several times using different 
initial points, that led to the same optimal solution. The solver generated an optimal solution after 443 iterations with a running time of 
157.8 s. The objective function value for the obtained optimal solution is $47,488.
Fig. 4 presents the results of the base run – the pump power demand (4a) and the tank elevation (4b) throughout the day. The results 
are compared with a solution for the independent operation of the water network, when it is not coupled with the power grid.
It is evident from Fig. 4 that the combination of the two optimization problems yields a significantly different solution for the 
operation of the water distribution network. When the water distribution system is solved independently, the constraint for the tank 
elevation becomes binding, as shown in Fig. 4b. The volume in the tank is exploited in an optimal way – all stored water in the tank is 
consumed, which reduces the pump’s speed and saves power. In order to meet the closure constraint for the tank, pump power is 
increased during the night. When the water network is operated conjunctively with the power grid, the system aims to minimize power 
generation costs at the conventional generator, which leads to accounting for power availability at the solar-powered generator. As a 
result, it is no longer optimal to empty the tank completely. Instead of the pump power being the parameter influencing the most on the 
objective, the exploitation of solar energy becomes the most prominent factor. The availability of solar power dictates the time periods 
in which the tank should be filled, and as a result – when pump power demand should peak. Since pump power peaks at noon, the 
pump can operate under reduced power demand during the evening and night, as shown in Fig. 4a.
Fig. 5 shows the pump status obtained in each of the solutions.
It can be observed that the pump is operating most hours of the day, as is also shown in Fig. 4a. However, there is a slight difference 
during early morning hours. Since the tank is set to be full at the beginning of the simulation, using its contents is favoured over 
pumping during that period. When the WDS is solved independently, larger amounts of stored water could be exploited, as discussed 
above, which allows for the pump to stay shut for a longer period of time during the morning hours. When operated conjunctively, the 
system aims to keep the tank level at a higher level in order to not invest power in the filling of the tank, which encourages pumping to 
start earlier.

Table 7 helps grasp the level of complexity of each of the problems. In illustrative case study I, the WDS optimal operation problem 
includes more decision variables than the OPF problem, although the number of constraints is lower. Illustrative case study II presents 
a different scenario, where the power grid requires more decision variables and more constraints as well. The WDS problem requires 
significantly more computation time, which is a result of the formulation of the problem. The power flow problem was formulated 
using the bus injection model, which allows to formulate the problem as a quadratic programming problem. The WDS problem, 
however, was formulated explicitly as a non-linear problem.
As mentioned in the methodology section, the objective value of the two independent problems solution was obtained by solving 
the optimal operation of the WDS, with the objective of minimizing pump power, and then inserting the solution as known loads to the 
OPF problem. As the conjunctive operation problem is far more complex than either of the sub-problems, it can be observed in Table 7
that its solution requires significantly higher number of iterations and a longer running time, which exceed the combined number of 
iterations and running times for the solutions of the independent optimization problems.
Fig. 16 presents reduction rates of total power generation cost as a result of solving the conjunctive operation problem, compared to 
solving the two problems separately, for each of the illustrative case studies, as presented in the previous section. In order to calculate 
the power generation cost for the independent WDS, the optimal solution obtained for the independent WDS was inserted as a known 
load into the power grid, and the power grid operation was then optimized, meaning that this solution was virtually obtained in two 
stages, as opposed to the conjunctive model which was solved all at once.
4. Conclusions
In this study, the problems of optimal WDS operation and optimal power flow are combined into one joint optimization problem. 
This conjunctive model is fully formulated and applied on two illustrative case studies, for which it is solved using a built-in MATLAB© 
solver. The problem formulation contains a unique formulation of the pump scheduling problem as an NLP problem, which affects the 
entire optimization problem and helps avoid a MINLP problem. In these two illustrative case studies, a power grid containing a 
conventional generator and a sustainable energy source is supplying a WDS with power for pumping purposes. The objective of the

conjunctive operation is minimizing power production costs. The obtained results reveal a complex relationship between the two 
systems, which influences the optimal operation of the WDS. This effect is prominently observed by the comparison of the conjunctive 
optimal operation to the optimal operation of the two independent systems, as was presented for the two illustrative case studies.
The goal of case study I was to examine the solution on a very simple system, so trends could be relatively prominent and clear to 
trace. The goals of case study II were to slightly complexify the examined system, allow for a more flexible operation and broaden the 
solution space, inorder to understand how it would affect the optimal operational policy. These goals were to be achieved while 
maintaining a system that is feasible to solve in reasonable running times, considering the non-linear solver that was used as part of the 
solution method. While both case studies are relatively small scaled, their examination and analysis were kept mainly qualitative, 
focusing on the different trade-offs and interrelationships between components such as pumps, tanks, sustainable power generators 
and conventional power generators. This was done in order to draw general conclusions about the nature of the conjunctive operation, 
which would be beneficial for larger and more realistic infrastructure systems, such as the location of a pumping unit inside the power 
grid, the manner in which pump power peaks could change depending on the type of generators in the power grid, or the very idea of 
considering the operational costs of the power grid when developing an optimal operation policy for the WDS.
The considered objective function, presented in equation (2), is commonly used for generator cost. As such, along with the 
mathematical cost, it represents the environmental impact and operational cost of using a power generator, and therefore serves as a 
meaningful basis for comparison. The suggested conjunctive solution results in reduced total power generation costs of 11.35 % and 
14.58 % for case studies I and II compared with the two-staged solution. With objective value reduction rates exceeding 10 %, this 
result highlights the potential and significance of combining the two models together, and moreover, the importance of a holistic 
approach to solving operation problems. The consideration of both networks at the same time allows the trade-offs between the 
different components, which were discussed throughout the paper, to come into effect, and for the algorithm to take advantage of those 
trade-offs.
One of the purposes of this paper is to provide more generic and accurate modelling of the WDS compared to other studies that 
considered joint management (e.g., flow direction, tank location, etc.). The NLP pump scheduling formulation provided here is suitable 
for systems using variable speed pumps, and relies on reasonable, practical and sensible engineering assumptions. The formulation 
allows avoiding a mixed-integer non-linear programming problem, which can be computationally impractical to solve.
The two networks pose significant mathematical challenges and complexities, and future work should focus on different optimi­
zation methods and solution approaches, such as bi-level optimization, mixed-integer non-linear programming and multi-objective

optimization. As mentioned, the results were obtained using a built-in MATLAB© solver. Although in both examined case studies the 
power generation costs managed to be reduced, as the problem grew, it became more challenging for the solver, which is reflected by 
the increased number of iterations and computation time. As such, the direct use of built-in solvers might not be suitable for large-scale 
real-life power grids or water distribution networks. This paper is aimed mainly at presenting an approach of a conjunctive operation 
and identifying its advantages. Future work will be dedicated to developing better solution methods that would take advantage of the 
problem characteristics and allow us to apply this concept to much larger networks. That said, it is important to mention that this 
conjunctive approach is would best suit systems of a regional scale, as those systems are usually fitting for the non-linear formulation 
that was presented, and more importantly, pumping stations would play a more significant role inside the power grid, thus enabling a 
significant cost reduction to benefit operators.
As water quality is a major challenge in WDS operation, future work could also look into the effects of such an approach on water 
quality, as it may pose additional challenges or even damage water quality.
Many possible research directions are also available on the power grid side. One pf them could be the inclusion of various power 
grid components such as switches and transformers, which could make the problem much more complex and make the operation more 
flexible. Another is the inclusion of different power sources such as hydroelectric generators or wind, which may pose different 
challenges to the operation of the entire system. The inclusion of power quality constraints is also an interesting direction, which would 
limit the magnitude of voltage and current fluctuations.
