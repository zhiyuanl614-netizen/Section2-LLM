A B S T R A C T  
The optimal power-water flow (OPWF) is a fundamental tool for operating integrated power and water networks. 
To address uncertainties in renewable energy and load demands, researchers have used chance-constrained 
models to investigate OPWF under uncertainty. However, existing work has limitations, such as over­
simplified network representations, intractable problem formulations, and high computational burdens. This 
paper presents a chance-constrained OPWF formulation that incorporates detailed network constraints and 
stochastic uncertainties from wind speeds and electricity/water demands. By leveraging the concept of quantiles 
of stochastic variables and convexification techniques, the model is recast into a deterministic mixed-integer 
second-order cone programming (MISOCP) problem with uncertain margins. To efficiently solve this problem, 
we propose a novel iterative algorithm assisted by deep neural networks (DNNs). The algorithm integrates two 
DNNs: one designed to solve power and water flow equations, accelerating the probabilistic evaluation of un­
certain margins; and the other to predict pipeline flows and refine variable ranges, facilitating a warm-start 
approach to the MISOCP model. Case studies conducted on the IEEE 123-node feeder coupled with a 67-node 
water distribution network validate the two DNNs and the overall iterative algorithm. The findings demon­
strate that the proposed DNN-assisted approach effectively balances system security and economy. Compared to 
existing algorithms, the relative error of the optimization solution is less than 0.03%, with a speedup ratio 
exceeding 200.   
1. Introduction 
1.1. Motivations 
The electrical power and water systems are typically planned and 
operated as separate entities, although they are intrinsically interde­
pendent. This interdependency, known as the water-energy nexus [1,2], 
has become increasingly significant due to the growing demand for both 
electricity and water resources. It is estimated that approximately 40 % 
of the water withdrawn is used for power production, specifically for 
cooling and fuel extraction [1]. On the other hand, water and waste­
water services are highly energy-intensive, accounting for about 3−4 % 
of the total electricity consumption in the USA [3], and the average 
energy consumption for urban water supply in China in 2011 was 
recorded at 0.29 kWh/m3 [4]. In addition, over 80 % of the energy 
consumption within the water sector is ascribed to water-pumping ac­
tivities [2]. 
Over the last decade, global attention has intensified on the 
development of multi-energy systems [5,6]. This evolution has led to the 
emergence of diverse multi-energy configurations, including power-gas 
[7,8], power-heat [9], power-transportation [10], power-gas-heat [11], 
and power-gas-hydrogen systems [12]. The goal of these integrated 
systems is to identify synergies between different energy carriers, 
enhancing overall efficiency, reliability, and sustainability. Recently, 
there has been growing interest in exploring synergies between power 
and water systems, aligning with the broader concept of multi-energy 
systems. Numerous studies have proposed the coordinated operation 
of these two systems from an integrated perspective [3,13-16], with a 
specific problem known as the optimal power-water flow (referred to as 
optimal PWF or OPWF, and also named optimal water-power flow in 
[13]). 
The OPWF problem is an extension of the classic optimal power flow 
(OPF) problem for electrical power systems within the context of inte­
grated power and water systems (IPWSs). It aids system operators in 
making well-informed decisions to minimize the overall operational 
costs, meet power and water demands, and satisfy engineering and

1.2. Literature review 
1.2.1. OPWF models and methods 
Many efforts have been made to address the OPWF problem at the 
distribution level, as outlined in [13-16]. Ref. [13] is an early contri­
bution to OPWF formulation, where the PDN is depicted using the bus 
injection model, and the WDN is characterized by pipelines with pre­
determined directions and variable-speed pumps. The Darcy–Weisbach 
equations are employed to articulate the head losses in pipelines. Later 
investigations, as indicated in [14-16], consistently apply the branch 
flow model for the PDN, presupposing unfixed pipeline directions. 
Ref. [14] and [16] apply the Darcy-Weisbach equations, whereas [8] 
adopts the Hazen-Williams equations for pipeline description. In terms 
of pump speed control, variable-speed pumps are investigated in [15] 
and [9], whereas [14] is dedicated to fixed-speed pumps. 
The OPWF problem is inherently a non-convex and non-linear pro­
gramming problem. To address this problem, a variety of approaches 
have been employed, including piecewise linearization [15], 
second-order cone (SOC) relaxation [14,16], the convex hull technique 
[14],

In addition to the OPWF problems at the distribution level, there are 
also studies on OPWF involving bulk transmission networks. For 
instance, Refs. [3] and [17] have explored the potential of water dis­
tribution networks (WDNs) in providing energy flexibility [3] or ac­
commodating wind power [17] to bulk transmission systems through 
OPWF. 
1.2.2. Chance-constraied OPWF 
All the studies in [3,13-17] are deterministic optimizations, 
neglecting the inherent uncertainty of RE sources and load demands. 
However, 
these

Common methods for addressing uncertainties in optimizations 
include stochastic optimization and robust optimization. Stochastic opti­
mization typically involves modeling uncertainties as random variables 
with known probability distributions and dealing with expected values 
and probabilistic constraints [18]. In contrast, robust optimization seeks 
solutions that are feasible across all uncertainty scenarios, emphasizing 
the worst-case scenario [19]. It operates without assuming known dis­
tributions, however, robust solutions can be overly conservative. Both 
methods are widely used in power and energy system optimization [6, 
20]. Additionally, there are hybrid methods of stochastic and robust 
optimization, such as distributionally robust optimization [19] or the 
stochastic-robust model proposed in [21] to optimize the operation of 
isolated energy communities. 
In this paper, we focus on chance-constrained programming (CCP), 
which is a specific form of stochastic optimization. It introduces addi­
tional chance constraints to deterministic optimization, ensuring that 
the probability of violating certain constraints remains below a specified 
threshold. 
CCP has been extensively studied in the OPF formulation in power 
systems. Recently, several studies have also resorted to CCP to address 
the OPWF problem under uncertainty. Ref. [22] formulated an OPWF 
problem incorporating uncertain RE generation, modeling the RE gen­
eration as a CCP problem and solving it using the probability-efficient 
point (PEP) method. The authors of [23-26] expanded their series of 
related studies to address uncertainties in water loads [23], power loads 
[24,26], or both [25] by incorporating chance constraints of violating 
network constraints into the distribution-level OPWF problem. They 
tackled these chance-constrained OPWF (CC–OPWF) problems using 
scenario-based methods [23-25]. However, due to the limited scalability 
of the scenario approach for larger problems, the authors proposed in

1.2.3. Fixed-point-iteration-based CC-OPF and CC-OPWF 
The fixed-point-iteration algorithm was proposed in [29] as a solution 
to chance-constrained OPF. It is built upon the concept of uncertain 
margin previously defined by the same authors in [30]. The uncertain 
margin represents an estimation of the quantiles of uncertain variables 
and is interpreted as network constraint tightening to accommodate 
uncertainties from RE generation and power demands. The 
fixed-point-iteration algorithm is notable for its ability to decompose the 
CCP optimization process into successive steps of solving a deterministic 
OPF problem and conducting a probabilistic evaluation of uncertain 
margins. Initially, the iteration algorithm was developed based on par­
tial linearization of the AC power flow equations as well as the 
assumption of Gaussian uncertainties to facilitate the analytical refor­
mulation of chance constraints [30]. The sample-based approach, such 
as the Monte Carlo (MC) method, was subsequently incorporated into 
the iteration to accommodate non-linear models and non-Gaussian un­
certainties [31]. However, the evaluation of uncertain margins typically 
requires tens of thousands of MC simulations at each iteration, posing a 
challenge to computational efficiency. To address this issue, alternative 
approaches such as the three-point estimation [32] and the 
response-surface approach [33] are employed to accelerate the uncer­
tainty evaluation. Moreover, the non-convex nature of the OPF problem 
presents another challenge for the iteration algorithm, as it relies on 
repeatedly solving the problem. Consequently, [32] employs a linear­
ized network model, while [34] applies SOC relaxations to address the 
convex problem. 
To the best of our knowledge, our previous work [28] was the first 
and the only attempt to apply the fixed-point-iteration algorithm to 
CC–OPWF, however, it had limitations in network representation, 
intractability, and computation efficiency. Specifically, the power sys­
tem was oversimplified by using the DC power model, the OPWF 
formulated was intractable due to nonlinear and non-convex constraints 
within the water network, and the probabilistic evaluation is conducted 
through repeatedly solving of PWF equations using the Newton-Raphson 
(N-R) method, posing great challenges to computation efficiency. 
1.3. Research gap and contributions 
From the literature review, a comparative summary of the studies on 
CC–OPWF is presented in Table 1. The research gaps in the context of 
CC–OPWF can be identified as follows:  
1) Incomplete modeling of uncertainties, network representation, and 
regulations. For instance, most studies do not account for regulations 
concerning pipe direction and pump speed in CC–OPWF.  
2) Challenges in effectively and efficiently solving the CC–OPWF 
problem. The scenario approach used in [23-25] and the 
fixed-point-iteration method we previously used in [28] are not 
applied to large-scale systems. Additionally, the reformulation 
methods proposed in [26] and [27] require uncertainty factors to 
follow Gaussian distributions and necessitate model linearization. 
Therefore, further research is needed to develop a more efficient and 
scalable method to address the intractability and computational 
burden associated with CC–OPWF. 
To fill these identified gaps, this work aims to develop a novel 
method for addressing the challenges in modeling and solving the 
CC–OPWF problem, particularly with the assistance of deep neural 
networks (DNNs). Recent years have witnessed the growing successful 
application of DNNs in power flow [35], probabilistic power flow [36, 
37], optimal power flow [38,39], optimal gas flow [40], optimal 
power-gas flow [41], and the integrated scheduling of power and gas 
systems [42]. Inspired by these applications, particularly in fitting high 
non-linear power flow equations [36,37] and predicting the optimal 
range of gas flow [41], as well as specifying warm-starts for power-gas 
optimization [40], we propose leveraging DNN to improve the 
fixed-point-iteration algorithm for CC–OPWF. 
The main contributions of this work are twofold:  
• CC–OPWF formulation. A CC–OPWF problem is formulated by 
integrating detailed power and water network constraints, bi- 
directional water flow, pump speed regulation, and considerations 
of uncertainties in power and water demands, as well as RE gener­
ations. This problem is subsequently reformulated into a tractable 
MISOCP problem through the application of uncertain margins, SOC 
relaxation, and the McCormick envelopes. 
• CC–OPWF solution. A novel fixed-point-iteration algorithm incor­
porating two DNNs, referred to as Deep-PWF and Deep-OPWF, is 
proposed to provide an efficient and scalable solution to the 
CC–OPWF problem. Deep-PWF is developed and trained to predict 
PWF solutions, thereby accelerating the MC simulations for uncer­
tainty evaluation. The cascade of Deep-OPWF and Deep-PWF is

3.4. Fixed-point-iteration algorithm for R-CC-OPWF 
We apply the fixed-point-iteration algorithm from [31] to solve the 
CC–OPWF problem. The algorithm alternates between solving the 
deterministic R-CC–OPWF problem under the given vector of tighten­
ings λ(k−1) to obtain the optimal [x(k),u(k)], and evaluating λ(k) based on 
[x,u](k) through either analytical methods or sample-based methods 
such as MC simulations, where kis the iteration count. The process ter­
minates when the tightenings λ do not change between iterations. 
Details of the fixed-point-iteration algorithm to solve the CC–OPWF 
problem are provided in Algorithm 1. MC simulation has clear advan­
tages over analytical methods since it requires neither simplification 
(such as linearization) of PWF equations nor any particular assumption 
about the probability distribution of uncertainties. Results of MC sim­
ulations are also typically used as a reference to verify the analytical 
methods. However, MC simulation is usually computationally intensive. 
Consequently, in this work, we apply MC simulation to evaluate λ and 
utilize the DNN technique to mitigate the computational burden. 
Algorithm 1: Fixed-point-iteration algorithm for CC–OPWF 
1. Initialize the iteration count k=1 and uncertain margins λ(k-1) =0. 
2. While k<kmax do 
3. Solve the deterministic R-CC–OPWF problem with λ(k−1) to 
obtain the optimal control variables u(k) and dependent variables x(k). 
4. Evaluate λ(k) via MC simulation: 
1) Generate samples of forecast errors in power and water loads, and 
wind speeds based on their probabilistic distribution. 
2) Calculate the corresponding power and water loads and wind 
powers via (8a)–(8c) and (10). 
3) Solve the PWF equations (7b) based on u(k) and power/water loads 
and wind powers to obtain the samples of x(k). 
4) Conduct statistical analysis on the samples of x(k) by verifying 
(12c)–(12o) to detect the chance constraints that do not meet the pre­
defined violation probabilities of εv, εl, εP, εQ, εq, and εH. 
5) Update the uncertain margins λ of the violated chance constraints 
via (16a) and (16b). 
5. Check convergence by comparing the maximum change |λ(k)- 
λ(k-1)| with the predefined threshold η. If |λ(k)-λ(k-1)|≤η, go to step 7 to 
stop the iteration; otherwise update k=k+1. 
6. end while 
7. Output the final u(k) and x(k). 
4. The proposed DNN-Assisted solution for CC-OPWF 
Although the fixed-point-iteration algorithm successfully transforms 
the original CC–OPWF into alternating between solving the determin­
istic R-CC–OPWF and updating the uncertain margin λ, there are still 
two challenges. First, evaluating λ based on MC simulation is compu­
tationally intensive since it requires repeatedly solving the PWF equa­
tions (7b) multiple times. The second challenge is how to solve the 
nonlinear and nonconvex R-CC–OPWF problem. 
To address the first challenge, we propose training a DNN-based 
surrogate model, referred to as Deep-PWF, to map the power and 
water loads, wind speeds, and control variables u to the dependent 
variables x. As for the second challenge, we suggest reformulating the R- 
CC–OPWF problem into a tractable problem with SOC relaxation and 
McCormick envelopes. Additionally, we recommend utilizing another 
deep neural network, referred to as Deep-OPWF, to predict the optimal 
1) nodal heads and 2) flow rates of pipes and pumps, and then use these 
predictions as warm starts to facilitate the solve of the R-CC–OPWF 
problem.

1) Data acquisition. Due to the lack of the historical power and water 
flow data of the integrated system studied, we generate training data 
by simulation. More specifically, input data—wind speeds, power 
and water loads, and control variables u={Pgen, Qgen, vslack, ω, 
qsrc}—are randomly generated. The corresponding output data are 
derived by solving the PWF equations (7b) via the N-R method. All 
data are then divided into training and testing sets based on a 
specified ratio, such as 4:1.  
2) Preprocess. We apply the z-score normalization method [46] to 
preprocess both input and output data. This method standardizes 
each feature by subtracting the mean and dividing it by the standard 
deviation (STD), resulting in a distribution with a mean of 0 and an 
STD of 1. This preprocessing not only equalizes the scales of different 
features but also reduces the influence of outliers, thereby facili­
tating a more stable and effective training phase.  
3) Activation function choice. The widely recognized ReLU activation 
function is chosen due to its effectiveness in addressing the vanishing 
gradient problem and its computational efficiency.  
4) Loss function definition. The mean squared error (MSE) is used as the 
loss function to evaluate the performance of the Deep-PWF. MSE 
provides a clear and straightforward measure of the average squared 
difference between the predicted and actual values, which guides the 
optimization process toward minimizing prediction errors.  
5) Learning algorithm and rate adjustment: The root mean square 
propagation 
(RMSProp) 
algorithm 
[47] 
is 
chosen 
as 
the 
back-propagation algorithm. We initiate the learning rate at 0.001 
and incorporate a rate reduction strategy that diminishes the 
learning rate by half when the loss function shows a decrease of less 
than a specified threshold over 5 consecutive epochs, ensuring that 
fine adjustments are made as the network approaches convergence.  
6) Early stopping mechanism: To safeguard against overfitting and to 
promote the development of a generalizable model, an early stop­
ping strategy is integrated into our training routine. This technique 
intervenes if the validation loss does not improve by more than a set 
threshold for 20 consecutive epochs, indicating that the model has 
potentially begun to memorize the training data rather than learning 
to generalize from it.

4.2. Deep-OPWF 
Similar to representing the PWF problem with the mapping (19), the 
R-CC–OPWF problem can also be expressed through another mapping: 
M OPWF : yOPWF→zOPWF
(21) 
The difference between MPWF (19) and MOPWF (21) lies in the defi­
nitions of inputs y and outputs z. In the newly defined MOPWF, yOPWF 
collects the variables of wind speeds, power loads, water loads, as well as 
uncertain margins λ, while zOPWF corresponds to the optimal solution of 
the R-CC–OPWF problem. 
From (21), the Deep-OPWF with a structure similar to the Deep-PWF 
shown in Fig. 3 can be constructed and trained. We also use ReLU 
activation, the z-score-based pre-process, the RMSProp algorithm, and 
the MES loss function to train the Deep-OPWF. However, more hidden 
layers and more neurons in each hidden layer are needed in Deep-OPWF 
than in Deep-PWF because the mapping MPWF is essentially the PWF 
equations with a relatively clear relationship between yPWF and zPWF, 
while the relationship between yOPWF and zOPWF of the mapping MOPWF 
is more complex. Moreover, the data for training Deep-OPWF is gener­
ated by solving the R-CC–OPWF problem under massive wind and load 
scenarios with the solver SCIP [48]. 
To speed up the training and guarantee satisfying the equality con­
straints (7b) of the R-CC–OPWF, we only use the neural networks to 
learn control variables, rather than simultaneously learning both control 
variables and state variables. That is, only u rather than [u, x] is selected 
as the output of the Deep-OPWF. After the Deep-OPWF has been trained, 
it can now be used to predict u given yOPWF, and x can then be deter­
mined via solving the PWF equations (7b). Recalling that the Deep-PWF 
has already been trained to solve the PWF problem, the optimal solution 
of the R-CC–OPWF is predicted through a cascade of the two neural 
networks, as depicted in Fig. 4. 
Although the optimal solution predicted in Fig. 4 satisfies the 
equality constraints of the R-CC–OPWF, it does not necessarily ensure 
all the inequalities are satisfied. Therefore, the predicted solution is not 
directly applied to the fixed-point iteration, but rather as warm-starts for 
the R-CC–OPWF problem. In addition, since the R-CC–OPWF is 
intractable due to the nonlinear power equations (1d), as well as the 
hydraulic Eqs. (4a)–(4d), we will apply the SOC relaxation and 
McCormick envelopes to approximate these constraints before using the 
learned warm-starts to help solve the problem.

5.2. Evaluation of the Deep-PWF 
As explained in Section 4.1, the trained Deep-PWF functions essen­
tially as a surrogate model of the PWF equations (7b). Consequently, its 
forward propagation can be considered a deterministic solver for these 
equations. We evaluate its performance in this section with the 
following steps: 1) Generate massive samples randomly by sampling 
Gaussian forecast errors in loads and the Weibull random wind speeds 
via MC simulation. 2) Determine all the voltages, currents, power flows, 
nodal heads, and water flow rates through all pipes and pumps of each 
scenario given a set of control variables using the trained Deep-PWF and 
the N-R method respectively to solve (7b). 3) Evaluate the relative errors 
between the results of Deep-PWF and the N-R method. 
Both C1 and C2 are simulated and similar evaluation results are 
obtained. Only the results of C2 are provided and discussed below. Let σ 
be the standard deviations (STDs) of the Gaussian forecast errors in 
power and water loads (8a)–(8c). Assuming σ is 10 %, 15 %, and 20 % of 
the forecasted loads. Table 3 compares the mean values and STDs of the 
10,000 scenarios by MC simulation with the trained Deep-PWF and the 
N-R method. Due to space limitations, only four variables are presented 
as demonstration examples. 
Table 3 shows that both means and STDs derived from the two 
methods are almost the same across all assessed variables and levels of 
load forecast errors. The maximum relative error (ε%) of means and 
STDs does not exceed 6.7e-2. This suggests that the Deep-PWF is a 
reliable surrogate for the N-R method in predicting system states. 
Furthermore, it is noted that with an increase in the STD of forecast error 
(σ), the STDs of all four variables increase accordingly, while the means 
remain relatively stable, as anticipated. 
The probability distributions obtained by the two methods are also 
compared. Fig. 7 and Fig. 8 respectively present the cumulative distri­
bution of H10 for σ being 10 % and 20 % of the forecasted value. The 
distribution curves of the Deep-PWF closely match those of the N-R 
method very well. Additionally, the distribution of H10 closely resembles 
a Gaussian distribution, with a mean of approximately 70 m, consistent 
with the simulation results in Table 3. When comparing the scenario 
with a 10 % STD in Fig. 7 to a 20 % STD in Fig. 8, the PDF curve in Fig. 8 
appears flatter and wider, indicating a broader range of possible values 
for H10 and a more dispersed distribution as expected. 
Results of Table 3, Fig. 7, and Fig. 8 validate the precision of the 
Deep-PWF. We also evaluate the computation efficiency of both methods 
in Table 4 with an STD of 10 % in load forecast error and MC sample 
sizes of 5000, 10,000, and 50,000. The results clearly indicate that the 
Deep-PWF exhibits a significant efficiency advantage over the N-R 
method, completing all simulations within seconds and achieving a 
speed-up of over 280 for all sample sizes. Moreover, as the sample size 
grows, the computation time of the Deep-PWF only slightly increases, 
while the time required by the N-R method is nearly proportional to the 
number of samples. These findings indicate that the Deep-PWF model is 
more scalable and better suited for larger simulations.

6. Conclusions 
We have expanded upon our previous work on CC–OPWF by 
reformulating a tractable problem and integrating DNNs into the fixed- 
point-iteration algorithm. The optimization model has been recast as a 
tractable MISOCP problem through SOC relaxation and McCormick 
envelope. Two DNNs are developed and trained to enhance the algo­
rithm: one predicts the PWF solution to accelerate the uncertainty 
evaluation based on MC simulations, while the other provides warm- 
starts for optimization by predicting water flow directions and refining 
variable limits. Numerical results from the IEEE 123-node feeder 
coupled with the 67-node water distribution network validate the 
computational accuracy and efficiency of the two DNNs and the overall 
iterative algorithms. 
The main results are summarized as follows:  
1) DNN accurately replicates the PWF solution and achieves over a 200- 
fold speed-up for uncertainty evaluation.  
2) With DNN warm starts, the cost gap for OPWF reduces from 1.2 % to 
0.01 %, and the maximum violation of relaxed equality constraints 
decreases from around 0.7 to below 4.5e-3, while saving nearly 40 % 
of computation time.  
3) The proposed DNN-assisted approach provides an effective balance 
between security and economy for power-water network coordina­
tion under uncertainty. Compared to the existing iterative algorithm, 
the DNN-assisted algorithm proposed achieves a relative error of 
only 0.03 % in operation costs, while significantly reducing the so­
lution time with a speedup ratio exceeding 200. 
In future work, we plan to expand the proposed iterative approach to 
the OPWF problem with joint chance constraints. Additionally, we 
intend to explore physics-informed learning approaches to enhance the 
learning capabilities of our DNNs, as suggested in [56].
