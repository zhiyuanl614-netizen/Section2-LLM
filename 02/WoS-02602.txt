A B S T R A C T  
A new energy generation system combining a solar chimney power plant (SCPP) with a nuclear power plant 
(NPP) cooling tower has been developed and assessed in Irbid, Jordan. The surplus hot air from the NPP is 
directly routed into the SCPP collector, increasing the initial air temperature and boosting the air profile of the 
SCPP, improving the air velocity inside the chimney and boosting electricity and water production. The inte­
gration of these two systems (SCPP+NPP) has shown significant improvements in electricity and water pro­
duction compared to a standalone SCPP. The integrated system provides reliable electricity production 
throughout the day (24/7), thanks to the contribution of heat from the NPP side. Key results include a maximum 
electricity production rate of about 238 kW at approximately noon, twice the productive value of a conventional 
SCPP. The average monthly water and electricity production increased from 9.44 ktons and 25.73 MWh in the 
SCPP to 22.96 ktons and 63.81.64 MWh in the integrated system. The SCPP+NPP system peaks electricity 
production of 69.4 MWh in December, underscoring the positive influence of the added nuclear heat. While 
March emerges as the month of the maximum of water production (25.6 ktons) in the SCPP+NPP system, the 
SCPP water production peaks at 14.9 ktons in July. The results also show that the integrated system demonstrates 
a remarkable monthly electricity and water production of 63.81 MWh and 22.96 ktons, surpassing the standalone 
SCPP’s production (25.73 MWh and 9.44 ktons), respectively. These results demonstrate this innovative solu­
tion’s potential for optimizing excess heat produced by the NPP and the potential to revolutionize the energy 
industry.   
1. Introduction 
Considering a critical worldwide energy crisis and the necessity for a 
stable future, there is an increasing focus on implementing renewable 
and sustainable technology (Al-Ghriybah et al., 2023). The primary 
objective is to reduce dependence on fossil fuels and limit the accom­
panying CO2 emissions (Ayadi et al., 2022; Fawaier et al., 2021; Abdalla 
et al., 2022), (Ubando et al., 2020). Such initiatives are a proactive 
response to current energy challenges and a deliberate stride toward 
fostering lasting environmental resilience and energy stability (Naqvi 
et al., 2021; Atabani et al., 2023). The utilization of solar energy tech­
nology is a promising and eco-friendly approach for clean power gen­
eration from renewable sources, harnessing the sun’s radiant energy 
through various technologies such as photovoltaic cells (Bani Yaseen 
et al., 2020; Farajollahi et al., 2023; Khalil and Hassan, 2020). This 
method significantly reduces pollution compared to conventional fossil 
fuels, contributing to a healthier planet by lowering the carbon footprint 
and combating climate change (Khalil and Hassan, 2020; Parsamehr 
et al., 2019). Solar power also offers decentralized solutions, improving 
energy security and minimizing transmission inefficiencies, with struc­
tures being installable on rooftops, in remote areas, or integrated into

The SCPP integrates vital components for solar energy capture and 
electricity production, illustrated in Fig. 1. The collector, a broad, 
transparent-coated surface, allows sunlight to heat the air beneath. A 
central chimney facilitates the upward flow of heated air, with a turbine

ergy. The generator then transforms this into electrical energy for the 
grid. A cooling system may be added to prevent overheating, while a 
control system monitors and regulates parameters for efficient and safe 
operation. Together, these components utilize natural convection and 
temperature differences, creating an eco-friendly and sustainable elec­
tricity generation system (Pradhan et al., 2021; Krumar Mandal et al., 
2022; Maghrabie et al., 2022; Jim´enez-Xam´an et al., 2019). 
Several advancements have been explored in conventional solar 
chimneys. Abdelsalam et al. (2020a) introduced a hybrid solar chimney 
power plant (HSCPP) design, combining a cooling tower with conven­
tional technology, demonstrating nearly 50% more power production 
than traditional SCPPs. Salameh et al. (2022) used MATLAB simulations 
to analyze the SCPP performance in the UAE, finding differences in 
water production and power generation between Sharjah and Al Ain. 
Alkasrawi et al. (2021) investigated the integration of photovoltaic (PV) 
chimneys into SCPPs, resulting in a 17% increase in water and 30% in 
electricity production, reducing energy costs by 36%. Pandey et al. 
(2021) proposed a SCPP model with a heat exchanger for nighttime 
operation, achieving a 5% increase in efficiency during cloudy and 
nighttime conditions using computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Despite 
scientific research and the growth of published papers on the SCPP, 
thermal efficiency still needs to improve (Kassai and Al-Hyari, 2020; 
Kafiah et al., 2020). The focus of different studies is how to improve the 
performance of the SCPPs, enhance their economic viability, and 
examine their capability for reliable performance. Various numerical 
and simulation studies have contributed to the understanding and 
optimization of the SCPPs. Guo et al. (2015) employed a 3D numerical 
method to simulate SCPPs, considering different turbines, solar loads, 
and radiation to determine optimal power-regulating approaches. Lo 
Sciuto et al. (2016) proposed a hybrid energy storage technology, 
integrating soil and water with neural network-based calculations 
showing agreement with finite element simulations. Attig-Bahar et al. 
(2021) utilized CFD to investigate the impact of collector diameter, 
elevation, and chimney radius on electricity production capacity, lead­
ing to optimized dimensions for enhanced power generation. Abdelsa­
lam et al. (2023) assessed the feasibility of deploying a HSCPP in the 
Arabian Gulf. The work assessed the performance of the HSCPP in 
different cities in the Arabian Gulf and the impact of weather variations 
on its performance in producing electricity and distilled water. Miqdam

The SCPPs extend their potential beyond power generation by 
contributing to sustainable water production, addressing the growing 
global demand for potable water. This approach provides an eco- 
friendly energy source for desalination, mitigating environmental im­
pacts associated with conventional desalination technologies (Gha­
lamchi et al., 2016). The process involves utilizing solar radiation to 
heat seawater below the collector, forming vapor, which is then 
condensed to produce potable water. The chimney effect, driven by 
temperature differences between the collector and the chimney, opti­
mizes air circulation in desalination. Key variables influencing SCPP’s 
effectiveness in desalination include temperature distribution within the 
collector, humidity, wind velocity, and chimney size. 
The current literature trend emphasizes integrating SCPPs with other 
systems to enhance performance, increase power generation, and reduce 
overall energy costs. The goal is to leverage the strengths of each tech­
nology while mitigating their limitations. However, gaps and obstacles 
in knowledge include ideal arrangements, operational settings, and 
regulatory procedures for optimal integrated system performance. 
Thermal compatibility poses a challenge, especially in managing tem­
peratures and utilizing surplus heat from nuclear facilities for SCPPs. 
Precise models considering dynamic factors like weather conditions, 
energy demand fluctuations, and variations in nuclear power production 
are essential (Younas et al., 2022). The optimization of ideal parameters 
revealed that a collector diameter of 900 m, a chimney elevation of 
0.462 kilometers, and a radius of 5 m yield an annual power output of 
2.1 GWh. A Solar Chimney Power Water Desalination Plant (SCPWDP) 
was designed and analyzed for the port of Aqaba in Jordan (Abdelsalam 
et al., 2020b). The mathematical model for the SCPWDP was developed 
and verified. The plant demonstrated the capability to sequentially 
produce 123,000 tons of distilled water annually and generate 481,440 
kWh of power. The economic study revealed a Levelized Cost of Energy 
(LCOE) for the SCPWDP at $1.86 per kWh. This investigation examines 
the thermal characteristics of a solar chimney plant, utilizing a carefully 
devised and executed experimental system to assess parameters such as 
ambient temperature, temperature distribution, ground temperature, 
and air velocity. The outcomes underscore the substantial impact of 
solar radiation and environmental temperature on the system, eluci­
dating a temperature differential from 21 to 26 ◦C between ambient 
conditions and the collector’s discharge air temperature. An experi­
mental setup for an integrated system prototype of solar chimneys has 
been established, and its performance was assessed under practical 
weather conditions in Nanjing, China. The study revealed that the in­
tegrated system could simultaneously achieve multiple objectives, 
encompassing energy production and freshwater generation (Zuo et al., 
2021). 
Other studies showed improved electricity production by to using 
waste heat from Nuclear Power Plant (NPP). However, the potential of 
water desalination when integrating the SCPP with NPP was never 
addressed. Hence, this work proposes an innovative integration method 
connecting a conventional SCPP with an NPP. The surplus hot air from 
the NPP’s cooling tower is channeled into the SCPP’s collector to assess 
performance. The study explores waste heat’s impact on temperature 
distribution, efficiency, electricity output, and water production. It in­
vestigates continuous SCPP operation, even at night, using the updraft 
strength. A MATLAB-based mathematical model, relying on mass and 
energy balance equations, evaluates the proposed integration using 
yearly weather data. Irbid a city in Jordan was chosen for its proximity 
to an NPP and favorable weather conditions. Comparisons with standard 
SCPPs provide crucial insights, contributing to knowledge development 
in this field. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. System Description 
This paper presents a novel approach for converting wasted thermal 
energy from NPP into electrical energy when coupled with an SCPP. 
Steam generated in the nuclear reactor passes over the turbine blades, 
produces mechanical energy, and is then directed to the condenser, 
which is cooled and turned into the liquid phase using seawater. 
To utilize this wasted heat, the hot air from the cooling tower is 
directly routed into the collector of the SCPP, as shown in Fig. 2. The hot 
air entering the solar chimney operates as a greenhouse, raising its 
temperature above 35◦C. At the base of the solar chimney is a seawater 
pool was installed. The hot air passes over the water, evaporating some

boosts the temperature of the air under the SCPP, hence increasing water 
and electricity production.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Weather profile 
Fig. 3a displays the annual variation in solar radiation for 24 hours. 
The studied area experiences significant seasonal variations in solar 
radiation, influencing the efficiency and functionality of the SCPP. The 
average solar radiation ranges from 118.12 W/m2 in December to a peak 
of 354.16 W/m2 in June. The varying solar irradiance levels directly 
impact the SCPP’s energy-harnessing potential. During winter, when 
solar radiation is at its lowest, the SCPP may face challenges in gener­
ating sufficient thermal updrafts for effective convection. In contrast, the 
peak solar radiation in summer enhances buoyancy within the chimney, 
contributing to more robust updrafts and increased electricity genera­
tion. The seasonal average solar irradiance highlights a progressive in­
crease from January to June, reaching its peak during summer. This rise, 
from 125.17 W/m2 in January to 354.16 W/m2 in June, signifies the 
country’s transition from winter to summer and the corresponding in­
crease in solar exposure. This pattern is crucial for the SCPP, as higher 
solar irradiance contributes to more robust thermal updrafts and 
increased electricity generation. The maximum solar irradiance values, 
peaking in June with 1073 W/m2, demonstrate the intense solar expo­
sure during the summer season. The months of May to August mark a 
period of high solar energy availability, with a maximum of 1073 W/m2 
and a minimum of 1009 W/m2, providing the SCPP with ideal condi­
tions for harnessing renewable energy. The descending values from 
March to December signify the gradual reduction in solar intensity, 
impacting the SCPP’s performance during the latter part of the year. 
Adaptable SCPP designs must account for these seasonal fluctuations to 
optimize performance and ensure consistent energy production 
throughout the year. 
Fig. 3b depicts the annual variation in ambient temperature over 
24 hours. The highest temperatures align with the peak solar radiation 
in the afternoon, contributing to decreased SCPP efficiency due to 
increased heat loss. These fluctuations are crucial for comprehending 
the potential of solar systems in electricity generation. Seasonal changes 
in ambient temperature, ranging from a minimum of 3.95◦C in January 
to a maximum of 39.54◦C in July, play a pivotal role in shaping the 
thermal dynamics of the SCPP. Monthly temperatures gradually rise 
from an average of 7.01◦C in January to 23.01◦C in August, peaking 
during summer when solar irradiance is also at its highest. This inter­
connected influence of temperature and solar irradiance underscores the 
significance of understanding their combined impact on the SCPP. 
Winter months (November to February) with lower average tempera­
tures (e.g., 13.41◦C in November) may pose challenges for thermal up­
draft generation. More pronounced in summer, diurnal temperature 
variations highlight the season’s impact on temperature dynamics and 
the corresponding SCPP efficiency. While warmer temperatures foster 
vigorous updrafts and increased electricity generation, cooler times, like 
early morning or late afternoon, enhance the solar chimney’s efficiency 
in converting solar energy to electricity. 
Fig. 3c provides a comprehensive view of the annual humidity 
variation over a 24-hour cycle. The seasonal fluctuations in humidity, 
ranging from 31.13% in June to 53.81% in January, play a pivotal role 
in influencing air density, subsequently shaping buoyancy and airflow 
dynamics within the SCPP. During winter (January to March), the 
moderate humidity levels, averaging between 37.39% and 53.81%, 
present challenges in achieving optimal convection within the SCPP. 
The lowest relative humidity in March and lower ambient temperatures 
can increase air density positively, benefiting the SCPP’s performance. 
However, the risk of condensation within the chimney demands careful 
management. Transitioning to spring and summer, a decrease in hu­
midity, reaching its minimum in June at 31.13%, enhances air density, 
fostering more efficient convection within the chimney. This decrease 
contributes to a more robust updraft, ultimately boosting electricity 
generation in the SCPP. Conversely, autumn and early winter witness a 
gradual increase in humidity, peaking at 47.61% in October. This rise in 
humidity and lower solar radiation levels pose challenges in maintaining 
optimal temperature differentials and air buoyancy within the SCPP. 
Integrating humidity considerations into SCPP design becomes crucial 
for adaptability to varying climate conditions and consistent energy 
production throughout the year. The maximum (95%) and minimum 
relative humidity (68%) values in the figure underscore the extremes the 
SCPP system must navigate. Operating effectively across this spectrum 
of humidity conditions emphasizes the significance of flexible and 
resilient SCPP designs capable of harnessing renewable energy in 
diverse climatic scenarios. The observed lower humidity during summer 
months corresponds to higher air density, promoting efficient convec­
tion and enhancing SCPP performance. However, the potential for 
condensation in the chimney during winter necessitates careful 
consideration, highlighting the importance of seasonal adaptability in 
SCPP design. 
The average wind speed (Fig. 3d) ranges from a minimum of 3.00 m/ 
s in October to a peak of 5.30 m/s in February and March, playing a 
essential role in shaping the convection process within the SCPP. The 
moderate wind conditions persisting throughout the year contribute 
significantly to natural ventilation within the chimney, fostering robust 
updrafts crucial for turbine operation and efficient electricity genera­
tion. Peak wind speeds reaching 5.3 m/s in specific months create 
favorable conditions for optimizing power generation and overall sys­
tem performance. Higher wind speeds correlate with increased turbine 
rotation, generating augmented electricity output.

variations; January has an average wind speed of 4.70 m/s, with a 
maximum and minimum of 5.7 and 4.2 m/s, respectively. February

4.70 m/s, with maximum and minimum values of 5.2 m/s and 4.5 m/s,

As the efficiency and performance of the SCPPs are closely tied to the 
natural convection of heat and mass transfer, establishing a connection 
between weather data and energy output is crucial to determine the 
expected outcome. For conventional SCPP, the air temperature at the 
entrance (i.e., ambient temperature) significantly influences the devel­
opment of the temperature profile within the SCPP structure. The 
development temperature profile is a crucial component in determining 
the dynamic movement of the air, the corresponding power generation, 
and system efficiency. The elevated air temperature at the entrance in­
creases the upward force of air inside the chimney, enhancing its overall 
productivity. In contrast, cold temperature might offer a more signifi­
cant differential temperature, potentially increasing system efficiency. 
Steady-state assessments indicate that power output can be affected by 
lower ambient temperatures or increased static ambient pressure. 
Therefore, there is an optimal temperature that the inlet air should have 
to achieve ultimate electric power generation and system performance. 
The performance of the SCPP, on the other hand, is significantly influ­
enced by the wind speed. Higher wind speeds enhance natural ventila­
tion, resulting in a stronger airflow through the chimney and 
maximizing system performance. Research has demonstrated that 
abrupt decreases in nighttime air temperature can amplify power gen­
eration, exposing the complex dynamics involved. Wind can redirect 
indoor hot air via the collector or boost efficiency by generating a suc­
tion effect at the chimney exit. Computational studies demonstrate that 
fluctuations in the surrounding wind speed can influence the overall 
power generation of SCPPs (Zhou et al., 2017). Zhou et al. (2017) 
showed that a temperature difference of 24.1 ◦C between the ambient air 
and the collector outlet can provide a significant driving force for 
airflow within the system. Koonsrisuk and Chitsomboon (Koonsrisuk 
and Chitsomboon, 2009) highlighted the importance of air temperature 
and solar heat absorption on the dynamic flow of the air through the 
system and the corresponding power generation. Ahmed and Hussein 
(2018) showed the importance of the construction material of the SCPP 
base on the development of air flow and the production of electricity. 
The presence of humidity must be considered as it directly impacts 
the density of the air. Increasing the humidity of the air decreases air 
density, which can potentially affect the buoyancy inside the solar 
chimney. High levels of humidity can also affect the water evaporation 
from the sea pool and reduce water desalination. Nevertheless, it has 
been discovered that elevated levels of humidity can cause the 
condensation of vapor, resulting in the release of latent heat and 
enhancing the power capacity of the plant. Furthermore, daily power 
production is affected by relative humidity, and fluctuations in humidity 
levels result in a reduction in the peak-to-valley ratio of the daily power. 
This highlights the complex relationship between humidity and perfor­
mance. Zhou et al. (2017) demonstrated that humidity levels can lead to 
vapor condensation, thereby releasing latent heat and enhancing the 
power potential of the plant. Essentially, adding additional heat, derived 
from the latent heat generated by vapor condensation, acts as a miti­
gating factor, preventing a significant decrease in power output during 
daytime despite a notable increase observed during nighttime.

cooling tower is an innovative and promising solution for harnessing 
and optimizing excess heat generated by NPPs. The system addresses the 
challenges of conventional solar chimney setups and establishes new

4. Conclusion 
Integrating an SCPP with an NPP has been shown to be a highly 
effective solution for improving the production of electricity and pure 
water. The study results demonstrate that the new integrated system can 
produce a maximum electricity production rate of about 238 kW, twice 
the productive value of a conventional chimney, at approximately noon. 
Moreover, in June, the upgraded system reached a peak production rate 
of 69.4 MWh of electricity and an impressive 25.6 kton of distilled 
water. A traditional chimney only generated a meager 40.5 MWh of 
electricity and produced 4.72 kton of pure water, the lowest production 
rate. The integrated system’s ability to produce electricity throughout 
the day, even without sunlight, and produce significantly more pure 
water than a standalone solar chimney makes it a promising solution for 
sustainable energy generation. In addition, the ability to optimize excess 
heat produced by nuclear power plants using this new system can 
potentially revolutionize the energy industry. Producing water from 
renewable energy sources is an attractive characteristic of the SCPP that 
can help the need for clean water in countries where access to clean 
water is scarce. The generated electricity can also be used for additional 
water production using other water purification techniques. Future 
work may include addressing a practical approach of integrating the air 
from the NPP to the SCPP. Future work could also include optimizing the 
design of the SCPP to fit the integration with the NPP.
