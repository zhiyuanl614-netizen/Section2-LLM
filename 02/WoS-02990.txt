A B S T R A C T
A crucial aspect of a sustainable city is ensuring that energy and water supplies can adequately meet urban
demand. With the scarcity of natural resources and growing electricity and water demand, it becomes increas-
ingly important for consumers to manage their resource usage more efficiently. This paper proposes a novel
perspective of demand-side management coordination strategy for a building’s water-energy nexus to enhance
the resilience and efficiency of the overall electricity-water-heating system. The model is formulated to optimally
coordinates the onsite greywater recycling system, heating, ventilation, air conditioning (HVAC) loads, and
distributed energy generation systems with a bidirectional grid connection in a residential building. All sub-
systems are controlled by a model predictive controller (MPC) receiving real-time time of use (ToU) pricing from
electricity and water utilities. The presented mixed integer linear programming model is verified to meet the
customers’ demands while reducing the operational costs. Results are compared with benchmark systems lacking
the water recycling or energy storage system showing 8.3 % operational cost reduction while reducing potable
water consumption by 21.5 %. The effect of increased MPC control horizon is also studied showing reduction in
cost with increased horizon. Detailed analysis of the proposed framework computational burden and effect of
prediction errors is performed to prove the MPC adaptability and robustness. Testing under increased room size
and different user preferences further validate the efficacy of the proposed scheme in reducing the operational
costs.
1. Introduction
1.1. Motivation
Energy demand has been increasing in a pattern that can be
described as exponential [1]. This puts a huge burden on electricity
suppliers. The burden becomes even greater with the added re-
quirements to reduce carbon-based generation to mitigate climate
change [2]. Renewable Energy Sources (RES) have been introduced into
the grid as a solution for the aforementioned issues [3]. Such an intro-
duction has not been a straightforward process due to RES intermittent
nature and lack of dispatchability [4,5]. Therefore, different topics
related to RES integration to the conventional grid and enhancing their
efficiencies have gained much interest in the energy literature [6,7]. The
end goal is to achieve building resilience by increasing building inde-
pendency from large utilities [8,9]. Resilience of Building also depends
on two more systems, water supply and electric loads. Minimizing and
optimizing water usage is a necessary for future efficient and more
decentralized agents [10]. Electric loads can have great impact also on

1.2. Micro water-energy nexus of building
To facilitate the adoption of small-scale RES, the idea of Microgrid
(MG) has been introduced. In a MG, generation units of small capacity
are installed at distribution or consumption level. The generation units
are then called distributed energy resources (DERs), which have small
capacity and are usually renewable sources [4,13,14]. The transition
from centralized grid structure to MG structure can be achieved on
several scales of MG. One scale is the building itself. Each building can
act as a MG having its own sources, loads, power conditioners and
controllers, which can be referred to as building microgrid (BMG)
[15,16]. BMG can be grid connected or standalone without grid
connection. The latter model of operation is known as islanded mode
[1,17]. It is usually the case when designing a standalone MG that en-
ergy storage systems (ESS) is used to make up for the uncertainty of
DERs. Battery energy storage (BES) is the most common type [18].
The operation of electric-water networks has run independently for
decades. Recent research tends to explore the coupling between electric
and water networks in a form called nexus and correlate both systems to
achieve joint targets, such as efficiency and carbon reduction [19–23].
Integration of energy-water hubs have expanded beyond basic models
and economic assessments, encompassing a wide range of technological,
environmental, and policy considerations. However, challenges in un-
derstanding the multifaceted nature of the water-energy nexus and in
resource conservation still exist. To address these issues, investments in
large-scale water infrastructures have been made to enhance urban
water availability. On the other hand, these efforts have led to increased
per capita water usage and high dissipation costs, with limited recycling
or reuse of water [24].
However, most of the research targeted the generation/transmission
large levels [25–27] or the microgrid context [19,20] along with the
operational aspect rather than a residential building level. Previous
research [28] has basically focused on a spatial optimized distribution of
household rainwater harvesting and grey water recycling system on the
daily basis instead of hourly basis. Another research considered a case
study from a city in Unites Arab Emirates and various types of buildings
grey water recycling economic assessment [29]. The models in [28] are
very basic and do not incorporate renewable energy integration for
recycling of greywater and scheduling of storage system under such

traditional utility systems. Similarly, water-energy CO2 emissions nexus

alongside CO2 emissions. The study found that agriculture dominated
national water consumption but had low efficiency, while industries and
the service sector were identified as water-, energy-, and carbon-
intensive. The research emphasized the significant impact of upstream
sectors on resource consumption and emissions, highlighting the
importance of targeted strategies for sustainable development. The
water-energy nexus is critical for addressing resource scarcity and
environmental challenges, with research driving integrated manage-
ment strategies that optimize both water and energy use. Understanding
these interconnected systems enables more informed decision-making
by policymakers and industries, fostering sustainable development and
resilience [35]. Ultimately, water-energy nexus studies pave the way for
innovative technologies and practices that balance human needs with
environmental conservation.
1.3. Water demand of building and potential of greywater recycling
Another demand of a building is water. The current person average
water usage is 500 l per day for developed countries [36]. The global
fresh water demand is anticipated to surpass the supply by 40 % by 2030
[37]. Water demand for various activities including cooling and venti-
lation systems [38], irrigation, etc. mandates significant amount of en-
ergy. In most countries around the world, water supply system is
centralized and thus, provided from the main utility [39,40]. Under
natural hazards, maintenance, etc., the water network may be out of
service. Water problems in arid climate regions include low amounts of
groundwater with increased salinity and narrow utilization of used
water. This results in surging the cost of water production [41]. Water
security is considered as an emerging area. Therefore, many countries
invested in desalination plants, for more than 40 % of the fresh water
demand [42,43]. Of the strategies recognized for mitigating water
scarcity, enhancing water utilization efficiency is the guaranteed
approach and most recommended. This can be achieved through the
treatment of used water for use in other applications, such as flushing,
irrigation or even in temperature control systems [44,45]. Greywater is
the wastewater obtained after water is used except for toilet use [44].
The reuse of grey water on household level can offer huge environ-
mental advantages. It can also be more economical in the long term.
Therefore, onsite greywater recycling is recommended and can form a
major cornerstone in a resilient and sustainable city. A work that took
the city of Boston as a case study and modeled the use of both rain water
harvesting and grey water recycling was done utilizing decentralized
facilities for water supply [28]. Though, challenges exist in imple-
menting the greywater recycling for housing complex as identified in
[44]. Greywater treatment can be done using a series of processes, such
as trapping, aeration, blending and employing filters [46]. In [45],
several chemical processes for greywater treatment are reported, some
include photo catalytic oxidation, electrocoagulation and magnetic ion
exchange. It is also reported that electrocoagulation techniques are
favored over conventional ones. The importance of segregating grey-
water at the source for decentralized treatment is highlighted in [47].
This can lead to increased resilience and adaptability of local water
systems and reduced transport costs.
Greywater treatment technologies can be classified to biological,
pyhsico-chemical and nature-based [47]. Biological methods, such as
membrane bioreactors and sequencing batch reactors, are effective at
removing organic pollutants and nutrients. They can handle varying
pollutant loads and generally have lower operating costs compared to
physicochemical methods. However, they require careful control of
environmental conditions and may be sensitive to toxic substances in
greywater

assessing the long-term viability of greywater systems. Models that
quantify uncertainties in cost estimates and the importance of socio-
economic acceptance are needed to ensure successful implementation.
1.4. HVAC load of a building
Another important aspect in buildings is the heating, ventilation, and
air conditioning (HVAC). HVAC load can reach around 70 % of elec-
tricity consumption and contributes to around half of buildings water
consumption [52,53]. HVAC can be used as a demand response option in
ways that provide some sort of flexibility to control building’s temper-
ature within some constrained limits based on inhabitants’ need
[54,55]. Additional objectives include making use of the flexibility to
increase operating efficiency and decrease costs. Technical objectives
were also investigated, such as the use of HVAC for the grid frequency
regulation from sudden disturbances [56]. Several HVAC models have
been introduced previously for use in demand response applications
[57]. Effective HVAC control strategy and accurate modeling are
essential to account for the thermal dynamics of buildings, optimizing
energy consumption while ensuring indoor comfort is maintained.
Modeling buildings HVAC systems for is commonly done using grey

Hysteresis and proportional-integral (PI) controls are the most
commonly used techniques for HVAC within buildings. This is attributed
to the inherent simplicity and resilience [60]. MPC has been deployed
for HVAC control for its flexibility in optimizing decisions while inter-
acting with the changing conditions and constraints. This is especially
effective for managing complex heat transfers and addressing the
delayed thermal responses caused by the building’s thermal mass and
indoor heat sources [61]. The major challenges for MPC of HVAC are
achieving real-time requirements and employing simplified HVAC
models [62]. A typical building MPC has around 15 min window for
optimal decision-making. This is rather challenging for buildings ther-
mal controls, where environmental conditions, residents behavior and
other subsystems interaction is continuously varying and uncertain
[63]. Recent studies have demonstrated significant improvements in
HVAC control using advanced optimization strategies. 31 % reduction in
pollutant concentration and 17.7 % energy savings using a dynamic
optimization strategy for AHU control was achieved in [64]. In [65], the
authors developed an occupancy-based, energy-efficient MPC for multi-
room buildings, demonstrating a potential energy savings of 29–80 %.
Multi-objective optimization of thermal comfort, air quality, and energy
use for HVAC systems was tackled in [66], finding an effective trade-off
with only 2 % higher energy use. In [62], a quantum annealing-based
optimization solution for HVAC MPC was proposed. The approach
significantly reduced computation time from hours to seconds. Their
method demonstrated an 80 % reduction in total electricity consump-
tion and a 21 % reduction in electricity bills when considering day-
ahead price time-of-use demand response signals. Artificial intelli-
gence methods were also applied for HVAC controls. Various deep
learning approaches were investigated for optimizing HVAC control
across different systems and configurations [67]. Deep reinforcement
learning demonstrated 2–14 % reduction in energy consumption and
improved indoor temperature comfort compared to conventional
methods, with a 66 % faster convergence rate [68]. Existing studies
usually study HVAC as an isolated system trying to optimize its energy
use and cost. However, this is not the case for real buildings, where other
electric loads and water demand are also required to be optimized. With
the inclusion of variable external conditions, such as weather and time
of use (TOU) pricing, optimizing a single system cost does not guarantee
a minimum cost for the whole building operations.
1.5. Distributed generation for building
Buildings can consume 20–40 % of the energy in developed regions.
It is why they propose a good fit for acting as independent agents, with
their own PV and storage, within modern energy networks [69].
Buildings are then required to be flexible and interactive with utilities
for optimizing energy usage and reduce the burden on carbon-
dominated utilities. Among the proposed solutions are the involve-
ment of flexible of HVAC demand, accurate dispatch of energy storage
and bidirectional energy trading with utilities [70,71]. It is why there
has been great interest in optimizing the energy management operations
of buildings. The Optimal energy management is performed in [72–74]
to increase building’s efficiency and reduce operating costs, with addi-
tional objectives, such as decreasing battery degradation and supply
continuity while maintaining technical system constraints. Other works
have focused on investigating architectures and optimizing the design
for buildings resources to reduce the expected operational costs and
increase autonomy [70,75].
1.6. Model predictive control of a building management system
An efficient controller for both water and energy processes is a must
to achieve the optimal performance from resources and cost point of
views [76,77]. Controllers at different BMG can be interconnected to
provide increased dependability and operability for buildings’ water-
energy systems within a smart grid structure [78–81]. Several works
used MPC as a controller for energy systems or water systems as seen in
[54,82,83]. MPC uses a dynamic system’s model to find the necessary
inputs to achieve a certain performance. It is then required to have an
accurate model of the system to achieve an optimal response [84,85].
MPC will be deployed in this paper to control the energy-water system in
the BMG. Up to our current knowledge, no previous research explored
the possibility of exploiting building resilience that benefits of all of the
followings: renewable energy integration, energy management, demand
response and intelligent control strategies in a building microgrid that
considers onsite grey water recycling system and works upon interactive
time-dependent pricing of utilities, and that is the goal of this paper.
1.7. Contribution
In this paper, a study is presented for a grid-connected BMG con-
sisting of Photovoltaics (PV) plant with BES. Electrical loads modeling is
classified into HVAC controllable loads and other noncontrollable loads.
Building water related operations is controlled by the same central BMS.
A greywater recycling structure is designed for the building using pumps
and tanks. This allows water operations to be responsive and have wide
margin of controllability by the BMS. The target is to find the minimum
operational cost through the optimal coordination of the operation of
water and energy resources in the building. This is constrained by
achieving the variable HVAC temperature requirements and meeting
other electrical and water demands.
All resources and services, including HVAC loads demand response
and electricity and water systems considering the use of recycled grey-
water services, are controlled via a single BMS controller that is
instructed to minimize building’s operating cost, which makes BMS
prefer certain actions at different times. Given that said, the contribution
of this paper filling the literature gaps can be summarized as follows:
• This paper proposes a structure for resilient building through energy,
water, and cooling systems. Renewable energy PV source, battery
energy storage (BES) system, HVAC and other non-controllable loads
and onsite grey water recycling are the major building subsystems.
• The paper implements all subsystems within a building and develops
a predictive central controller for electric, water and thermal re-
quirements in the building. Demand response capabilities in BES,
HVAC, and water pumping, recycling and storage with utility time of
use pricing of both energy and water are used to coordinate the
operation of the building.
• The predictive controller is based on a MILP formulation. The MPC-
based BMS continuously takes decisions during the 4-day simulation
period, selected to test the controller under variant operating con-
ditions, with the goal to make use of building resources based on
updates in the data provided to it, limitations of resources and
customer needs. Effect of longer control horizon for the BMS is
studied to show the possible improvements, which depend on
available the BMS computational power and efficiency in long
forecasting.
• The results demonstrate an 8.3 % operational cost reduction and a
21.5 % reduction in potable water consumption compared to
benchmark systems lacking the water recycling or energy storage
system. Testing under variable building size and user preferences
show similar improvements.
• Detailed exploration of the MPC framework reveal the framework is
robust under a wide range of input prediction errors. It is found that
under error predictions, increasing the control horizon does not

2.1. Building electrical system
The proposed building energy system is made of a rooftop PV power
plant and battery energy storage system to achieve dispatchability of PV
power plant. It also includes grid connection at a point of common
coupling (PCC), and the electrical load. HVAC load is the main source of
electrical demand. Another constant-power, non-controllable load PNC,
is used to consider other building’s loads. These are all controlled by an
efficient MPC for energy management purpose, as developed in this
paper. The proposed building electrical diagram is presented in Fig. 2.

3. Control methodology
A linear MPC-based centralized controller is implemented to control
1) room temperatures through HVAC, 2) utility interchange of powers
and batteries operation, and 3) water system operation comprising its
pumps and valves. Such control is possible through appropriate
communication and coordination with the power grid, the availability of
real-time energy measurements of distributed generation systems
including BES and PV plant. Moreover, to meet water demand, the
controller should control the operation of the two pumps to assure
availability of water in tanks. This is done by communicating with the
water utility to obtain the variable water price to minimize the cost.
For MPC to offer reliable outcomes, it is necessary to have precise
modeling of the system. [59,93]. The control variables in the system are
Pbat,ch, Pbat,disch, Pgrid,sell,Pgrid,sell,PHVAC,s1,s2,s3 and s4. Four binary control
variables, u1,u2,u3 and u4, are added to prevent simultaneous charging
and discharging of batteries and simultaneous electricity selling and
buying from and to the grid. By controlling the values of these control
variables, the control can take the optimal control action to reduce the
energy consumption cost, which can be achieved mainly through pre-
cooling of the building zones whenever abundance of PV power is
available, when the battery constraints would not be violated or when
energy is used from the grid at times of low-cost rates of electricity
consumption. The optimal control problem is converted to an optimi-
zation problem using a single shooting technique [94]. MPC parameters
are Δt=15 min, nc = 10 = (2.5 hours), and np = 384 = (4 days).
The sampling time is reduced for the sake of capturing the dynamics
of the HVAC operation. The control horizon is set to 10 steps only to
allow for practicality as buildings are not long-term forecasted and
relatively quick simulations and thus, better diagnostics despite the fact
that it reduces the efficiency of MPC.

For every time step, the problem is solved for an upcoming control
horizon. The objective function is comprised of three parts. The first part
is the net energy cost from/to the grid, where Cbuy is always higher than
Csell. The second part of the objective function tries to minimize the
overall HVAC load consumption over the whole prediction horizon,
usually done by precooling. The third part is the same as the second part
but rather for water demand. It is also done by filling the water tanks
earlier, if possible, at times of low utilities cost. The constants vector w is
used to tune the controller to achieve the required performances. Its
elements’ values are the same here to obtain an objective function that
represents the overall system cost.
Constraints (14–18) limits HVAC power, battery charging power,
battery discharging power, power bought from the grid and power sold
to the grid to predefined limits, respectively. Constraint (19) assures the
temperature comfort of the inhabitants within predefined limits.
Constraint (20) tries to avoid problems that happen to BES from over-
charging or deep discharging. Constraint (21) is the power balance
equation from the grid to satisfy the load demand. Constraint (22) is to
constraint the water flow states to be binary. Constraint (23) is for as-
suring certain maximum and minimum levels in the water tanks to
prevent water overflow from tanks and preserve some amount for
emergencies such as potable water pump failure. Eqs. (2), (4), and
(10−12) are used for updating the system states and variables. Con-
straints (24–30) keep the uv variables binary and prevent the simulta-
neous operation mentioned before. These equations are solved for the
whole prediction horizon at each time step k.
Each control variable will be obtained as an array of length equals
the prediction horizon number of steps. After each iteration of MPC, only
the first step is applied to the system and then the system states are
updated to be ready for the next iteration. The rest of the resulting
prediction horizon is used as an initialization for the upcoming step,
with the last prediction repeated to make up for the first prediction
omitted.
The model is tested on a four-days test case. This optimization MILP
problem. CasADi library is used in MATLAB for solving the problem
using BONMIN solver [94,95].

4. Results and discussion
4.1. System inputs
For the purpose of simulation, the starting three days are weekdays
(WDs) and the fourth day is a weekend (WE). Weather data is obtained
for a site in Masdar City, Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates. A TOU is
developed based on utility costs in United Arab Emirates and electricity
and water demand profiles. Furthermore, a non-controlled electrical
load power is devised to represent other building’s loads [105–107]. The
non-controllable load PNC is diversified for every hour of the day with a
maximum value of around 30kW. Figs. 6 and 7 show the TOU pricing for
electricity and PNC load for the simulated period, respectively. Besides
that, both potable and grey water demand curves are demonstrated in
Fig. 8. In terms of PV power generation, driven by Eq. (1), its variability
is represented by Fig. 9.
4.2. Simulation results
The problem is solved at each iteration, meaning that a total of 375
problems are solved. The remaining prediction horizon is filled with
results from the last iteration, so as to obtain a closed loop performance
for the period of four days. Each iteration has 13 control variables per
each control horizon totaling 130 control variables in each step. Each
iteration has 130 control variable constraints and 120 state constraints,
in addition to the 80 integer constraints. For all cases studies, any system
state (e.g., battery SoC and tanks water levels) is initialized at the
minimum value to provide fair comparisons. All introduced cases are
solved using Branch and Bound technique in BONMIN solver limiting
the maximum iterations to 1*106 and the limit of nodes to 1*106.
Results of the simulation are demonstrated with curves representing
the controllers’ decisions effect on each subsystem. First, Fig. 10 shows
the charging and discharging operation of the BES through the battery
SoC. Both processes respect the limits assigned by to the controller and
simultaneous charging and discharging is avoided by including u1and u2
in the problem formulation. It can be seen that, by relating Fig. 9 and
Fig. 10, the controller choses to charge the battery from excess PV power
and discharge it when PV power is not sufficient or available until its SoC
reaches the minimum assigned limit at nighttime.
In Fig. 11, the building’s electric energy interchanges with the power
grid given the ToU pricing for purchasing and selling energy from and to
the grid. It is found that, in spite of the low control horizon, the
controller always purchases power from the grid at times of low pur-
chase price cost and sells at times of higher selling cost when possible.
The controller respects the limits on sold and purchased power from the
grid, especially for the case of selling, when at times it seemed that
exceeding the limit could have been more profitable. Simultaneous
assignment of values to both selling power and buying power is avoided.
In Fig. 12, HVAC operation is validated. The controller always
modulates the power to the cooling units to adapt to the surrounding
temperatures variations. The operation is tested on a variable setpoint
temperature. The controller successfully tracks the changing setpoint
exactly, without violating the minimum temperature limit. Precooling is
done to make use of the available excess energy and decrease the energy
to be bought later. The precooling periods and their variations were
determined by the optimization algorithm based on several factors and
optimized to balance energy cost savings with thermal comfort. The
algorithm aimed to utilize excess energy when available while main-
taining temperatures within acceptable limits. The longer precooling
period on the first day likely resulted from different initial conditions or
predicted energy availability compared to subsequent days. The smaller
temperature decrease on the second day was likely due to a combination
of factors such as different outdoor temperatures, internal heat gains, or

4.3. Comparison with benchmark systems
To try to capture the improvement that the proposed building
configuration provides in comparison with the proposed cases, addi-
tional three scenarios representing different systems were implemented.
These additional systems are optimized using the earlier optimization
technique and a centralized controller scheme. These proposed systems
are compared to the case when the building is dependent only on the
water and energy utilities, “Utilities’ dependent System”. All systems are
subject to the same inputs and loads. These three systems are:
1) Benchmark base system: A system without battery and without any
greywater recycling structure.
2) Proposed system with battery: A system where a battery storage is
added to the benchmark base system case, without any greywater
recycling.
3) Proposed system with greywater recycling: A system where benchmark
base case is improved with building’s greywater recycling infra-
structure, without any energy storage.
The previously devised and detailed system is considered as the
fourth system that is the complete proposed system. Comparison of resulting
costs from all the aforementioned systems are found in Table 4 below.
Results clearly demonstrate the cost reduction occurring with the
addition of both battery energy storage and greywater recycling in cases
2, 3 and 4. In addition to its considerable effect on cost reduction of the
4-day utilities cost, greywater recycling results in a reduction in water
usage form utility by 6.15m3, which accounts for 21.8 % reduction in
utility water consumption. This is also the case for both systems 3 and 4,
which reflects the possible effect of having decentralized water recycling
units within buildings. It is worth mentioning that for systems 1 and 3,
an optimization problem would have been infeasible had the same limits
of electricity buying and selling used for systems 2 and 4 been used. That
is why limits of purchasing and selling powers from and to grid had to be
increased for systems 1 and 3 to 80 kW and 60 kW, which in an actual
implementation would increase the ratings of the used electrical com-
ponents. The proposed complete system reduces net utilities’ cost to US
$182.71, which represents around 20 % of that required to operate the
building if it is totally dependent on the utilities. This is an addition to
the improved security achieved by energy reserved in battery and water
in tanks, that can act as a backup for cases when emergencies occur.
Utilizing the battery increases the BMG reliability as its main purpose
is to operate in standalone mode. The effect on reducing the net cost can
be observed when transitioning from system 1 to system 2 and from
system 3 to system 4. Specifically, the net cost of utilities decreases from
$199.33 to $195.90, representing a reduction of approximately 1.72 %,
when moving from system 1 to system 2. Similarly, when transitioning
from system 3 to system 4, the net cost of utilities decreases from
$185.59 to $182.71, a reduction of about 1.55 %.
These cost reductions mainly result from the bidirectional electricity
flow capability and the time-dependent cost of selling and buying energy
from the grid. With these flexibilities, buildings can trade their stored
energy with the utility at optimal times when the selling price is higher.
This strategy leverages the nature of residential loads and the multiple
demand-response capabilities under the controller’s influence, such as
HVAC, greywater recycling, and the battery itself.
4.4. Effect of increased control horizon
Another attempt is done using a control horizon nc of 20 steps (5 h).
Increased horizon should give more information to the controller to take
better decision. The overall cost is reduced through a reduction in both
electricity and water utilities cost. The reduction in energy cost has been
experienced through reducing the amount of energy bought from the
grid and increasing the utilization of battery as shown in Fig. 14, which
compares the SoC of energy in both 10 and 20 horizon cases. Conse-
quently, the amount of energy sold to the grid is reduced and thus,
reducing the cost of energy selling. Moreover, the amount of energy used
for cooling is reduced, and the precooling is less than the original case.
The other part of net cost reduction comes from the reduction in the
cost of potable water from utility. For both cases, potable water pump P1
is operated for 18 times. In the case with 10 control horizon, only 7 times
the controller fills the water at times of low utility potable water cost in
comparison with 12 times for the case with 20 control horizon.
A comparison between the overall characteristics and cost results of
both developed controllers is performed in Table 5. As the selected
control horizon increases, the simulation becomes exhaustive. For one
step of the 375 steps of simulation, 260 control constraints and 240 state
constraints are solved.

rizons or even compromise the system’s ability to maintain constraints
or satisfy water and energy demands. Fig. 17 shows resulting net cost
over the 4 simulation days when the prediction errors in PV output, non-
controlled load and water demand are introduced. Increasing nc from 5
to 10 decreases the net cost, as expected. Further increasing nc to 15
leads to a slight increase in net cost, suggesting that the negative impact
of forecast errors begins to outweigh the benefits of a longer horizon.
Interestingly, when N is extended to 20, the net cost decreases again,
indicating a complex interplay between horizon length and prediction
accuracy. These findings confirm that in the presence of input prediction
errors, extending the control horizon does not guarantee improved
performance. The relationship between net cost and control horizon
length is irregular and nonlinear, depending on both the specific prob-
lem characteristics and the magnitude of prediction errors.
It is worth noting that with nc ≥25, the mean time for MPC opti-
mization step greatly increase, exceeding the 15 min window available
to take decisions in some instances. This computational constraint can
potentially compromise the integrity of the MPC’s optimal solution, as
the solver may be forced to terminate before converging on the global
optimum. A more powerful hardware or dedicated computational re-
sources could potentially alleviate these constraints. However, even
with more powerful resources, the trade-off between control horizon,
prediction accuracy, and computational complexity would still exist. As
nc increases, the problem’s dimensionality grows, leading to an expo-
nential increase in the number of decision variables and constraints. This
inherent complexity suggests that there will always be a practical upper
limit to the control horizon, beyond which the marginal benefits of
extension are outweighed by the computational costs and increased
sensitivity to prediction errors.
4.6. MPC computational complexity
The proposed method solves a problem with a huge decision space,
combining water, energy and HVAC systems. Hence, the computational
burden associated should be investigated. Hence, scenarios with nc of
5,10,15 and 20 were performed to show the time complexity and burden
of the problem. The codes are implemented using MATLAB 2021b on a
laptop with Intel Core i7 CPU, and 16 GB RAM (OS: Windows 10). Mean
time for each optimization step for different nc is reported Fig. 18. As
expected, the computation time generally increases with the length of
the control horizon due to the added complexity of predicting further
into the future. Overall, the results demonstrate that while the compu-
tation time increases with the length of the control horizon, the pro-
posed
method
remains
computationally
feasible
for
real-time
application with the mean step computation time less than the 15 min
sampling time of the used MPC.
