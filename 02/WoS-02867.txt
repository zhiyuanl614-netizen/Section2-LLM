A B S T R A C T
The proliferation of interconnected Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and their connectivity with internet is 
expanding the attack surface, making them vulnerable to cyber-threats such as ransomware, malware, and 
targeted attacks. A cyber-attack launched on a critical infrastructure (CI), such as a water treatment plant, 
chemical plants or power grid could lead to anomalous behavior. Due to dynamic nature and variety of 
attributes in cyber data, the detection and prevention of these anomalous behavior is still an open challenge. 
Cyber physical systems (CPS) includes both the information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT) 
data. The detection of anomalous behavior is possible using both the IT and the OT data. The study conducted 
here has used the OT data. A supervised machine learning technique based on decision trees was used to mine 
the invariants from the OT data. The proposed approach was also compared with the Association Rule Mining 
(ARM) for generating invariants. The entire study was conducted in the context of scaled down version of 
water distribution plant (WaDi). The validation of generated invariants was performed using the operational 
plant and also using the physics of the plant.
1. Introduction
Cyber Physical Systems (CPS) are the combination of both the 
computational and physical components. The example of such system 
includes the smart power plants, water treatment and distribution 
facilities, transportation, mechanical technologies, and autonomous 
cars. These systems are usually massive and geographically dispersed, 
therefore, they are connected with network for remote monitoring 
and control. This connectivity makes them vulnerable to cyber attack. 
The detection of these cyber attacks is possible using different ap-
proaches like signature-based, specification-based, and behavior-based 
approaches (Umer et al., 2022). The signature based approaches uses 
a predefined dictionary of attack patterns, while specification based 
approaches create a mathematical model which can be later used to de-
tect the anomalies. The behavior based approaches rely on operational 
data of CPS to detect these anomalies. The proposed study has used the 
operational data of a water distribution plant to mine the invariants of 
the system. An invariant is the normal behavior of the plant whenever 
it is in the given state (Umer et al., 2020). There are studies in the 
literature which have used the invariants for the same purpose (Adepu 
and Mathur, 2016; Feng et al., 2019; Mujeeb Ahmed et al., 2021; 
Hudani et al., 2022; Umer et al., 2017). However, the approach used by

A rule-based machine learning technique was proposed in Umer

The standard BATADAL dataset was utilized to train a variety

contains 14 sophisticated attacks with attack detection system defences. 
To detect anomalies, the output of the networks was applied to a 
cross-validation dataset and an error threshold was computed using the 
multivariate MD rule. A voting-based ensemble strategy that combines 
the outputs of the several algorithms was also employed to improve

only exception was Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), which under 
performed when applied to test data.
Many machine learning techniques have proven to be successful at 
conducting anomaly detection but only a few considered the sequential 
nature of data. A sequential approach was proposed in Oliveira et al. 
(2021). The performance of a Random Forest (RF), a Multi-Layer Per-

on the CIDDS-001 dataset. LSTM showed better performance than RF

algorithms were used to monitor the data collected by sensors from 
operational technologies.
The study in Kim et al. (2022) describes a host-based approach for

control system (DCS) network using DCS vendor-proprietary protocols. 
Normal and abnormal traffic were gathered in a nearly equivalent set-
ting to the real working environment. Depending on the time window, 
it was determined whether or not the traffic was usual. The traffic 
attack types were determined by studying the TCP/IP layer-4, i.e., the 
network’s basic hierarchical structure. Proposed work shows that XG-
Boost is appropriate for multi-class classification attack identification. 
However, ML-based random forest and decision trees are superior for 
anomalies detection and attack identification.

3. Water distribution testbed
The Water Distribution (WaDi) is a testbed available at Singapore 
University of Technology and Design (Ahmed et al., 2017b). It is a 
valuable resource for researchers working to create safe and secure 
cyber physical systems (CPS). WaDi is a natural extension of SWaT 
available at the same geographical location (Mathur and Tippenhauer, 
2016). WaDi has been in operation since 2016. WaDi accepts some 
of SWaT’s reverse osmosis permeate and raw water, resulting in a 
comprehensive and realistic water treatment, storage, and distribution 
network. WaDi is made up of multiple pipes that traverse a vast, 
and frequently less secure region. Physical attacks on a distribution 
network like WaDi are highly vulnerable to cyber-attacks. In addition to 
attacks and defenses on PLCs and networks, WaDi can provide valuable 
information on the consequences of physical attacks such as water 
leakage and malicious chemical injections.
WaDi’s cyber component includes a multilayer communications 
network, three National Instruments PLCs and two Schneider Electric 
PLCs, Human Machine Interfaces (HMIs), Supervisory Control and Data 
Acquisition (SCADA) workstations, and a Historian. Sensor data is 
provided to the SCADA system and is recorded by the Historian for 
further study.
WaDi has three stages, P1:water supply, P2:distribution network, 
and P3:return water system, as shown in Fig. 1. Each stage is connected 
with PLCs and PLCs are controlled and monitored by SCADA system of 
WaDi.

4. Invariant mining using decision trees
Decision Tree is a supervised learning machine learning approach. 
It can be used for classification and regression problems. It is a tree-
structured classifier in which internal nodes contain data set attributes, 
branches represent decision rules, and each leaf node represents the re-
sult. Decision trees have the ability to select the most relevant features. 
This ensures that the generated rules are based on significant charac-
teristics rather than noise. Because each path from the root to a leaf 
represents a distinct decision rule, they offer excellent interpretability, 
making it easy to identify patterns. Decision trees are also scalable and 
effective in handling large datasets, which is essential for rule mining.
The proposed approach was used to generate a number of decision 
trees for different sensors or actuators. For example, Fig. 3 represents 
a decision tree for 1-AIT-005-PV. We call this tree an invariant. The 
decision node makes the decision based on the value of any particular 
sensor or actuator. The leaf node represents the value of the particular 
class, which in this case is 1-AIT-005-PV. The proposed approach 
has used the Classification and Regression Tree (CART) algorithm to 
generate these decision trees. The CART used the 14 days of operational 
data from WaDi to generate the decision trees (Ahmed et al., 2017a). 
However, the proposed approach can also be extended to other ICS 
datasets. The dataset of WaDi contains 120 attributes and 1 048 571 
transactions with a sampling rate of one second. For this study, we fo-
cused on identifying attributes that exhibited dynamic behavior within 
the dataset. This led us to concentrate primarily on attributes from 
Stages 1 and 2, where we observed notable variations. After careful 
analysis, we determined that the attributes in Stage 3 did not demon-
strate significant behavioral changes; most remained in a constant state. 
Consequently, we were unable to identify suitable Stage 3 attributes for 
inclusion in our final attribute set. The selected attributes, derived from 
Stages 1 and 2, are listed in the ‘‘Class Attribute’’ column of Table 4. The 
dataset of WaDi contains the continuous valued-attributes. However, 
the proposed approach aims to create decision rules based on each 
attribute, therefore, the continuous valued attribute were discretized 
into different classes using the binning method. Binning is the process

ber of generated rules, that is, 1565, followed by 2-PIT-003-PV which 
has 1319 generated rules. It shows that 2-LT-002-PV and 2-PIT-003-PV

by the algorithm were 14 639. The number of rules depends on the 
intervals defined for each attribute. For example, In pump, only two 
values of status were considered, that is, 0 or 1, which represents OFF 
and ON, respectively. For this study, pump speed was not considered; 
we focused solely on the ON and OFF states of the pumps to simplify 
the model and manage the complexity of the generated invariants. 
Similarly, for motorized valve, there exist three values, i.e. 0, 1 and 2, 
where 0 represents the CLOSED state, 2 for the OPEN state, and 3 for 
the valve transition state. In our analysis, we only considered the OPEN 
and CLOSE states of the valve. The other attributes related to different 
sensors are continuous-valued attributes. Since it was not feasible to 
generate rules for these continuous-valued attributes, we discretized 
these attributes by creating intervals. This makes the rules more effi-
cient and accurate and limits the number of rules. In Table 5, some 
samples of generated rules are shown for different sensors or actuators. 
These generated rules (invariants) serves as monitors to detect process 
anomalies. For example, the invariant in entry 5 of Table 5 states that 
motorized valve (1-MV-001-STATUS) should be CLOSED given that 1-P-
001-STATUS = ON, 1-MV-004-STATUS = OPEN, 1-LT-001-PV = HIGH, 
1-AIT-005-PV = HIGH, 2-FIT-001-PV = HIGH, 1-P-005-STATUS = ON, 
2-MCV-401-CO = OPEN, and 2-PIT-003-PV = HIGH. If an adversary 
changes the value of any sensor or actuator (e.g., turns off pump 1-P-
001-STATUS), the invariant will immediately generate an alert because 
it will no longer be valid in the resulting scenario.
4.1. Comparison of invariants generated using decision trees vs. invariants

invariants. We used the same approach discussed in, Umer et al. (2020) 
to generate invariants for WaDi. The detailed discussion of invariant 
generation using ARM is not the scope of this paper; therefore, we have 
only presented the results obtained using ARM to draw a comparison 
with decision trees. The invariants generated using the ARM are shown 
in Table 6. ARM has the limitation that it works on binary data while 
its strength lies on identifying relationships between existing or non-

4.2. Rules validation
There are multiple ways to validate the invariants generated using 
the proposed decision tree approach. (i). Live validation on the opera-
tional testbed. (ii). Offline validation using a different dataset collected 
from the same testbed. (iii). Validation by domain expert using the 
physics of the plant. (iv). Validation using the digital twin of the plant.
We performed live validation on the operational testbed and also 
validated the invariants against the plant design, using domain expert 
input. A random subset of invariants was chosen and validated based 
on the physics of the plant. However, due to operational issues with 
the WaDi testbed (faulty sensor/actuator) at that time, we were unable 
to conduct it in a more meaningful way and relied more heavily on 
domain expert validation. Therefore, only one rule was validated on 
WaDi, while the rest were verified using the physical principles of 
the WaDi plant and the WaDi HMI/SCADA system. Table 7 presents 
some of the rules, which were validated either live on the opera-
tional plant or through the plant’s physics. For example, the first rule 
given in Table 7 was validated on live plant which states that if 
motorized valves (2-MV-601-STATUS), (2-MV-301-STATUS), (2-MCV-
201-CO), (1-MV-004-STATUS), and (2-MV-201-STATUS) are open and 
chemical analyzer (1-AIT-005-PV) level is high then the valve (2-MCV-
101-CO) will be open. The second rule, which was validated using the 
physics of WaDi states that if tank levels (1-LT-001-PV) and (2-LT-002-
PV) are high, and line water flow indicator (2-FIT-001-PV) is high, then 
the valve (1-MV-004-STATUS) will be closed. The third rule states that 
the pump at stage 1 will be OFF if motorized valve (2-MV-003-STATUS) 
is open and flow indicator (2-FIT-001-PV) is high. According to the 
plant’s physics, this was not possible and therefore not validated. For 
further verification, we also checked the operational data. We found 
that the pump was ON in this situation, even though the rule states that 
it should be OFF. The same approach can be adopted to understand the 
other rules in Table 7.

RQ2: Decision trees and ARM are two different techniques and their 
method of analyzing data is also different. They do not generate same 
set of invariant even with same dataset. Reason is that decision trees 
are mostly used for regression and classification tasks while ARM is 
employed to identify interesting relationships or associations between 
variables in large datasets. Decision tree invariants are frequently con-
nected to decision rules that continue across branches. These invariants 
are typically the thresholds or conditions that influence the tree’s 
decisions whereas, ARM discovers invariants as patterns or relation-
ships that persist throughout the dataset. They describe how features 
interact or co-occur, and they are often statistical associations instead 
of decision rules.
RQ3: Invariants produced with decision trees (DT) usually con-
centrate on identifying patterns based on important characteristics 
and offer unambiguous understandable rules, particularly for big and 
varied data sets. Conversely, invariants produced by Association Rule 
Mining (ARM) identify stronger associations and capture common co-
occurrences in the data. The effectiveness of the comparison depends on 
the purpose and the complexity of the dataset. In general, ARM works 
well with binary and categorical data, but it can produce a lot of rules, 
which can be hard to maintain. ARM offers broader pattern discovery, 
potentially revealing more nuanced invariants, while Decision Trees 
give superior interpretability and structure.
RQ4: In ARM, invariants are based on antecedents and a conse-
quent. While in decision trees, invariant derived are typically rules 
or conditions that based on the split of the tree. These invariants 
represent logical conditions that classify or predict outcomes based 
on feature values. These outcomes are similar as antecedents in ARM 
and predicted class in decision tree, e.g., class 0 or class 1 is similar 
as consequent in ARM approach. This means there is a similarity in 
both invariants and feasibility of integration is possible. Moreover, the 
invariants generated by both the approaches are different, therefore, 
it will be good to integrate the invariants generated through both the 
approaches. The combined set of invariants can serve as monitors to 
detect process anomalies in the plant.
6. Conclusion and future work
The work presents a decision tree-based supervised machine learn-
ing approach to generate invariants for a water distribution plant. 
In this context, invariants are conditions or rules that remain consis-
tently true under typical operating conditions; deviations from these 
conditions may indicate potential system malfunctions or anomalies. 
The proposed approach used operational data of the plant to generate 
invariants using decision trees. A comparison of the proposed approach 
with ARM is also provided. These invariants can serve as monitors to 
detect the irregularities in the water distribution process. A subset of 
generated invariants was validated live on operational testbed, and also 
against the design of the plant. However, during live validation, faulty 
sensor/actuator caused challenges for the investigation, increasing the 
need for domain expert validation. The robustness of invariants can be 
further tested by generating various cyberattacks on the ICS, ensuring 
no faulty sensor/actuator influence. Future work will extend this con-
cept to other ICS by applying other machine learning and deep learning 
algorithms.
