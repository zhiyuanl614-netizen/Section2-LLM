Modeling and optimization of photovoltaic serpentine type thermal 
solar collector with thermal energy storage system for hot water 
and electricity generation for single residential building
Srimanickam Baskaran1 · Christopher Sathiya Satchi2 · Saranya Amirtharajan1 · Metilda Manuel Swami Durai3
Received: 29 September 2021 / Accepted: 24 March 2022 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2022
Abstract
Increasing surface temperature has a significant effect on the electrical performance of photovoltaic (PV) panels. A 
closed-loop forced circulation serpentine tube design of cooling water system was used in this study for effectively 
management of the surface temperature of PV panels. A real-time experiment was first carried out with a PV panel 
with a cooling system at heat transfer fluid (HTF) flow rates of 60 kg ­h−1, 120 kg ­h−1, and 180 kg ­h−1. Based on the 
experimentation, a correlation for a nominal operating cell temperature (NOCT) and thermal efficiency for collector 
was developed for experimental validation of useful energy gained, cell temperature, and electric power generation. 
The developed correlations were validated with the use of electric power electrical power and useful energy gained in 
photovoltaic serpentine thermal solar collector (PV/STSC) and fitting into the experimental results with a deviation of 
1% and 2.5% respectively. Further, with the help of developed correlations, a system was developed in the TRNSYS tool 
through which an optimization study was performed based on electric and hot water demand. The findings indicated an 
optimal system with an 8-m2 PV/STSC area, a HTF flow rate of 60 kg ­h−1, and thermal energy storage (TES) system 
having a volume and height of 280 l and 0.8 m could meet 91% and 33% of the hot water demand for Ac loads and 78% 
or DC loads, respectively.
Keywords  Nominal operating cell temperature · TRNSYS · Solar photovoltaic · Forced circulation · Solar fraction

Introduction
Energy consumption patterns, the greenhouse effect, the 
depletion of fossil fuel supplies, and fossil fuel price fluc-
tuation all contribute to an increase in the use of renew-
able resources in energy generation units (Ahmadi et al. 
2021; Pranesh et al. 2019; Said et al. 2021a). Solar power, 
on the other hand, is one of the most widely available 
clean energy sources (Christopher et al. 2021b, a; Thakur 
et al. 2020). Solar energy is typically used to generate 
heat energy and electric energy using thermal collectors 
and PV systems. While the use of solar energy has grown, 
some limitations, such as high installation costs (Fakouri-
yan et al. 2019; Srimanickam et al. 2015), low energy con-
version efficiency (Srimanickam et al. 2017; Srimanickam 
and Saranya 2021), and the need for storage systems due 
to supply and demand curves that differ have a negative 
impact on its use in comparison to other renewable energy 
sources (Wu et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2020; Xu and Zhao 
2019, 2016, 2015). Many research articles on photovol-
taic/thermal (PV/T) technologies have been published in 
an attempt to resolve the above said barriers (Giwa et al. 
2020; Lamnatou et al. 2021; Reji Kumar et al. 2020).
A PV/T technology can meet both electricity and heat 
energy demands simultaneously and reduce the amount 
of space needed for installation compared to traditional 
solar energy systems (Khoshgoftar Manesh et al. 2022; 
Navakrishnan et al. 2021; Saitoh et al. 2003). Lowering 
the PV panels surface temperature can help attachment 
of improved conversion efficiency. Different heat trans-
fer fluid (HTF) flow techniques achieved a reduction in 
the surface temperature of the solar panel. For instance, 
Kluth (2008) explored the advantages of water as a cool-
ing agent. Two small solar panel models were made for the 
purpose. One model was left uncooled, while the others 
were cooled by a fan blowing water on it. A solar panel 
with water cooling was seen producing more power pro-
duction compared to another one. Cooling the solar panel 
surface by spraying water with a fan was inefficient as 
the water would not be spread over the entire panel sur-
face, leaving certain areas of the PV panels uncooled. This 
approach often results in a significant amount of water 
loss. Odeh and Behnia (2009) used a surface cooling sys-
tem for a reduction in the heat at the surface of the panel. 
A water system was installed on the PV panel surface for 
delivering of water through sprinkling. This helped a 15% 
increase in electricity production during the peak of solar 
radiation. For the months of June and December, Bahaida-
rah (2016) explored the cooling of a PV panel using jet 
impingement both experimentally and computationally. 
Compared to uncooled PV panels, the authors found 
increase in electric energy yield by 51.6% in June and 
49.6% in December. Eisapour et al. (2020) employed wavy 
tubes to investigate a PV/T collector’s in terms of energy 
and exergy efficiency. The wavy tube improved energy 
and exergy efficiency by 6.06% and 4.25%, respectively, 
compared to the straight tube. Farahat (2004) worked on 
the electrical efficiency of a PV/T system that used both 
direct water and heat pipe cooling. In comparison with a 
direct water cooling system, it demonstrated the ability 
of the heat pipe cooling technology to achieve improved 
electrical efficiency. Rahimi et al. (2015) examined single 
and multi-header microconfigurations for PV cell cooling. 
PV panels with multiple header configurations eliminated 
larger heat and generated more electricity, than PV panels 
with a single header design.
The performance of a PV/T collector can be improved 
through use of an appropriate circulating fluid (Huaxu et al. 
2020; Sheikholeslami et al. 2021). For instance, Prakash 
(1994) investigated the performance of PV/T technology 
from two different circulating HTF viz. air and water. The 
findings showed variation when water was used as a cool-
ing fluid, the thermal efficiency varied between 50 and 67%. 
With replacement of water by cooling air, the enhancement 
ranged from 17 to 51%. Hissouf et al. (2020) investigated the 
effects of dispersed copper in pure water on PV/T collector. 
The electrical efficiency of copper nanomaterial dispersed 
in water improved by 1.9% having a volume concentration 
of 2 wt% compared to pure water results seen with. The 
efficiency of a PV/T module using brine as the circulating 
coolant HTF was evaluated by Saitoh et al. (2003). When 
the brine temperature was kept constant, the PV/T conver-
sion efficiency increased by 10 to 13%, while the brine tem-
perature at the outflow increased from 20 to 40 °C and the 
photo-thermal efficiency seems to be between 40 and 50%. 
Apart from earlier research, various numerical and experi-
mental investigations were performed to reduce the surface 
temperature of PV using nanofluids as a coolant to improve 
energy efficiency and output power (Al-Shamani et al. 2016; 
Al-Waeli et al. 2017; Bianco et al. 2018; Hjerrild et al. 2016; 
Sardarabadi and Passandideh-Fard 2016; Yazdanifard et al. 
2017).
In PV/T, water has shown to be more effective than air 
as a coolant. In PV technology, addition of nano-/micro-
additions to the coolant improves conversion efficiency 
(Said et al. 2022, 2021b, 2018). However, dispersing the 
particle in a large amount of coolant HTF (water) is not 
practicable and cost-effective. According to literature, 
the solar simulator was used as a source of energy in a 
majority of the studies. The effect of a PV panel varies 
depending on solar radiation, surface temperature, and 
other variables like shadowing, dust, and panel height 
from the ground (Dwivedi et al. 2020). The influence 
of above said parameters could not be predicted in the 
simulator-based experimental setup. Considering the

TRNSYS system description
Figure 4 depicts the TRNSYS flow circuit of the PV/STSC 
paired with a stratified TES system. A modeled PV/STSC, 
stratified TES system, pump, pipelines, auxiliary heater, 
weather data, integrator, online plotter, printer, and reservoir 
were included in the system. Cold HTF is converted to hot 
HTF by solar radiation captured by the PV/STSC system. 
The cumulative collection of hot HTF in the TES system 
facilitates occurrence of stratification within the storage 
tank. As a result, hot HTF which stayed at the top level and 
cold water is settled at the bottom level of the TES system. 
Meanwhile, cold HTF is directed to the bottom portion of 
the TES system for taking the hot water on the top layer 
to the auxiliary heater. While the cold HTF pumped at the 
bottom side of the TES system is retro into the PV/STSC 
for getting heat up and again heated HTF from the solar 
collector is sent to the TES system. This process continues 
throughout the year to calculate the optimum design parame-
ters for PV/STSC systems. The simulation is run with 0.25 h 
time steps. The mathematical method used in the study is a 
successive method.

Variations in PV/STSC area
Details of the impacts of changing the collector’s aper-
ture area from 1.6 to 9.6 ­m2 under identical weather con-
ditions with an increase in step size of 1.6 ­m2 on solar 
fraction were computed, and the results are presented in 
Fig. 11. Which shows replacing collector area from 1.6 
to 3.2 ­m2 causing an increase in the annual solar fraction 
to 39%, replacement of the area from 3.2 to 4.8 ­m2 and in 
the annual solar fraction to 12.4%, 4.8 to 6.4 ­m2 and its 
annual solar fraction to 4.0%, 6.4 to 8 ­m2, annual solar 
fraction to 1.2% and 8 to 9.6 ­m2 an annual solar fraction 
to 0.013% respectively. A comparison between the solar 
fraction readings for various collector areas was compared; 
it was noted that as the collector area increased; the solar 
fraction increased as well because it has a direct impact on 
the rate of heat transfer. However, the value of solar frac-
tion beyond the collector area of 8 ­m2 did not show any 
significant effect in solar fraction. Considering the space 
availability and affordability, the collector aperture area for 
the current study considers being 8 ­m2.

A single household consumes the electric power usage

PV generator accounts for 51% of energy consumption 
without any conversion losses. While, considering the

contribution to the household is seen as around 33%. The 
inverter conversion efficiency can be eliminated through 
use of DC powered appliances. In general, the power con-

Conclusion
Evaluation of the electrical and thermal performance of PV/
STSC coupled was done using TRNSYS under transient set-
tings for an entire year. The system was optimized for a hot 
water demand of 129 l and an electricity demand of 18.376 
kWh ­day−1. The following conclusions have been drawn.
•	 The optimized system exhibits an annual solar frac-
tion of 0.91 with parameters of PV/STSC area of 8 ­m2, 
mass flow rate of 60 kg ­h−1, volume, and height of TES 
system of 280 l and 0.8 m.
•	 The optimized system has the ability to meet the elec-
tricity demand for DC appliances to around78% annu-
ally, whereas it is 33% for AC appliances.
Thus from present work, it is predicted that the proposed 
system would be unable to provide 100% of a single house-
hold’s hot water and power energy demands in a tropical 
nation like India. The proposed system can be used as a sec-
ondary energy source for a saving in the cost of electricity.
