A B S T R A C T
Intelligent cyber-physical systems (CPS) are increasingly exposed to sophisticated cyberattacks, yet existing
machine learning (ML)-focused surveys provide only fragmented coverage of the resilience lifecycle. They often
separate attack modelling from defence strategies, overlook cross-sector insights, and lack clear evaluation
benchmarks, limiting practical applicability. This review offers the first lifecycle-oriented synthesis of ML-
enabled CPS resilience, covering detection, defence, recovery, and adaptation. It integrates diverse ML-based
attack techniques with corresponding resilience mechanisms and provides a comparative analysis across two
critical CPS sectors—power and water—to highlight shared vulnerabilities and sector-specific behaviours. The
review further distils key limitations in current ML approaches, including data scarcity, interpretability chal-
lenges, and limited real-world validation. Finally, it proposes five actionable research directions and resilience
quantification considerations to guide future development of robust and transferable ML-based CPS resilience
frameworks.

1. Introduction
Rapid technological advancements have transformed infrastructure
systems into “smart” networks by integrating communication technol-
ogies, giving rise to cyber-physical systems (CPS). These advancements
have significantly enhanced operational efficiency and facilitated
automated decision-making. However, they have also introduced sub-
stantial cyber vulnerabilities, which are often exploited through sector-
specific cyberattacks, reflecting the unique challenges inherent to
different infrastructure domains.
In power systems, the integration of renewable energy sources has
introduced novel vulnerabilities, particularly due to the proliferation of
distributed generation resources such as wind and solar (He et al.,
2022). These vulnerabilities were starkly highlighted in 2022 through a
series of high-profile incidents. In February, a cyberattack on the Viasat
KA-SAT satellite network disrupted the remote control of 5800 Enercon
wind turbines in Germany. Similarly, in April, another attack on Deut-
sche Windtechnik impacted 2000 wind turbines (Trakimaviˇcius). These
events underscore the urgent need for enhanced cybersecurity measures
to protect the renewable energy sector. The White House has further
emphasized the necessity of a comprehensive cybersecurity overhaul for
the nation’s clean energy infrastructure (Calma).
Cyberattacks on water systems also pose significant threats to public
health and national security. For instance, in 2023, a cyberattack in
County Mayo, Ireland, disrupted water treatment processes, compro-
mising water quality for 160 households over two days (‘ﺡﻡﻝﻩﺱﺍﯼﺏﺭﯼ

targeted five water and wastewater treatment facilities in the United

To address the growing challenges posed by cyberattacks, the
concept of resilience has emerged as a critical framework for managing
interruptions and incidents in infrastructure systems. Resilience is a
widely debated concept (Jufri et al., 2019), a well-known definition is
defined by the 4Rs: robustness, rapidity, resourcefulness, and redun-
dancy (Bruneau et al., 2003). Some definitions emphasize absorptive,
adaptive, and restorative capabilities (Nan and Sansavini, 2017), while
others define resilience as the ability to predict, absorb, adapt to, and
recover from disruptive events (Hosseini et al., 2016).
In the context of CPS within the infrastructure sector, resilience fo-
cuses on the ability to maintain continuous operation under adverse
conditions. This includes prioritizing safe degradation and preventing
significant performance interruptions (Segovia-Ferreira et al., 2024)
(Clark and Zonouz, 2019) (Arghandeh et al., 2016). Resilience, in this
sense, is the system’s capacity to endure disruptive events and efficiently
return to normal operations (Ti et al., 2022). As technology continues to
evolve, the complexity and unpredictability of cyber threats are
increasing, necessitating adaptive and robust approaches.
In this study, resilience in infrastructure CPS is defined in alignment
with ISO 22372:2024 as the system’s ability to absorb and adapt to a
changing environment. So, here we conceptualize resilience in two
stages, as shown in Fig. 1. Stage 1 — absorption — refers to the system’s
immediate ability to withstand the onset of a cyberattack, limiting
damage and maintaining essential functionality as performance de-
grades toward its lowest point, Pmin. This stage captures short-term
resistance and the system’s capacity to prevent uncontrolled failure.
Stage 2 — adaptation — involves the recovery and improvement pro-
cess, during which the system rebounds from Pmin and progresses toward
regaining optimal functionality Popt. From trec to topt, the system ideally
needs to surpass its original performance level, P0, to reach a higher
performance Popt to demonstrate the system ’s improved adaptability
and preparedness for future threats.
Other attributes such as reliability, robustness, survivability, safety,
and maintainability are noted for context but are not examined further,
as this study focuses specifically on resilience as the most comprehensive
measure of CPS response to cyber disruptions.
This study focuses on specific cyberattack types, including FDIA, DoS
attacks, machine learning attacks, and hybrid attacks, as these represent
significant challenges to the resilience of CPS. For each selected attack
type, we provide detailed modelling formulations to offer a compre-
hensive understanding of their underlying mechanisms. While a wide
range of cyber threats exists, this paper does not aim to cover all possible
attack types. Instead, it concentrates on these key examples to enable
focused and in-depth analysis.
The machine learning techniques presented in this paper primarily
focus on enhancing resilience; however, some ML-based cyberattack
modelling techniques are also discussed. While all resilience enhance-
ment countermeasures are ML-based, not all attack modelling tech-
niques rely on ML. As illustrated in Fig. 2, machine learning plays a
critical role in combating cyberattacks through various activities.
However, it is a double-edged sword, as it can also be leveraged to
optimize attack strategies, as shown in Fig. 3. While ML strengthens
resilience through detection, defence, recovery, and adaptive design,
modern cyberattacks can also exploit ML technique. At the same time,
CPS resilience is supported by non-ML mechanisms—including redun-
dancy, encryption, and control-theoretic safety—showing that ML acts
as both an enabler of resilience and a potential source of new
vulnerabilities.

focus narrowly on individual phases of the resilience lifecycle—such as

adaptive design, resulting in an incomplete and fragmented under-
standing of resilience. Furthermore, few reviews integrate cyberattack
modelling with ML-based countermeasures, leaving unclear how spe-
cific ML techniques address particular attack mechanisms. The table also
shows that existing works tend to emphasize either high-level CPS
concepts or single-sector analyses, with limited cross-sector comparison
across domains such as water and transportation, reducing the trans-
ferability of their findings. In contrast, our review is the only study that
systematically covers attack modelling, deep learning, reinforcement
learning, resilience analysis, defensive strategies, recovery approaches,

The contributions of this review are as follows:
1) We provide an in-depth review of diverse cyberattack strategies,
with a focus on advanced algorithmic and dynamic attacks designed
to cause severe disruptions.
2) We examine machine learning methods through the lens of a
comprehensive resilience process, covering the full spectrum from
anomaly detection to adaptive system design
3) We examine both the attack strategies and ML-based resilience ap-
proaches from the perspectives of two CPS domains—power and
water systems—providing a comparative analysis and outlining
tailored pathways forward for each sector
4) We discuss the limitations of current ML approaches, emphasizing
challenges related to real-world data compatibility and interpret-
ability in state-of-the-art algorithms
5) We outline five future research directions, each supported by prac-
tical examples, to guide the development of ML-driven resilience
solutions in CPS

2. Cyberattack modelling
Cyberattack modelling is the preliminary stage of resilience analysis.
The design and understanding of cyberattacks are essential to identify
system vulnerabilities and simulate potential threats effectively. This
stage involves constructing detailed attack scenarios to analyze how
cyberattacks compromise the functionality and performance of CPS.
Such models provide critical insights into attack vectors, methods, and
impacts, serving as a foundation for developing robust defence
mechanisms.
The following subsections introduce four key types of cyberattack
modelling techniques, focusing on their methodologies, applications,
and specific impact to power and water CPS.
2.1. False Data Injection Attacks

cyberattack where an attacker strategically injects false data into a
system to manipulate its operation and outcomes. FDIA is first intro-
duced by Liu et al., represent a significant threat to data integrity (Liu
et al., 2011). This attack involves the manipulation of sensor readings to
introduce errors in the calculation of state variables and values. The
terminology related to FDIA is summarised in Table 2.

the operations of the SG, undermining the reliability and stability of the
system (Nafees et al., 2023). There are two characteristics of FDIA to

2.3. Machine learning attack
ML attack: ML attack involves adversarial strategies aimed at
compromising the performance and reliability of ML models by target-
ing their training or inference processes (Takiddin et al., 2021), as
shown in Fig. 9. These attacks can be categorized into two main types:
data manipulation (poisoning attacks), which inject false or corrupted
data to degrade model training, and inference manipulation (white-box
or black-box attacks), where adversaries exploit model vulnerabilities
during real-time decision-making to achieve malicious goals. The ter-
minology related to ML attack is summarised in Table 4.
Poisoning attack: A poisoning attack involves manipulating the
training process by injecting or altering malicious data to degrade the
model’s performance or influence its behavior in a targeted manner (Sun
et al., 2022). There are some similarities and difference between FDIA
and poisoning attack. Regarding the similarities, they both manipulate
data, aim to corrupt the decision making which can cause incorrect re-
sponses. Regarding the difference, the target of FDIA is to modify the
real monitoring data of CPS, result in incorrect real time decision
making, while the target of poisoning attack is to generate the incorrect
training data and inject them to the machine learning algorithm. How-
ever, it is worth note that the FDIA can have the profound impact, if
without being detected or corrected, the recorded historical false data
can be used as the ML training data, can ultimately result in a poisoning
attack.

ML attack in water sector: Machine learning is increasingly used in
the water sector for critical applications such as water leakage predic-
tion, flow optimization, and quality monitoring, where accurate and
timely decision-making is essential. ML attacks exploit vulnerabilities in
these systems by tampering with sensor data or injecting adversarial
samples, enabling attackers to mask anomalies such as tank overflows,
pipe bursts, or contamination (Sikder et al., 2023). These attacks
leverage the spatial distribution of pipelines and reservoirs, creating
localized disruptions that avoid immediate cascading effects, while
exploiting slower physical propagation to evade detection. By manipu-
lating temporal and spatial data dependencies, adversaries can mislead
Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), compromising water quality, supply
reliability, and public health, making water systems particularly
vulnerable to stealthy and prolonged adversarial manipulations (Zizzo
et al., 2020).
Fig. 10 summarises the main categories of machine learning (ML)
attacks relevant to cyber-physical systems. White-box attacks assume
full access to model parameters and gradients, enabling gradient-based
adversarial generation methods such as FGSM, iterative attacks,
ensemble gradients, and momentum-driven variants. Model-behavior
simulation attacks focus on imitating or reverse-engineering a model’s
behavior, including PUF modelling, system imitation, and replay/
impersonation techniques. Data poisoning attacks corrupt training data
to degrade model performance, affecting federated learning, distributed
training, or inducing targeted label-flipping errors. Black-box attacks
operate without internal model knowledge, leveraging transferability,
query-based probing, or zeroth-order optimization to craft adversarial
inputs.(see Fig. 11)
All methods focus on exploiting vulnerabilities in machine learning
models used in CPS, primarily through the generation of adversarial
inputs. Two key characteristics emerge: (1) the deliberate modification
of input data—whether through gradient-based evasion, poisoning, or
simulation-based inference—to degrade model performance or evade
detection; and (2) the ability of ML attacks to target both inference
(evasion) and training (poisoning) phases, highlighting the need for end-
to-end security measures. As ML becomes increasingly integrated into
critical infrastructure, there is a growing need for robust, explainable,
and attack-resilient architectures, particularly in high-stakes domains
like energy management and smart grid security.
2.4. Hybrid cyberattacks
Hybrid cyberattacks: A hybrid cyberattack involves multiple types
of attacks, strategically arranged to maximize impact. These attacks may
occur sequentially, where one facilitates the next, or simultaneously.
The aim is to exploit vulnerabilities in a coordinated manner, potentially
leading to significant damage.
A cyberattack typically involves several phases. According to the
cyber kill chain, a successful attack progresses through seven stages: 1)
reconnaissance, 2) weaponization, 3) delivery, 4) exploitation, 5)
installation, 6) command and control, and 7) actions on objectives. For
example, during the reconnaissance phase, which occurs in the early
stages, the primary objective is to identify the target and potential vul-
nerabilities. In the later stages, the attacker builds on this groundwork to
carry out the attack.
Hybrid cyberattacks can take several forms. One type follows a
structured sequence, aligned with the phases of the cyber kill chain
(Presekal et al., 2023). Alternatively, some hybrid attacks occur simul-
taneously rather than in a specific order, often targeting different
components to amplify their overall impact (Abazari et al., 2024) (Khan
et al., 2022) (Chaudhary et al.) (Saxena et al., 2024) (Li et al., 2024b)
(Akbari et al., 2023).
Hybrid attack in power sector: In power systems, hybrid cyber-
attacks can have devastating impacts due to the interconnected nature of
generation, transmission, and distribution components. These attacks
often exploit vulnerabilities in Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition
(SCADA) systems, communication protocols, or state estimation pro-
cesses to manipulate critical operational parameters such as load, fre-
quency, and voltage. By combining multiple attack vectors, such as FDIA
to mislead operators and malware to disable protective relays, attackers
can destabilize the grid. This can lead to cascading failures, system-wide
blackouts, and even physical damage to infrastructure such as trans-
formers and generators. The interdependence of power systems with
telecommunications and other critical infrastructures amplifies the
consequences, creating widespread societal and economic disruptions.

3.1. Stage 1 – absorption
In Stage 1, which focuses on applying machine learning to enhance

3.1.1. Anomaly detection
Anomaly detection is a crucial technique in identifying unusual
patterns or deviations from normal behavior, allowing for early detec-
tion of potential cyber-attacks. By analyzing data in real time, anomaly
detection models can flag suspicious activities, enabling timely inter-
vention to prevent or mitigate damage to the system (see Fig. 12).
3.1.1.1. Deep learning-based approach. In this section, we focus specif-
ically on semi-supervised and unsupervised deep learning methods,
given the scarcity of labeled data in real-world scenarios; the underlying
mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 13.
1) Semi-supervised prediction-based methods:
This semi supervised methods indicate the operator have normal
data, which feed into the deep learning model first, and then this
model detects the anomalies by the prediction error.
These methods usually incorporate two steps. The first step is to
train the model (e.g., LSTM, GRU) using only normal data to learn
the typical temporal and structural patterns of the system. The model
learns to reconstruct or predict future values of the input time series.
(Al-Hawawreh et al., 2021) (Chawla et al., 2023a) (Kumar et al.,
2023) (Karanfil et al., 2023) (Machina et al., 2023). Second step
During deployment (inference phase), the trained model is fed with
new (possibly anomalous) data. The model attempts to reconstruct or
predict the input prediction error (for LSTMs, GRUs, etc.) is
computed. If this error exceeds a defined threshold, the instance is
flagged as anomalous.
2) Unsupervised reconstruction-based methods:
These methods operate without prior knowledge of what defines
normal behavior. Instead of relying solely on normal data, they allow
flexibility by incorporating assumptions about the rarity or distinc-
tiveness of anomalies. Typically, reconstruction-based models such
as AE are used, where the model learns to reconstruct input data

3.1.1.2. Reinforcement learning-based approach. Compared with deep
learning, RL-based anomaly detection is not well developed, primarily
due to its complexity in implementation, reliance on interaction-based
learning, and significant computational demands, which make it less
practical for real-time applications.

bilities with more advanced and adaptive techniques. For example, Yang
et al.’s Agent-based Dynamic Thresholding (ADT) framework demon-
strates how RL, combined with an AE and DQN, can dynamically adjust
detection thresholds in real time (Yang et al., 2024). This approach al-
lows for the identification of anomalies under challenging conditions,
such as noisy or delayed feedback, while maintaining low false positive
and negative rates. The ADT framework highlights the promise of RL to
increase anomaly detection accuracy and adaptability, particularly in
dynamic and high-stakes environments like water systems, where timely
interventions are critical for ensuring operational reliability and
cybersecurity.

optimal strategies to combat cyberattacks. These approaches estimate
the value, or expected utility, of potential defensive actions, allowing
control systems in CPS to learn and adaptively refine their responses to
evolving threats. By prioritizing actions that maximize detection accu-
racy and mitigation effectiveness, these RL techniques enable these
systems to enhance their defences, continuously optimizing based on
observed outcomes and reducing vulnerability over time.
Various Q-learning-based approaches have been explored in recent
studies to strengthen defences and optimize decision-making in systems
facing complex and adversarial conditions. Lakshminarayana et al. use
Q-learning to improve defences against FDIAs in cyber-physical control
systems (CPCS), where the system learns optimal responses by evalu-
ating actions that minimize estimation errors. By updating Q-values
based on rewards for effective actions, the approach creates a defence
policy that continuously adapts to new attacks (Lakshminarayana et al.,
2019). Another study applies deep Q-learning in microgrids, employing
a deep neural network to approximate Q-values for defensive actions.
This allows the system to dynamically adjust controls in response to
different attack scenarios, optimizing its defences based on historical
data (Rath et al., 2024). Similarly, Islam et al. use Federated DQN to
enhance UAVs’(Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) decision-making for task
offloading in uncertain environments (Islam et al., 2023). By framing
offloading as a Markov Decision Process, this approach enables
resource-efficient strategies, extending UAVs’ operational time.
Game theoretic Value-Based RL: Game-theoretic value-based RL
applies principles from game theory to value-based RL, enabling systems
to develop optimal defensive strategies in adversarial contexts. Unlike
standard Q-learning or deep Q-learning, which optimize individual
agent performance, game-theoretic RL explicitly models interactions
between competing agents (e.g., defender vs. attacker) in a zero-sum
framework, often leading to a Nash equilibrium. This equilibrium rep-
resents a stable state where both agents’ strategies are optimized in
anticipation of each other’s actions, making it particularly effective in
scenarios where adaptive, intelligent attacks threaten system stability.
Yu et al. construct an RL algorithm for a two-player zero-sum sto-
chastic game between a sensor (defender) and a malicious adversary
(attacker), where the sensor minimizes tracking error and energy use,
and the attacker seeks to maximize disruption. The RL approach in-
corporates a state-based adaptive reward function balancing tracking
performance with energy efficiency, leading to a Nash equilibrium that
ensures resilient tracking control while conserving energy, enhancing
system resilience under adversarial conditions (Yu et al., 2024). Simi-
larly, Guo et al. employ minimax-Q learning to create dynamic defence
strategies against Dynamic Load Altering Attacks (D-LAAs) on power
grids. This algorithm models adversarial interactions within a zero-sum
game, iteratively updating Q-values for state-action pairs to converge on
an optimal defence policy, which minimizes potential damage and
adapts to attacker strategies (Guo et al., 2021). Yuan et al. applies Nash
Q-learning in a dynamic Bayesian game for wireless communications,
integrating belief-based updates and discounted future rewards to
handle incomplete information. This adaptation enables players to
maximize cumulative payoffs, effectively addressing uncertainty and
strategic decision-making in adversarial settings (Yuan et al., 2024).
2) Policy based RL
Policy-based RL is a framework where the algorithm directly learns a
policy, or strategy, for selecting actions rather than estimating values for
each state-action pair, as in value-based RL. This approach is particu-
larly useful in continuous or complex environments, such as cyber-
physical systems, where fine-tuned, adaptive responses are needed to
counter cyber threats. Unlike value-based RL, which aims to maximize
expected rewards through Q-values, policy-based RL focuses on finding
optimal action policies, making it highly effective for scenarios requiring
continuous adaptation, such as maintaining system stability under
attack.
Yang et al. leverage the fuzzy Win or Learn Fast-Policy Hill-Climbing
(WoLF-PHC) algorithm, using variable learning rates and fuzzy state
aggregation (FSA) to optimize power allocation and beamforming at an
Intelligent Reflecting Surface (IRS). This approach enables efficient,
adaptable policy adjustments, effectively countering jamming attacks by
maximizing system rate and minimizing power use (Yang et al., 2021).
Meanwhile, Palanimuthu et al. use a policy-based RL scheme for wind
turbines to maintain stability amid aperiodic DoS attacks. Their
approach integrates Sliding Mode Control (SMC) and adaptive learning,
allowing real-time policy adaptation to handle changing wind condi-
tions and attack patterns without extensive modelling. Both studies
showcase how policy-based RL, enhanced with techniques like FSA and
SMC, enables systems to respond effectively and maintain performance
under adversarial conditions (Palanimuthu et al., 2023).
3) Actor-Critic RL
Actor-Critic RL combines elements of both value-based and policy-
based approaches, enabling systems to optimize their response to
cyberattacks by balancing action selection and evaluation. In Actor-
Critic methods, the actor selects actions based on a policy, while the
critic evaluates these actions by estimating value functions, guiding the
actor toward optimal strategies. This structure allows for more stable
learning and adaptability in continuous action spaces, unlike pure value-
based RL, which focuses solely on value estimation, or policy-based RL,
which directly optimizes policies without explicit value evaluation.
In the study from Tabassum et al. an RL agent using the Twin-
Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) algorithm is imple-
mented to mitigate FDIAs by continuously monitoring the microgrid’s
state and dynamically adjusting control actions to maintain stability
(Tabassum et al., 2024a). The TD3 algorithm leverages an actor-critic
framework with delayed policy updates, which stabilize learning by
preventing excessive corrective actions that could destabilize the sys-
tem. This approach allows the RL agent to adapt in real-time to the
severity of the attack, effectively minimizing frequency deviations and
sustaining the microgrid’s operational integrity. By doing so, the RL
agent significantly outperforms traditional PID controllers, demon-
strating the advantage of actor-critic methods in maintaining resilience
under adversarial conditions.
3.1.2.2. Deep learning approach. In the context of proposing defensive
strategies in real-time control during cyberattacks, these methods are
primarily focused on predicting system behavior or dynamics under

3.2. Stage 2 – adaptation
Stage 1 focuses on enhancing system resilience during an attack.
Stage 2 aims to recover the system, improve its robustness by
learning from failures, and adapt to future changes. The goal is not
only to restore performance to normal levels but also to surpass pre-
attack performance and prepare for evolving challenges. This stage
emphasizes two key activities: recovery strategies and adaptive
design.
3.2.1. Recovery strategy
3.2.1.1. Recovery of data. Machine learning techniques are increasingly
applied to address data integrity challenges posed by cyberattacks, such
as missing data from system disruptions or manipulated data from at-
tacks. For missing data, machine learning models can predict and
reconstruct gaps based on learned patterns, while for manipulated data,
they can detect and correct anomalies to maintain reliable system
operation. These capabilities are crucial in cyber-physical systems,
where ensuring data continuity and accuracy is essential for maintaining
situational awareness and operational resilience under adversarial
conditions.
Dealing missing data: To address missing data in complex, time-
sensitive systems like power grids, machine learning models such as
Overcomplete Denoising Autoencoders (ODAE) are commonly used.
These autoencoders learn representations from complete data, capturing
spatial and temporal correlations to predict missing values. By encoding
data into a higher-dimensional latent space, ODAEs leverage learned
patterns for accurate imputation, even when data gaps arise from dis-
ruptions like Denial of Service (DoS) attacks.
The reconstruction of missing PMU data in (Chawla et al., 2023b) is
achieved using an Overcomplete Denoising Autoencoder (ODAE). The
ODAE model encodes the input Phasor Data Concentrator (PDC) data

Dealing manipulated data: Data manipulation, such as that caused
by FDIA, poses significant threats over extended periods by undermining
system integrity and reliability. To address this challenge, machine
learning methods are often integrated with anomaly detection tech-
niques. Once anomalous data is detected, these methods initiate the
generation of corrective data to replace or adjust the manipulated en-
tries. This two-step approach—detection followed by correction—en-
ables the system to maintain accurate and reliable performance, even in
the face of ongoing data manipulation, thus bolstering long-term resil-
ience against cyber threats.
Machina et al. employ a Multi-Deep Neural Network (MDNN) for
anomaly detection, targeting FDI attacks in Direct Current Microgrids
(DCMGs) (Machina et al., 2023). An Error Detection Network (EDN)
monitors deviations in output voltages, trained on both normal and
attack scenarios, and generates corrective signals when anomalies are
detected. Chawla et al. utilize GAIN to correct corrupted data, mitigating
cyberattacks’ impact and supporting reliable operation of critical ap-
plications during attacks (Chawla et al., 2023a). Falas et al. (2020) and
Guibene et al. (2023) propose leveraging PINNs to recover manipulated
data in water system during cyberattacks by integrating physical laws
into sensor measurement predictions. This approach enhances accuracy
and reduces computational demands, enabling efficient real-time
recovery.
3.2.1.2. Recovery of system. Isolating strategy: An effective isolating
strategy is essential for the recovery of cyberattacks in CPS, as it enables
targeted removal of compromised data, thereby enhancing the accuracy
of subsequent state estimations. Lu et al. propose Representation-
Learning-Based Convolutional Neural Network (RL-CNN), which uti-
lizes representation learning to detect and isolate compromised mea-
surements (Lu et al., 2024). In this approach, RL-CNN leverages a deep
autoencoder to reduce feature dimensionality, allowing the algorithm to
treat the localization task as a multi-label classification problem and
accurately identify attack points across CPPS data. Once attacks are
localized, the Minimum Mean-Squared Error estimator is employed for
recovery, taking specific actions to exclude contaminated measurements
and estimate system states with minimized error variance.
Reconnecting strategy: In the recovery process following cyber-
attacks, an effective reconnection strategy is essential to maintain sys-
tem stability and prevent cascading failures, as abrupt restoration of
power lines can exacerbate disturbances. For power system, a well-
timed reconnection strategy allows for gradual synchronization of
affected regions, which minimizes the risk of power surges and ensures
smoother integration back into the operational grid.
Pacheco et al. employ the Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient
(DDPG) algorithm to facilitate recovery of cyber-physical power systems
following cyberattacks (Wei et al., 2020). Specifically, the DDPG
framework calculates an optimal reclosing strategy for the transmission
lines disconnected during an attack, aiming to prevent potential insta-
bility caused by immediate reconnection. This strategy involves intro-
ducing calculated delays for each transmission line to avoid excessive
current flows or power swings that could further compromise system
integrity. By selecting these delays within safe operational boundaries,
the proposed method seeks to restore system stability with minimal risk
of cascading failures, thus enhancing resilience against cyber-induced
disruptions.
Recovery system state: Effective system recovery is essential once
an attack has been isolated and the extent of data corruption is under-
stood. Zhang et al., propose a runtime recovery framework that (after
detecting a sensor attack) removes poisoned data, reconstructs the sys-
tem state, and uses a Linear-Quadratic Regulator (LQR) to compute a
safe control trajectory that drives the system back to a safe target region
before a specified deadline (Zhang et al., 2021b).
In the recovery analysis of CPS under cyberattacks, many strategies
focus on data-centric solutions, such as missing data imputation and
corrective data generation, to restore data availability and integrity.
However, there remains a scarcity of machine learning-driven ap-
proaches that address recovery from a broader, system-level perspec-
tive. Unlike data-focused solutions, system-level ML strategies, such as
(Wei et al., 2020), emphasize optimizing overall system resilience and
stability in the wake of an attack, marking a critical area for further
research and development in CPS recovery (Aslani et al., 2024b).
3.2.2. Adaptive design
Adaptive design aims to strengthen system components and pro-
cesses, making them resilient to potential disruptions caused by cyber-
attacks. By incorporating fault tolerance and redundancy, robust
design minimizes vulnerabilities, ensuring the system can continue
functioning effectively even under adverse conditions.

Optimization (DRO) framework, leveraging Conditional Value-at-Risk
(CVaR) to address sector interdependencies and mitigate FDIA impacts
in coupled water-energy systems (Zhao et al., 2021b). Together, these
studies highlight the versatility of ML and optimization in simulating
attacks, identifying weaknesses, and enhancing system resilience.
3.2.2.3. Explainable machine learning. A growing body of research has
begun to adapt explainability techniques specifically for CPS. Wang
et al. applied temporal–spatial attention visualisation to transformer
models to explain load-forecasting anomalies in energy systems. For
networked CPS, Lucic et al. proposed CF-GNNExplainer, which is a
method that generates counterfactual explanations for Graph Neural
Networks by identifying minimal perturbations (edge deletions) that
change the model’s prediction (Lucic et al., 2022). These emerging
techniques provide deeper insight into how hypothetical interventions
influence system states, enabling more transparent diagnostics and
supporting informed operator decision-making. However, the trans-
lation of XAI methods into real-world CPS deployments is still at an early
stage, with significant gaps between experimental demonstrations and
practical implementation.
Explainable ML supports the adaptation stage of resilience by
revealing why models behave as they do and which system factors drive
failures or attack impacts. These insights enable engineers to refine
models, update control strategies, and redesign system components in a
transparent, informed manner. By showing how small changes could
have prevented or mitigated disruptions, XAI facilitates continuous
learning and adaptive design, strengthening long-term CPS resilience.
4. Limitations of state-of-art
While ML approaches have shown significant potential in enhancing
the resilience of CPS, several critical limitations remain. The limitations
are mainly from the practical use of the ML in real world CPS
environments.
Limitation 1: Rigid anomaly assumptions in general-purpose
detection methods: Anomaly detection algorithms are typically built
on specific assumptions about what constitutes an anomaly, some
typical anomalies assumptions are shown in Table 11. However, in real
world CPS, anomalies can take many forms, and relying on a single
assumption often fails to capture this complexity. Therefore, current
state-of-art general-purpose anomaly detection methods across different
CPS datasets can lead to inaccurate results in real world application.
Limitation 2: Lack of adaptive and causality-aware learning in
ML for CPS: Current ML methods in CPS largely rely on static, offline
training and often lack adaptive learning frameworks capable of
responding to evolving system dynamics. These approaches typically
focus on detecting anomalies after they occur, rather than proactively
preventing them. Moreover, they tend to overlook the underlying
physical processes that generate anomalies, limiting their interpret-
ability and effectiveness in real-world systems. While causality-inspired
ML and root cause analysis have gained attention for early anomaly
detection, they often depend heavily on historical labeled data, which is
scarce and may not represent emerging disturbances (Zhao and Zhang,
2024) (Wang et al., 2023) (Han et al., 2025). Even when successful on
public CPS datasets, such models struggle in real-world CPS due to noisy
data,
heterogeneous
variables,
and
complex,
large-scale
inter-
actions—making it difficult to reliably identify true causal relationships
and generalize to unseen scenarios.
Limitation 3: Lack of physically meaningful interpretability in
ML models for CPS: Effective resilience in CPS requires a deep under-
standing
of
cause-effect
relationships
within
the
system.
While
explainable
AI
(XAI)
techniques—such
as
attention
mechanisms
(Vaswani et al., 2023)—aim to improve transparency, their outputs
often lack physical meaning. For instance, in attention mechanisms,
highlighting a specific time step or variable may indicate where the
model is focusing, but it often lacks context or physical mean-
ing—limiting its usefulness for system operators who need actionable
insights to prevent future disruptions.
To address these limitations, we propose a general four-step work-
flow that can be incorporated into future ML approaches for CPS
resilience:
●Step one: Identify and define multiple anomaly types based on his-
torical data, ensuring clear and domain-specific categorization to
address Limitation 1
●Step two: Enrich the anomaly context by identifying causal patterns,
time-lag effects, and links to subsequent system failures, aiming to
address the causality aspect of Limitation 2.
●Step three: Implement an explainable framework that facilitates
collaboration with domain experts to validate Step One and Step
Two, aiming to address Limitation 3.
●Step four: Enable adaptive learning by continuously updating the
model with new data to capture evolving disturbances. As new data
becomes available, repeat Steps One and Two to redefine anomaly
types and enrich contextual understanding—ensuring the model re-
mains aligned with the system’s changing behavior and maintains
long-term relevance, aiming to address the adaptive capability
aspect of Limitation 2.

5. Future direction
Chapter 5 presents the future directions emerging from this review,
focusing on how evolving cyber–physical threats require improved un-
derstanding and adaptive defence mechanisms. The chapter is organised
into four parts: pathways for understanding attack methods, absorption
and adaptation, and a set of actionable research directions.
5.1. Path forward for power system
Modern power systems face increasingly complex cyber–physical
threats, making resilience a critical research priority. This section
highlights key directions for strengthening grid security, including
improved attack modelling, advanced anomaly detection, and recovery
and adaptation strategies tailored to large-scale Cyber-Physical Power
Systems (CPPS).
Attack Modelling and Vulnerability Assessment:
• Move beyond maximizing operational errors to systematic vulnera-
bility assessment across grid components.
• Identify weak points in the infrastructure that could be exploited by
sophisticated adversaries.
• Investigate ML-specific vulnerabilities, including:
• Adversarial attacks on models
• Bias exploitation
• Data poisoning and training-time manipulation
• Develop robust ML models that can resist stealthy and adaptive
cyberattacks (Ibrahim et al., 2023).
Advanced Anomaly Detection for Large-Scale Power Grids:
• Develop anomaly detection algorithms capable of processing large-
scale, high-dimensional, real-time data.
• Account
for
strong
interdependencies
among
power
grid
components.
• Expand the use of graph-based anomaly detection to model struc-

• Incorporate time-lag effects, dynamic dependencies, and multivar-

• Integrate root-cause analysis to support timely and effective defen-
sive responses after detection.
Recovery, Adaptation, and Future System Design:
• Build on emerging ML-based recovery methods such as adversarial
training and resilient model architectures.
• Enhance the adaptability of Cyber-Physical Power Systems (CPPS)
under evolving attack scenarios.
• Address new vulnerabilities introduced by renewable energy inte-
gration, including intermittency and unpredictability.
• Examine risks arising from advanced automation and autonomous
control strategies.
• Prioritize scenario-based analyses to evaluate resilience against

• Design adaptive systems that can respond to changing operational
conditions and novel threats.
These research directions underscore the need for an integrated
resilience approach in power systems—one that proactively identifies
vulnerabilities, detects anomalies in real time, and enables adaptive
recovery. Advancing these capabilities will help ensure secure and
reliable grid operation under evolving cyber–physical threats.
5.2. Path forward for water system
Water distribution systems face unique cyber–physical risks due to
their strong interdependencies, large spatial footprints, and exposure to
environmental factors. Strengthening resilience in this domain requires
advances in attack modelling, improved anomaly detection under real-
world data constraints, and recovery strategies that prioritize public
health and safety. This section outlines key challenges and research di-
rections across these areas.
Attack Modelling & Vulnerability Analysis.
• Conduct more scenario-based cyberattack modelling to uncover
spatial and temporal vulnerabilities.
• Water systems have stronger interdependencies (clean water,
wastewater, sewage) →cascading attack risks.
• Hybrid physical–cyber-attacks exploiting factors such as rainfall or
infrastructure stress require deeper study.
• These scenarios pose elevated health and safety risks, including
contamination during service disruptions (Chen et al., 2019b) (Liu
et al., 2024).
Data and Detection Challenges.
• Water networks exhibit large spatial footprints and depend heavily
on external factors (e.g., weather).
• Limited availability of representative, real-world datasets constrains
anomaly detection research.
• Current studies rely mainly on SWaT and a few foundational testbeds
→need more diverse datasets (Narayanan et al., 2022) (Ntalampiras
and Potamitis, 2023).
• Defensive strategies must better incorporate spatial complexity and
environmental dependencies.
Recovery and Public Health Considerations.
• ML-based recovery schemes remain underdeveloped, especially at
the system level.
• Current recovery research focuses on data reconstruction, not oper-
ational system recovery.
• Water systems require fast, safety-critical recovery, particularly
under contamination scenarios.
• Future solutions should integrate spatial–temporal information to
isolate affected areas and restore service rapidly.
• Prioritize recovery methods that explicitly account for public health
risks during cyberattacks.
Overall, enhancing resilience in water systems demands more real-
istic cyberattack modelling, richer and more representative datasets,
and recovery methods that account for spatial complexity and health-
critical consequences. Progress in these areas will support safer, faster,
and more reliable responses to cyber–physical disruptions in essential
water services.
5.3. Actionable research directions
In Section 4, we outlined key limitations and proposed targeted so-
lutions to address them. Building on that foundation, Section 5 presents
five future trajectories aimed at advancing the use of machine learning
for resilience analysis in CPS.
Developing multi-modal data fusion frameworks to establish a
robust baseline: Combining operational, contextual, and environ-
mental data to establish a robust baseline for more realistic and effective
cyberattack countermeasures. Operational data refers to dynamic sys-
tem behavior, typically represented as time-series numerical data.
Contextual data includes static descriptive information, often in the
form of text, such as maintenance logs or system configuration. Envi-
ronmental data captures external factors that influence system behavior,
such as weather conditions, holidays, events, or news reports. Inte-
grating diverse data sources offers a deeper understanding of CPS

strategies.

6. Conclusion
Enhancing the resilience of infrastructure systems against advanced
cyberattacks has become an imperative. ML holds great promise for
strengthening the resilience of CPS. This paper has reviewed ML
approach across various stages, including two stages and four activities:
stage one absorption including (1) anomaly detection and (2) defence;
stage two adaption including (3) recovery, and (4) adaptive system
design.
