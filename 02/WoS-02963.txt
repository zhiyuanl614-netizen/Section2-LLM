Abstract—Demand response (DR) is a valuable tool in load
frequency control (LFC) of power systems, especially in improv-
ing the system’s regulating capacity. The pumps, with their
rapid response capabilities, can be effectively utilized as a
DR resource to further enhance LFC performance. However,
improper adjustment of pumps will cause the accumulation of
pressure pulsations throughout the water distribution system
(WDS), resulting in the water hammer effect. To address this
issue, this paper introduces a water hammer feedback loop in
LFC with DR to balance the power system stability as well as
WDS operational safety. With the wave tracking method (WTM),
the proposed LFC method enables efﬁcient modeling of the
hydrodynamic process with low computational complexity and
acceptable accuracy. A model predictive control (MPC) based
optimal control algorithm is also developed to adjust the pumps
and generation units. Case studies demonstrate that the proposed
model exhibits signiﬁcant improvement on the performance of
pressure pulsation regulation. Results also show the enhanced
stability and effective pressure ﬂuctuation mitigation in the
electric-water coordinated control.
Index Terms—Electric-water coordinated control, demand
response, water hammer effect, load frequency control, model
predictive control.

Water distribution system (WDS), as an energy-intensive
infrastructure, holds signiﬁcant potential for ﬂexible regula-
tion. WDS accounts for 7.1% of the total annual energy
consumption in California, USA [2] and up to 10% in
Changzhou City, China [3]. Frequency support capability of
the WDS is mainly derived from the rapid response charac-
teristics and inertial support of water pumps. The adjustment
range is continuously expended with the increasing adoption of
variable-speed pumps (VSPs) [4]. Since VSPs are connected
to the grid via the frequency inverters, their contribution to
inertial support is reduced or non-existent [5]. This means their
operation must be explicitly controlled, cannot autonomously
react to frequency ﬂuctuation. Their response is orchestrated
by the DR aggregator based on the control signal from the
control facility of power systems [6].
PumpcontrolisakeysteptostabilityoperationofWDS.WDS
exhibits “high inertia” and is slow to respond to disturbances
or adjustments, owing to the large mass of water within it.
“High inertia” increases system stability by buffering against
sudden changes. Thus, frequency ﬂuctuation of power system
can be partly transferred within the WDS by DR control
of pumps. However, Changes in pump speed bring pressure
pulsations, known as the water hammer effect, which propagates
as hydroacoustic pressure waves throughout the WDS [18].
The overly rapid response of VSPs causes drastic pressure
ﬂuctuations in WDS, leading to the vibration issues that may
jeopardize the system’s safety [19]. Existing studies treat water
hammer as an isolated issue. Those works focus on pipe design
optimization [29], active noise control [20], and pressure relief
valves [32]. The strategies primarily aim to accelerate the
attenuation of water hammer by increasing the damping in the
water transmission process. The dynamic interactions between
water hammer mitigation and the role of pumps in grid stability
were not considered. In fact, the water hammer effect can
be reduced at its source through proper pump control. It is
essential to balance the stability performance of the power
system and water distribution system when more water pumps
are incorporated into LFC as DR resources.

developed [7], [8], [9]. The ﬂexible regulation of DR increases

DR were also proposed, such as PID control [12], adaptive
control [13], model predictive control [14], and sliding mode

To the best of our knowledge, the modeling of water
hammer effect and its use in the LFC with pumps have not
been reported.
The hydrodynamic process of water hammer effect can be
described by the differential momentum and the continuity
equations, which are generally the partial differential equations
(PDEs) that do not have analytic solutions [21]. Numerical
methods, such as the ﬁnite difference (FD) method [22] and
the method of characteristics (MOC) [23], have been used
to ﬁnd the solutions. These methods are often applied in the
form of time and space discretization, described as ’‘char-
acteristic grids”. Under actual operating conditions, dense
spatial discretization, which consumes substantial computa-
tional resources, may not be necessary. Operators typically
focus on speciﬁc issues, such as local pressure surges at
pumps or other critical points [24]. Recently, the electric
circuit analogy has been applied to model the water transient
process [25]. But that study relies on Laplace transform, which
makes the model parameterization relatively challenging.
Most studies for LFC with DRs were developed based on
the idea that the consumption of controllable loads can be
changed instantaneously to improve the frequency stability
of power system [6]. In these works, DRs were treated as
independent units with constant frequency response dynamics.
This is not the case for the pumps in WDS, because the control
of one pump affects the water ﬂow and pressure of other
pumps. Thus, pumps need to be considered as interconnected
units in the WDS model to better reﬂect the dynamic response
of pump control. Fast pump control enables fast response
for frequency stability of the power system. However, it also
causes abrupt change in water pressure, which leads to the
water hammer effect. In a situation that requires the control
of multiple pumps, improper control can incur the cumulation
of water pressure ﬂuctuations, resulting in serious hammer
effects.
In this paper, a coordinated LFC model for the integrated
electrical-water system (IEWS) is proposed to enhance LFC
performance by achieving fast frequency stability of power
system and, at the same time, alleviating the water pressure
ﬂuctuation. The model considers pump pressure establishes a
water hammer feedback loop is developed to deal with the
above problem and to enhance the LFC performance. The
wave tracking method (WTM) [26] is employed to simulate
the transient process and the water hammer effect in WDS
with low computational complexity and high accuracy. Main
contributions are
a) Propose an electric-water coordinated load frequency
control framework considering the water hammer effect in

systems to improve the stability. First, the dynamic response
model for WDS is constructed based on the wave tracking
method. Then an IEWS-LFC model with water hammer
feedback loop is developed.

C. Implementation Procedure
The proposed MPC controller updates the control signals
in every sampling period. Its application procedure for IEWS-
LFC model can be summarized as follows.
Step 1: Calculate initial ﬂow rate and pressure, set the
simulation step ts, and the sample period h, and formulate the
state-space equations.
Step 2: Find a gain K such that all eigenvalues of Tk −kK
are inside the unit circle.
Step 3: Solve the terminal weighting matrix P in (26).
Step 4: Calculate the terminal constraints via (27).
Step 5: Simulate system behavior with time-step ts.
Step 6: Calculate the optimal control policy u*DR(tk) and
perform power regulation considering the control delays dDR
k,i
and dG
k .
In this process, steps 1 to 4 initialize and simulate the system
dynamics. After that, at each control step tk, the optimal
control policy u∗
DR(tk) is determined by solving the quadratic
optimization problem (17) online. System simulation continues
with u∗
DR(tk).

V. CONCLUSION
This paper has presented a controller design of an integrated
electrical-water system for balancing the frequency deviation
and pressure ﬂuctuation. The computational efﬁciency of
hydrodynamic process is obtained by eliminating unnecessary
spatial discretization using the water tracking method. Based on
the model predictive control, a stochastic Lyapunov equation
is established to ensure the frequency stability over an inﬁnite
horizon. An analytical feedback control gain is also derived.
Simulation results have demonstrated that the proposed
hydrodynamic transient modeling method is effective and the
coordinated control strategy can suppress the water hammer
effect. Performance of the frequency response is also improved
in comparison to the traditional LFC-DR.
In future work, effort will focus on the coordinated control
strategy between different generation units and the water
system. Additionally, controller parameter tuning methods will
be explored to improve the frequency regulation performance.
