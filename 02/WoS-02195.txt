Resilience of Cyber-Enabled Electrical Energy and
Water Distribution Systems Considering
Infrastructural Robustness Under Conditions of
Limited Water and/or Energy Availability
Scott Zuloaga
, Student Member, IEEE, Puneet Khatavkar, Larry Mays, and Vijay Vittal
, Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—New metrics and a new methodology for determining
system infrastructural–operational resilience are presented for the
optimal real-time operation of two critical interdependent infras-
tructure systems—the water distribution systems (WDS) and the
electric power system (EPS) under critical conditions of limited
water and/or limited electrical energy resulting from extreme
drought or electric grid failure, respectively. Operational resilience
(OR) and infrastructural resilience are deﬁned. The integrated re-
silience computation method is presented and utilizes results from
a combined optimization–simulation framework, which involves
capturing the interactions and associated dynamics between the
EPS and WDS. A realistic example of the WDS and EPS is used to
demonstrate the application of the resilience concepts to assess the
interdependent systems’ performance.
Index Terms—Electric power system (EPS), extended time-
domain simulations, infrastructural resilience (IR), operational
resilience (OR), real-time operations, water distribution systems
(WDS), water energy nexus.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE electric power system (EPS) and the water distribution
system (WDS) are two operationally interdependent criti-
cal infrastructure systems that have been recognized and studied
in broad generalities for some time. The main dependence of the
EPS on the WDS is the water required for the cooling cycle of
thermoelectric power generation. The main dependence of the
WDS on the EPS is the electric power needed for pumping water
from a source to the treatment plant and end-user through the
WDS. The interdependencies become critical under conditions
oflimitedwaterand/orelectricityavailability.Theoptimization–
simulation framework [1] used for this article accounts for these
interdependencies. If power plant cooling water demands are not
met, then the EPS may fail to supply adequate electric power for
WDS pumping stations leading to a cascading failure of both
systems.
This article presents new metrics and a new methodology for
computation of infrastructural–operational resilience (IOR) for
real-time operations of WDS and EPS under critical conditions
of limited water and/or limited electrical energy resulting from
extreme drought or electric grid failure. IOR is calculated by
weighting the operational resilience (OR) with the appropriate
infrastructural resilience (IR) metrics. The methodology for the
resilience calculations is depicted in Fig. 1 and considers the
impacts of both reduced water availability and electric power
supply. The computation presented involves calculations that
capture the robustness of a systems’ structural conﬁguration as
well as the operating parameters.
A. Deﬁnition of OR and IR for WDS and EPS Systems
1) Resilience of Critical Infrastructure Systems: Increasing
attention had been paid to resilience calculations for critical
infrastructure systems. The US National Infrastructure Advi-
sory Council broadly deﬁnes resilience [2] with four distinct
characteristics in the context of critical infrastructure systems.
1) Robustness—encompasses the ability of a system to
remain operable after contingencies occur.
Fig. 1.
Resilience computation methodology.
2) Resourcefulness—the ability to manage disasters.
3) Rapid recovery—the ability to manage disasters, limit the
impact, and return to predisaster operating conditions.
4) Adaptability within the system and its operating proce-
dures and policies to improve the robustness and recovery
characteristics for future events.
A few notable deﬁnitions of resilience of an engineered sys-
tem are given in [3]–[7]. The EPS and the WDS are inherently
reliable in their operation and resilient to disturbances that
impact the systems due to the extensive and thorough design
and planning procedures of the two systems. However, metrics
that capture interdependent quantities within each system and
methods by which to calculate those metrics have not been
previously explored.
2) Resilience of the WDS: OR of WDS is deﬁned as how
quickly the system recovers from not meeting acceptable pres-
sure levels. Aydin et al. [8] deﬁned OR as the ratio of the number
of times a satisfactory performance follows an unsatisfactory
performance and the number of times an unsatisfactory perfor-
mance occurs within the system. A few deﬁnitions of resilience
for WDS network structure and operations have been reported
in the literature. In [6], [9]–[15], different deﬁnitions for WDS
design and operations are presented. Shin et al. [16] investigated
the deﬁnitions of quantitative resilience measures for WDSs in
detail. Recently, a new methodology for the computation of OR
for WDS has been presented [17]. Most of the studies in the
literature focus on the resilience-based design of WDS except
for a couple of studies in [8] and [18], which are based on OR of
WDS. OR is deﬁned as the capacity of an engineered system to
absorb disturbances and reorganize while undergoing change
so as to still retain essentially the same function, structure,
identity, and feedbacks [5], [9]. This article uses the deﬁnition
of OR given by Aydin et al. [8] for developing a real-time
resilience assessment methodology for WDS operations under
emergency conditions. An unsatisfactory performance would

B. Previous Studies on Real-Time Operations of WDS and EPS
A few studies have reported on the real-time optimization
of WDS and EPS controls considering water–energy nexus
interactions. Fang and Chen [22] investigated the links between
WDS and energy systems for a city. Vakilifard et al. [23] pro-
vided a detailed literature review for techniques and approaches
for optimization of WDS considering water–energy nexus. An
optimization model for real-time operations of water–energy
nexus systems was presented in [24]. The model considered
optimization of pump schedules during power outages but did
not use a WDS simulation model. Oikonomou and Parvania [25]
presented a methodology for optimal coordinated controls of
WDS and EPS considering minimization of operational costs for
both the systems. The study does not consider WDS operations
for demand satisfaction under emergency conditions or simula-
tion of real-time WDS operations. The methodology presented
herein considers optimization–simulation of real-time opera-
tions of WDS under emergency conditions of limited power
and/or water availability interfaced with an OR computation
methodology for performance assessment.
II. OPTIMIZATION–SIMULATION APPROACH
The IOR methodology was applied using the results from
an optimization–simulation model application developed for
extended time-period WDS–EPS operations [1]. The power
system control optimization was implemented by a combination
of unit commitment (UC) for determining the ON/OFF status of
all the generators and a modiﬁed dc optimal power ﬂow (OPF)
for determining generator output set-points. The power system
network simulation for the operational time-frame simulations
was accomplished by the use of time-series power ﬂows. The
water system optimization–simulation was accomplished using
an extended period simulation coupled with a genetic algorithm
(GA) solution methodology for a control optimization model.
The water system and the power system were interfaced by al-
ternatively exchanging data between the power system and water

network simulations via a program control overlay developed in
python. Fig. 2 depicts an overview of this concept. The relevant
simulation data exchanged between the power system and wa-
ter network simulations included EPS network conﬁgurations,
pump electric power consumption as an identiﬁable component
of power system loads, and thermal generation cooling water
requirements, which were used as node demands within the
water system. The temporal dependence of the WDS on the
EPS is accomplished through the modiﬁcation of the operating
conditions in the EPS and vice versa. The spatial relationship
between the two systems is accounted for by placing those loads
or demands at the desired nodes or buses within the EPS and
WDS.
All the models for the optimization and control of the EPS
were solved via implementation with AMPL [26]. The complete
EPS network solution that obtained the net active and reactive
power injections as well as the voltage magnitudes and angles
at each bus was obtained using GE’s PSLF software [27].
The EPANET software [28] was used for the extended period
simulation of the WDS being analyzed, whereas a MATLAB
toolkit [29] was used to implement the genetic algorithm and to
interface the WDS simulation with the optimization procedure.
The objective of the optimization model (genetic algorithm)
used for optimal real-time WDS operations is to minimize
the difference between the requested demands (Dreq,z,t) and
satisﬁed demands (Dsat,z,t).
EPANET is a demand-driven simulation model that always
meets the demands irrespective of pressures at nodes in the
WDS. The solutions may have negative pressures at certain
nodes for operations where enough water is not pumped to meet
the water demands. For every time-step in the simulation, an
iterative process is conducted to avoid such conditions. The
iterative process consists of an optimization model solved using
a GA providing a new set of decision variables (including
satisﬁed demand multipliers and pump controls) to EPANET,
and a simulation is performed based on these new decision vari-
ables. A negative pressure penalty function with a large penalty
weight is used in the reduced optimization model to exclude

III. OR INDEX FUNCTIONS FOR WDS AND EPS
OR is a weighted metric that accounts for the pressure, pump
power, and demand satisfaction for WDS; voltage, transmission
line thermal limits, thermoelectric generation cooling water, and
load supply for the EPS. Index functions are deﬁned as indices
that allow for the current system conditions to be gauged in terms
of the selected operational parameters. The index functions for
both WDS and EPS are deﬁned in this section.
A. WDS: Pressure Function
Pressure bounds are an important performance assessment
criterion for WDS operations. During emergency conditions,
water demands maybesatisﬁedat lower pressures thangenerally
acceptable. Lower pressure bound violations represent a partial
failure of the WDS since prolonged lower pressure bound vio-
lations could be a public health safety hazard. Similarly, water
demands satisﬁed at pressures higher than the upper pressure
bounds may lead to failure of WDS components or household
faucets. Therefore, from an OR perspective, satisfaction of pres-
sure bounds is an important consideration for the demands that
can be satisﬁed.

The following quantities are used for calculating the OR index
function values:
1) bus voltage magnitude;

two systems. Each of these ﬁve quantities is evaluated for every
iteration (time period) within the simulation.
E. EPS: Voltage Index Function
Power system bus voltage magnitudes are maintained within a
small range of values during normal system operation. Because
electrical equipment is designed for operation at certain rated
voltages, therefore operation under conditions that differ greatly
from the rated speciﬁcations can have adverse effects. These
effects can include insulation breakdown for equipment that is
operated at voltage levels that are too high. The index function
relating the operational performance of the power system to bus
voltages is therefore deﬁned to have a value of 1 for voltages
within a small, nominal operating range. Outside of this range,
the resilience is assumed to decrease as the deviation away
from the nominal operating conditions is increased and reach
0 at some point. Following the description mentioned above
and using the values for thresholds, minimum, and maximum

transmission lines are designed to be operated under speciﬁc

will sag under increasing current (and therefore temperatures),
which can cause regulatory issues regarding minimum clearance
criteria as well as pose a potential hazard for objects beneath the
transmission lines.

given several different maximum apparent power operating lim-

H. EPS: Load Supplied (Overall) Index Function
The goal of power system operation is to satisfactorily supply
the requested electrical power demands of consumers while sat-
isfying constraints related to the quality of the power delivered.
A resilience measure along these lines is formulated in order to
quantify how well the system demands were met under extreme
system operating conditions. Based on consumer surveys [31],
consumers are willing to adjust their demands depending on
the perceived weather conditions. These consumers are also
observed to have different levels of willingness to change their
consumption based on the knowledge of how the water and
power systems interact as well as whether the curtailment of the
usage of water and energy is mandatory or not. For representa-

are decomposed into representative percentages for industrial,
commercial, and residential components. Within the residential
load component, it is further subdivided into single-family (sf)
and multifamily (mf) elements based on recent census data for
Arizona [32].

V. INFRASTRUCTURAL ROBUSTNESS METRICS
Robustness is the inherent capability of a system to resist
failures. This capability is an intrinsic property of a system based
on its structure and conﬁguration. This article considers metrics
for computation of the system level robustness based on system
structural conﬁguration. The robustness metrics for the WDS
and EPS are deﬁned in the following sections. The EPS metrics
are based upon those deﬁned in [33] and [34].

X. CONCLUSION
The performance of the WDS and EPS under conditions
of limited water availability and/or electric power outages is
an important consideration for the design and operation of
both systems, especially as the signiﬁcance of considering their

interdependent operation becomes critical in such scenarios.
This article presented a mathematical formulation for resilience
calculations of the two systems, which accounts for operational
and infrastructural considerations for each of the two systems
as well as identiﬁed interdependencies in their operation. The
new methodology for resilience computation, which has both of
these considerations, were named IOR.
The IOR computational method presented in this article pro-
vided for the inclusion of IOR calculations within a previously
developed extended time-period integrated EPS–WDS simula-
tion optimization framework. The IOR computation method-
ology (shown in Fig. 1) was applied to a regional level WDS
coupled with a corresponding EPS. Results of the application of
this computation methodology for the example systems show the
ability to capture system resilience when modeling short-term
and long-term electric power outages and drought scenarios.
This optimization–simulation framework used in conjunction
with the methodology for IOR computation would allow utili-
ties the ability to analyze real-time performance and facilitate
decision making. In addition, the IOR methodology could be
applied to forecasted, extreme, megadrought scenarios for more
accurate system simulations and anticipatory policy making.
The IOR computation methodology presented in this article
does not consider component failures in either system in the
computation procedure.
Because the resilience calculations for the test systems shown
are not computationally expensive (total calculation time of
1.37s for EPS + 1.90s for WDS = 3.27s total), it seems
that the IOR computation methodology could be used for
both postsimulation as well as real-time EPS–WDS perfor-
mance assessment. Therefore, future work will involve applica-
tion of the developed IOR computation methodology to larger
WDS and EPS test systems. The scalability of the proposed
metrics is currently being investigated for larger test systems
and will be incorporated in future research. Moving the ap-
plication toward larger, more realistic test systems also war-
rants the inclusions of a developed middleware architecture,
which emulates the supervisory control and data acquisition
(SCADA) communication that would be necessary for data
exchange between the two networks.
