A B S T R A C T
Water distribution systems (WDS) and power grids (PG) are critical infrastructure systems that are vital to all 
human activity. As such, their quality of service is of great importance for economic, environmental, and human 
welfare reasons. Although traditionally being analyzed separately, the two systems are interconnected and can 
mutually affect one another. In order to utilize the potential benefits that the two systems can produce for each 
other, their design and operation should be analyzed conjunctively. In this paper, a conjunctive optimal design 
approach for water and power networks is presented, with the objective of finding the dimensions of the systems’ 
facilities that will result in minimal overall costs, for both design and operation. The model is formulated and 
implemented on two example applications using an off-the-shelf nonlinear solver by MATLAB and compared to 
the optimal design of the independent WDS. A sensitivity analysis is performed to provide validity to the ob­
tained results. The conjunctive design is compared to the design of an independent WDS to emphasize the effect 
of including the PG in the optimization problem. Results show a clear link between the availability of renewable 
energy and sizing of WDS components. The design of the independent WDS leads to the violation of PG con­
straints, which are satisfied when including both systems under a single optimization model, demonstrating the 
importance of a holistic design approach.
1. Introduction
Water distribution systems (WDSs) are a collection of hydraulic el­
ements such as pumps, pipes, tanks, and valves, designed to convey 
water from sources to consumers. The behavior of the system is deter­
mined by physical laws of mass and energy conservation; water de­
mands; and its layout. The optimal design problem of WDS is the task of 
sizing certain elements in the system such that would satisfy its con­
straints, such as the supply of nodal demand, minimal pressure, and 
water quality standards, and result in an optimal objective value, 
traditionally set as minimal total construction and operation cost 
(Perelman and Ostfeld 2007).
Designing an optimal water distribution network presents a multi­
faceted challenge that requires careful consideration of hydraulic per­
formance, cost efficiency, and sustainability. Engineers must balance the 
complex interactions between pipe diameters, network topology, and 
operational constraints to ensure reliable water supply and efficient 
system operation. Factors such as pressure requirements, flow rates, 
water quality standards, and resilience to external disruptions further 
complicate the design process. Optimization involves minimizing both 
capital and operational costs while maximizing system reliability and 
resilience to future demands and uncertainties. The variables that are 
considered in such optimal design problems are traditionally pipe di­
ameters, tank volumes and pumping station capacities.
Due to the non-linear and non-convex relationship between flow and 
head in WDS, and the use of discrete diameter sizes, the problem is 
regarded as highly complex (Bragalli et al., 2011) and found to be NP- 
Hard (Yates et al., 1984). Linear programming techniques can be uti­
lized (Alperovits and Shamir 1977) to minimize costs while satisfying 
constraints on pipe diameters, flow rates, and pressure requirements. 
During the 1980′s and 1990′s several non-linear solution methods have 
been developed such as enumerations (Gessler 1985) and decomposition 
(Fujiwara and Khang 1990, Eiger et al. 1994). Over the past three de­
cades, a significant amount of research has concentrated on the devel­
opment and application of heuristic methods to solve the optimal design 
problem. Those methods can allow the efficient exploration of large 
solution spaces, which are tackled as part of the optimal design problem. 
Among those methods are genetic algorithms (Savi´c and Walters, 1997), 
simulated annealing (Zoppou and Reed 2001), shuffled from leap al­
gorithm (Eusuff and Lansey 2003), differential evolution algorithm

Power grids (PGs) are networks of transmission lines, substations, 
transformers, and distribution points designed to deliver electricity from 
power plants to consumers. Designing an optimal power grid is a com­
plex challenge that requires balancing numerous factors to ensure effi­
ciency, reliability, and sustainability. Engineers must consider 
geographical factors such as terrain and population distribution, as well 
as economic variables like cost-effectiveness and scalability. The grid 
must be robust enough to handle peak demands while also integrating 
renewable energy sources effectively to reduce environmental impact. 
Furthermore, technological advancements in energy storage and trans­
mission play a crucial role in enhancing grid resilience and stability. 
Achieving an optimal design involves navigating these multifaceted 
considerations to create a system that meets current and future energy 
needs efficiently and sustainably. Traditionally, decision variables for 
the power grid optimal design problem are generator capacities, sizes of 
energy storage facilities (Yang et al. 2020), transmission line sizes 
(Khalil and Ismail 2006), and the integration of renewable energy 
sources (Garni et al. 2018; Majidi et al. 2022). A similar problem which 
is often tackled in research is the problem of power grid expansion, 
dealing with the planning of an additional section of the grid (Kim and 
Hur 2021, You et al. 2016). These problems are deemed mathematically 
complex, due to the non-linear and non-convex nature of the power flow 
equations (Lavaei and Low 2010). Common methods used to overcome 
the computational challenges are linear and quadratic programming 
(Fortenbacher and Demiray 2019), second-order cone programming 
(Baradar et al. 2013), semidefinite programming (Molzahn et al. 2013), 
along with many other relaxation and approximation methods (Molzahn 
and Hiskens 2019).
The link between the systems has been studied from various per­
spectives (Hamiche et al. 2016), with most work linking WDSs and PGs 
concerned with their joint operation (Oikonomou and Parvania 2019; 
Takahashi et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2018). Hari et al. (2023) have consid­
ered a joint expansion problem, designing storage and generation fa­
cilities for the conjunctive system. In their work, they define the 
objective as maximizing the demand that is supplied by the system, 
rather than the traditional minimal cost objective. However, the 
network considered there is quite simplistic and does not consider loops, 
multiple water consumers, or multiple storage and pumping facilities, 
which can significantly complexify the problem. Yao et al. (2023) pro­
posed an optimal design problem of WDS under uncertainty, combining 
the uncertainty in water demand with uncertainty of solar power 
availability. A behind-the-meter (BTM) component was also designed as 
part of the work to support the supply of power to the WDS. The PG itself 
and its constraints were not considered. They found that while robust­
ness of the solution is improved, the design of a solar component did not 
affect the sizing of components inside the WDS. Yao et al. (2021) sug­
gested to optimally design water tanks in the system to enhance the 
flexibility of the power system and to improve water quality. In their 
work they do consider the PG’s structure and constraints, but the 
problem focuses on tanks rather than the design of the entire system, and 
without any decision variables on the PG side.
This paper proposes a conjunctive optimal design problem, 
combining the design of a WDS and a PG to explore ways in which the 
sizing of components in one system affects the other and the operational 
policies of both systems. On the WDS side, capacities of storage and 
pumping facilities are determined, and on the PG side – the capacity of a 
solar field is determined. This paper is aimed to provide two main 
contributions to existing literature. The first is the very presentation and 
mathematical formulation of the conjunctive design problem, which 
represents a relatively novel and more holistic approach to water re­
sources management, taking into consideration the explicit modelling of 
adjacent and interconnected systems (in this case, a power system), as 
opposed to the traditional approach which usually considers merely the 
WDS standpoint. Hopefully, this will spark a discussion about the joint 
examination of those two interconnected infrastructure systems. The 
second contribution lies in the analysis of the results, which clearly 
shows how those two systems depend on and even constrain one 
another. The joint analysis provides unique and often counterintuitive 
insights into the link between different components in those systems and 
proposes ways to design them together in a way that takes advantage of 
those links.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: section 2 includes a 
mathematical formulation of the conjunctive design problem; section 3
details the solution of two example applications, including sensitivity 
analyses; and section 4 includes the conclusions of the work. The work 
has the potential to help design better infrastructure in the future, which 
in turn will increase the systems’ efficiency, reduce operational costs, 
and environmental impact.
2. Problem formulation
The formulation of the conjunctive design problem is brought here, 
comprising all constraints and decision variables that form each of the 
two problems of WDS optimal design and PG optimal design.
2.1. Decision variables
Since a system’s operation is highly dependent on its design, the 
operational costs must be considered as part of the problem’s objective 
(as will be discussed further in the Objective function sub-section). 
Hence, the decision variables include also operational decisions and 
could be divided into two categories – design variables, and operational 
variables.

This formulation is novel in the sense that it allows formulating the 
problem as a non-linear program (NLP), instead of the well-known 
mixed-integer non-linear program (MINLP) formulation, as it is usu­

a pump is off, the result is a disconnection of a source from the network.

using variable speed pumps, modelling such a disconnection could be

off when pump flow is equal to zero. The pump rotation speed variable 
will then act as a free variable to adjust the head at the pump’s down­
stream node, virtually allowing the pump to function as a valve and 
regulate a virtual head gain along the pump.
The inclusion of variable speed pumps allows formulating the 
problem as a continuous non-linear problem and helps avoid the 
employment of binary variables, although the inclusion of regular 
pumps (i.e., single speed pumps) would significantly reduce the solution 
space on one hand, while limiting the flexibility of the WDS design and 
operation on the other hand, which is essential for presenting the ability 
to adjust the WDS operation to the operation of the PG in this particular 
work.

2.3. Objective function
Usually, the objective function of optimal WDS design problems is 
defined as the minimal cost of both operation and construction derived 
from the design. In this paper, the results of two different problems will 
be presented and compared: the first being an optimal design problem of 
an independent WDS, with its results imposed on the power grid as a 
given load; and the second being the conjunctive optimal design prob­
lem, when both systems are designed simultaneously. Here, both the 
objective function of the independent WDS design problem and the 
objective function of the conjunctive design problem will be presented. 
When formulating the independent design problem, energy tariffs were 
not considered for the operation of pumps. Those tariffs are usually the 
result of an optimization done by the PG operators. It was the authors’ 
goal to present a reasonable comparison between the solutions to the 
two design problems, and therefore the decision was made not to 
consider energy tariffs. This matter is addressed in more detail in the 
conclusion section. The objective function of the independent problem 
comprises of the cost of constructing all pumping stations and tanks in 
the system. Those costs can be calculated by the following expressions:

3. Example applications
The conjunctive optimal design problem was applied here to two 
illustrative examples – one relatively simple, and another slightly more 
complex – for the purpose of testing the effectiveness of the approach on 
different scales. In both examples, the same water and power demand 
patterns presented in Fig. 1a, along with the solar generation profile in 
Fig. 1b, were used. Tanks were assumed to have a constant cross-section 
area, and therefore the volume decision variable was replaced with the 
tank’s maximal level. An initial condition was set for the tanks to be full 
at the beginning of the system’s simulation. The model was coded using 
YALMIP optimization package through MATLAB, which is a MATLAB 
package that allows the straightforward formulation of optimization 
problems (YALMIP, 2023). Although the problem is quite complex, the 
examples presented below did not require any intervention in the so­
lution process of the development of a solution strategy, and they were 
simply solved using IPOPT local solver, with a 64-bit Intel i7 4-core CPU 
at 2.00 GHz and 8-GB RAM. The optimization horizon was set to equal 
24 h. However, since the problem is highly complex, timestep length was 
set to 3 h, resulting in a solution for 8 timesteps.
3.1. Illustrative example I
Example I comprise of a four-node WDS, with one tank located at 
node 4 and one pump, which is supplied with power by a three-bus 
power grid that contains one conventional generator that is located at 
bus 1. The system layout is presented in Fig. 2.
The WDS and power grid attributes are given below in Table 2.

generation curve presented in Fig. 1b to create the solar generation

the system to generate enough solar power required during off-peak 
hours, such as morning and evening.
As can be observed in Fig. 4b the independent optimal design pro­

minimal voltage bound constraints cannot be satisfied. This result 
highlights the importance of the conjunctive approach and advocates for 
the consideration of intertwined systems when designing a water dis­
tribution system.

3.2. Illustrative example II
Example II is slightly more complex compared to Example I. It 
comprises of a nine-node WDS with 11 pipes, two tanks located in nodes 
8 and 9, and two pumps placed on links 1 and 2. A 14-bus PG supplies 
the pumps with power, along with 6 other demand buses along the PG. 
Similar to example I, the PG includes one conventional generator located 
at bus 1, and a solar generation field to be constructed and connected to 
bus 2. The system layout is presented in Fig. 6. The existence of multiple 
pumping stations and multiple tanks allows higher flexibility in the 
design and operation, which significantly enlarges the solution space 
and complexifies the problem. Furthermore, this would allow the ex­
amination of trade-offs between different zones inside the WDS, and will 
enable to observe how each of them is affected by the power grid. On the 
PG side, more power consumers and transmission lines result in a more 
complex optimal power flow problem, which would also influence the 
complexity of the conjunctive problem and help gain meaningful 
insights into the design of the joint system.
The attributes of the WDS and the PG are given in Table 5.
In this example, the 24-hour optimization was divided into 3-hour 
long timesteps (meaning – a total of 8 timesteps), due to the 
complexity and difficulty of achieving a solution for a 24-timestep long 
optimization. Since the focus of this study is the optimal design of the 
two systems, the operational decisions can be grossly estimated with 
longer time steps that relax the computational challenge.

3.2.2. Sensitivity analysis
A more limited sensitivity analysis was performed to gain a slightly 
deeper insight into the interaction between the different components in 
this system. The examined scenarios are detailed in Table 7.
The results of the sensitivity analysis are shown in Fig. 9. *BR – base 
run.
Scenarios 1 and 2 examined the solution’s sensitivity to solar field 
construction cost. When solar field construction cost is reduced, we do 
not observe a significant change in results. When the cost increases, 
however, we see a reduction in the size of tank 8; an increase in size of 
tank 9; a reduction in pumping capacity of pump 1; and an increase in 
capacity of pump 2. This result validates the analysis that was previously 
presented, about the effects the different components have on the 
operation, and their relationship with the availability of sustainable 
power. We can also notice a 70-kW reduction in solar capacity, as a 
result of increased construction cost. The third scenario examines a 
different configuration of the system, where pump 2 is connected 
directly to bus 2, where the solar field is located. We obtained the same 
results as we did in scenario 2 but are now able to operate with a much 
smaller solar field. This result demonstrates the magnitude of the in­
fluence that both the voltage constraints and the loss of power along 
transmission lines have on the optimal solution.
4. Conclusion
This paper presents the novel concept of conjunctively optimizing 
the design of a water distribution system and a power grid. The opti­
mization problem is formulated such that the decision variables are 
storage and pumping capacities on the WDS side, and solar generation 
capacity on the power grid side. The conjunctive problem comprises of 
all constraints forming each of the two independent design problems, 
and the two systems are linked through the WDS’s pump power demand.
The concept is applied to two illustrative examples. The results of the 
conjunctive optimal design problems are compared to the results of the 
optimal design of the independent WDS, and the impact of the 
connection of the power grid to the WDS optimal design problem is 
noticeable. The inclusion of voltage constraints in the problem reveals 
the inability of the power grid to operate under the independent WDS 
optimal design requirements and forces the connection of the grid to a 
sustainable power source. The availability of a sustainable power source 
in the system influences the design of storage and pumping components 
in the WDS, favoring the upgrading of pumping stations at the expense 
of storage volume – a somewhat counterintuitive result that advocates 
for the importance of the conjunctive approach. The examination of two 
illustrative examples casts light on the complexity of the problem and 
the inner mechanisms that are at play. Analyzing the results of example I

could be considered a simple task, since the system includes only one 
tank and one pump, but as the system is of a slightly larger scale the 
relationship between components can become quite complex, even 
when looking at a relatively small system such as the one brought as 
example II. The different components respond differently to the 
connection of a sustainable source to the system, based on their adja­
cency to demand nodes and their connections to the power grid. The PG 
configuration favors the employment pumps located in the proximity of 
generation epicenters. Storage facility volumes inside the WDS are in 
turn affected by this based on their proximity to pumps, as they must 
operate in coordination with pump head gains. Regarding the cost, no 
clear trend was observed, as the conjunctive approach is able to reduce 
cost for example I but is not able to do the same for example II. As power 
generation is the most dominant component in the objective, it is 
evident that the dominance of the renewable energy source inside the 
power grid is responsible for the ability to reduce the total cost. 
Approximating the dominance of a renewable energy source in the PG is 
therefore a step that should be taken before the application of this 
conjunctive model, in order to assess whether total cost could be 
reduced.
A sensitivity analysis that was performed helped establish and vali­
date the obtained results, and to also gain novel insights into the opti­
mality of the obtained solution. It was observed that pipe diameters, 
along with transmission lines, could be very significant decision vari­
ables in this problem, and their inclusion could provide more deep and 
interesting insight.
An issue that required the authors’ attention is the basis for com­
parison between the independent design and the conjunctive design. 
Usually, the operation of WDS takes into consideration energy tariffs for 
pumps. Those tariffs are imposed on the WDS by the PG operators. Since 
the design and operation of the PG are also being solved as part of the 
conjunctive approach, the incorporation of those energy tariffs in the 
conjunctive problem would defeat the purpose of this work – here, the 
model is intended to be solved all at once, simultaneously, rather than in 
stages (optimizing the design and operation of the PG, obtaining energy 
tariffs for the WDS, and then optimizing the operation of the WDS with 
respect to the obtained tariffs), in order to utilize the interdependency 
between the systems.. In this work, we resolved this matter by assuming 
that a single operator oversees the operation of both the WDS and the 
PG. This assumption allows to neglect pump energy tariffs and only 
consider power generation costs from the standpoint of the PG. Although 
it is rarely the case in reality, it does allow us to present a relatively 
beneficial comparison in this work. Another option could be performing 
a calculation of energy tariffs in a dynamic way, during the optimiza­
tion. This option is much more complex. It requires a focus on the 
methods that are used for the determination of energy tariffs by PG 
operators and regarded by the authors as out of the scope of this paper. 
That said, the incorporation of energy tariffs does require more attention 
and poses a significant challenge that could be the focus for future work.
The optimization problem presented here is solved by IPOPT, an off- 
the-shelf non-linear solver. As the networks get more complex, the 
process of obtaining a solution this way would get more difficult, and at 
some point, perhaps impractical. Nonetheless, applying this conjunctive 
approach to case studies of a larger scale will help better understand the 
magnitude of their interdependency on optimal design of such systems, 
and is imperative for advancing the field of infrastructure operation and 
management. An additional work that’s currently being carried out is 
the development of a tailored solution method, that would allow the 
application of the conjunctive approach to larger case studies, and will 
shed light on new insights with respect to the scale of the problem.
As mentioned throughout this manuscript, the work presented here 
constitutes a first step in the direction of a more holistic approach for 
water distribution systems analysis, specifically with a focus on their 
relationship with the power grid. There are several suggestions that 
could be of use for future work that would expand the idea that was 
brought here. Widening the range of decision variables, to include pipe 
diameters, transmission line materials and even batteries for power 
storage in the power grid side would significantly complexify the 
problem, and is likely to generate rich insight into the interdependencies 
between the systems. The addition of decision variables is also likely to 
require more sophisticated and computationally complex solution 
approach, such as branch and bound, decomposition techniques like 
outer approximation (OA), heuristics or sequential quadratic program­
ming (SQP).Testing the model on a much larger scale systems is an 
interesting direction as well, which will require a closer look at the 
problem formulation in order to develop new and tailored solution 
methods. The development of such methods could be another research 
direction in and of itself, to allow for a more suitable optimization 
approach and provide robustness to the optimization process. In pur­
suing any of these directions, different objectives could be considered 
such as robustness, resilience or even water quality standards. A multi- 
objective problem could be tackled, and the competition between those 
different objective functions could be presented as a pareto front, to 
demonstrate the limitations of the conjunctive approach. Different as­
pects of uncertainty could also be examined, using different methods to 
solve the operation part of the problem in a more realistic way, such as 
robust optimization, folding horizon and predictive control models.
