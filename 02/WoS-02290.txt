A B S T R A C T  
Critical cyber-physical infrastructure networks such as electric power and water networks form the backbone of a 
functional society as these networks provide the main resources for economic productivity, healthy communities, 
and general day-to-day operations. However, (i) these infrastructure networks have become more interdependent 
on each other in terms of functionality, and (ii) community networks become more dependent on these critical 
infrastructures in terms of existence. Thus, studying the entirety of their resilience process—both vulnerability 
and recoverability against a disruption—is of importance, particularly analyzing how investing (i) before to 
strengthen components versus (ii) afterward to restore disrupted components can affect the resilience of infra­
structure networks in terms of system performance and time. This paper takes a first step toward analyzing the 
tradeoff between reducing vulnerability and enhancing recoverability in interdependent infrastructure networks 
with multiple time and budget allocations, accounting for not only the performance of the physical networks but 
also the potentially socially vulnerable communities that rely upon them. The proposed model (i) maximizes a 
measure of the resilience of the interdependent infrastructure networks, while it (ii) minimizes the total cost 
associated with the pre- and post-event resource allocation. The model is illustrated with networks in Shelby 
County, TN, USA.   
1. Introduction 
Critical infrastructure networks have been defined as networks of 
“independent, mostly privately-owned, man-made systems and pro­
cesses that function collaboratively and synergistically to produce and 
distribute a continuous flow of essential goods and services” [1], a 
definition that still holds today. These infrastructure networks are 
regularly subject to disruption and system failure. Since they are pri­
mary providers of human needs and enable a high quality of life, 
ensuring that networks are resilient against disruption is important. 
Recently, the importance of forming physically interdependent infra­
structure networks that are resilient against an eventual disruption is 
also emphasized by the Infrastructure Security Partnership [2] and ex­
ecutive leadership [3], where resilient infrastructures are defined as 
those that “should prepare for, prevent, protect against, respond, or 
mitigate any anticipated or unexpected significant threat or event” and 
those that are able to “rapidly recover and reconstitute critical assets, 
operations, and services with minimum damage and disruption.” 
However, due to the interdependent nature of these infrastructure 
networks [4], they have become more vulnerable to disruptions [5,6], 
not to mention the expanding expectations for their operation. Hence, 
considering the interdependencies between infrastructure networks is 
especially important for conducting more realistic performance analysis, 
pre-event preparedness investments, and post-event restoration sched­
uling investments [7]. Further, not taking interdependencies into ac­
count could lead to the inefficient assignment of available preparedness 
and restoration resources (i.e., hardening resources, number of work 
crews) or to additional inoperability in the system of networks [8]. 
Further, since modern societies are so heavily dependent on the 
continuous and proper operation of critical infrastructure networks [9] 
as illustrated in Fig. 1, their vulnerability leads to the vulnerability of 
surrounding communities. This responsive effect of critical infrastruc­
ture disruptions over the dependent society is also stated by the National 
Academies of Science [10], which suggested that a means “to reduce the 
impacts of disasters on the nation and its communities is to invest in 
enhancing resilience.” The resilience of a community is shaped by its 
ability to cope with disruptions successfully and stand stable in all 
economic, social, and environmental aspects through planning recovery

For all the reasons above, the need to plan for resilient interdepen­
dent infrastructure networks – from both pre- and post-disruption point- 
of-view – motivates this work. We aim to analyze the tradeoff between 
investing beforehand to strengthen the infrastructure components 
versus investing after the fact to allocate restoration resources to dis­
rupted components. We compare the time and budget requirements of 
strengthening versus restoration activities to enhance the resilience of 
critical infrastructure networks in terms of both time and performance 
[13]. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study for resilient 
interdependent infrastructure networks that assesses the tradeoff be­
tween pre- and post-disruption investments in terms of cost and time, 
while accounting for the vulnerability of the community being served. 
Our method explicitly aims to (i) minimize the total cost of pre- and 
post-disruption activities, and (ii) maximize the resilience of the inter­
dependent infrastructure networks while inherent social vulnerabilities 
of the affected community are considered. 
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: a related work in 
Section 2 is followed by our proposed methodology with the mathe­
matical formulation in Section 3. Later, the illustrative example of our 
proposed approach with the data of interdependent infrastructure net­
works in Shelby County, TN, is represented in Section 4. Finally, 
concluding remarks and future work are presented in Section 5. 
2. Related work 
In this section, we conduct a literature review in line with our scope, 
on infrastructure network and community resilience studies with a focus 
on vulnerability, recoverability, and their tradeoff. We also summarize 
studies that are specifically concerned with power and water infra­
structure networks. Lastly, we address the contribution of our novel 
approach in detail. 
2.1. Infrastructure network resilience 
The resilience of infrastructure networks is a well-studied topic, both 
from network science [14] and engineering perspectives [15]. 
Infrastructure resilience is often defined as ‘the ability of an infrastruc­
ture system to withstand, adapt to, and recover from a disruption’ [16]. 
Multiple approaches are introduced to model and measure the resilience 
of a system, such as qualitative [17] and quantitative [18] analyses. 
Network resilience can be described along two primary dimensions: 
vulnerability and recoverability [19]. Network vulnerability is defined 
as “the magnitude of damage in network performance due to a disrup­
tive event” [20], whereas recoverability is defined as “the speed at 
which the network reaches to a desired performance level” [21]. 
In line with the above network vulnerability and recoverability 
definitions, we adopt the below infrastructure network resilience para­
digm [19,22]. Here, network resilience is quantified as the ratio of the 
network performance recovery over the network performance loss at a 
given time, Я(t⃒⃒ej) = Recovery(t)/Loss(td ). As in Fig. 2, ej represents the 
disruptive event, and network performance is illustrated in with φ(t), 
which could represent network flow or many other performance 
measures. 
In this resilience paradigm, both recovery and loss are measured by 
the change in network performance, φ(t), over time. In a less resilient 
network, either recovered performance would be lower (lower φ(tf) −
φ(td)) or the losses would be more substantial (higher φ(t0) −φ(td)) after 
a disruption. Thus, Я(t⃒⃒ej) would be lower for a less resilient network. On 
the contrary, in a more resilient network, the converse would be true and 
thus Я(t⃒⃒ej) would be higher. Once the system loss is completely 
recovered, the network would be back to its original performance such 
that Я(t⃒⃒ej) = 1. 
2.2. Network vulnerability and preparedness planning 
In the literature, infrastructure network vulnerability studies mainly 
emphasize the risk assessment of natural and man-made attacks [23] or 
the effects of potential cascading failures [24]. Also, numerous meth­
odologies for determining the criticality of infrastructure network 
components are proposed in vulnerability studies [25]. 
For preparedness planning of power and water networks, several 
works have: developed a vulnerability evaluation model of water supply 
systems [26], proposed an adaptive robust framework to optimize the 
pre-hazard resilience enhancement plans for interdependent water and 
power infrastructures [27], modeled a resilient distribution network 
planning problem to coordinate the hardening and resource allocation 
of power grids with the objective of minimizing the system damage [28], 
and lastly formulated a tri-level defender–attacker–defender model to 
enhance the resilience of water and power systems by protecting weak 
components and building new components to increase redundancy [29]. 
The practical pre-disruption preparedness investments for power 
networks (considered in this study) could include replacing existing 
transformers with higher interchangeability-having transformers, 
hardening transmission lines, towers, circuit breakers, and servers, and 
restricting the physical accessibility to components by encapsulating 
them in protected zones [30]. Similarly, for water networks some of 
these pre-event investments could be hardening water facilities against 
disruptions, segregating water facilities to restrict access, and building 
redundant water pipes [31]. 
In this study, rather than differentiating among specific preparedness 
tasks, we focus on the identification of the infrastructure components 
that would be strengthened before a potential disruption. Thus, the aim 
of the preparedness investments is pinpointing and strengthening the 
components that would reduce the overall system vulnerability the 
most. Also, during the preparedness process, the total cost associated 
with strengthening the nodes and links is minimized.

The performance recoverability of infrastructure networks is a 
commonly studied topic in the literature [32,33]. More specific to power

rupted critical infrastructures in the presence of unobserved and

mixed-integer program model to incorporate all nine of the identified 
interdependency subtypes. Lastly, Karamouz et al. [36] proposed a 
scheme for financial resource allocation to waste water treatment plants 
with the goal of improving resilience and reliability. 
The practical post-disruption restoration investments for both power 
and water networks (considered in this study) could include hiring work 
crews to repair the disrupted components after an event. The restoration 
process for power networks generally consists of the initial inspection, 
damage assessment, repair, and reenergizing actions [37]. For water 
distribution networks, the restoration activities could include rehabili­
tating deteriorated pipes, replacing brittle pipes, and adding new pipes 
[31]. 
In this study, we only focus on assigning work crews with a specific 
order to schedule the restoration of disrupted infrastructure network 
components after an event. Thus, the aim of the restoration investments 
is to enhance the disrupted network performance afterward. Also, dur­
ing the restoration process, the total cost associated with network flow, 
unmet demand, disrupted node and link restoration are minimized. 
2.4. Tradeoff between pre- and post-disruption planning 
There is substantial literature related to pre- and post-disaster 
planning in the context of humanitarian supply chains and relief dis­
tribution [38,39]. For example, Sherali et al. [40] proposed a nonlinear 
mixed-integer programming model for pre-disruption shelter allocation 
which would minimize the post-disruption congestion-based evacuation 
time. Balcik and Beamon [41] developed a pre-disruption distribution 
center number and location optimization model which would ensure 
that the post-disruption relief demand is met. Abazari et al. [42] 
developed a multi-objective mixed-integer non-linear programming 
with uncertain parameters considering different type of relief items, 
relief centers, demand points, and transportation methods for pre- and 
post-disaster planning. Lastly, He and Zhuang [43] formulated a 
two-stage dynamic programming model to balance pre-disruption pre­
paredness and post-disruption relief investments. More recently, Alem 
et al. [44] developed an optimization model to address location and 
capacity planning for prepositioning of aid, and relief distribution de­
cisions with the inclusion of social vulnerability. 
However, these previous studies do not aim to enhance the resilience 
of interdependent infrastructures through both pre- and post-disruption 
investments. Thus, to the best of our knowledge, there are only a few 
models that are more aligned with our scope to analyze the tradeoff 
between pre- and post-disruption investments specifically for resilient 
interdependent infrastructure networks. 
2.5. Social vulnerability 
There exist multiple approaches to quantify the inherent vulnera­
bility levels of the communities that are served by critical infrastructure 
networks. The proposed approaches account for the socio-economic 
characteristics of the served community [45] as they are important in­
dicators from evacuation compliance during an event to successful 
long-term recovery after one [46]. The common perspective of these 
approaches is the fact that socially vulnerable communities are more 
likely to suffer in a disaster and less likely to recover after [46]. 
Social vulnerability is defined as the set of characteristics of a group 
that influences their capacity to anticipate, cope with, resist, and recover 
from the impact of a hazard [47]. Some of the most well-known social 
vulnerability measures that are suitable for any geographic community 
are the Social Vulnerability Index for Disaster Management (SVI) [48], 
the Baseline Resilience Indicators for Communities (BRIC) [49], and the 
Community Resilience Index [50]. All these indices are derived from the 
Social Vulnerability Index (SoVI) [51], the state-of-the-art measure to 
compute a vulnerability score for each community based on the statis­
tical distribution of the predetermined sub-groups of socio-economic 
characteristics. Cutter et al. [51] suggest that these sub-groups repre­
sent the more fragile parts of the population, thus it is assumed that 
vulnerable sub-groups would require more and prior recovery in­
vestments, governmental support, and relief initiatives to ensure the 
desired level of resilience after a disruption and to satisfy their daily 
needs. 
In our study, we adopt the SoVI-Lite algorithm [52] to quantify social 
vulnerability. SoVI-Lite is another social vulnerability index that is 
based on the widely used SoVI score. SoVI-Lite also quantifies social 
vulnerability according to the statistical distribution of predetermined 
socio-economic sub-groups in the population. However, we choose to 
adopt SoVI-Lite (rather than other indices) since it is (i) more 
data-efficient and easier to apply, (ii) transparent and understandable by 
practitioners, and finally (iv) reasonably representative of the results 
generated from the original SoVI [52].

2.6. Our contribution 
The tradeoff between pre- and post-disruption investments for 
interdependent infrastructure network resilience has not been addressed 
adequately in the literature [53]. So far, pre- and post-disruption in­
vestments for interdependent infrastructure networks are generally 
evaluated separately. However, we propose a novel interdependent 
infrastructure network resilience-driven multi-objective optimization 
model to analyze the tradeoff between pre-disruption strengthening 
activities and post-disruption restoration activities while accounting for 
community resilience. The proposed approach in this study, that is built 
upon our previous studies, (i) maximizes a measure of the overall 
resilience of interdependent infrastructure networks in terms of system 
performance and time, (ii) minimizes the total cost associated with both 
strengthening and restoration activities, and finally (iii) prioritizes the 
preparedness and restoration investments of infrastructure network 
components according to a social vulnerability index. 
More aligned with our work, Lobban et al. [31] provided a 
decision-making framework to determine the allocation of defensive 
resources that accounts for the social vulnerability of the served com­
munity, and Karakoc et al. [54] proposed the restoration planning of 
critical interdependent infrastructure networks after a possible disrup­
tive event by mainly emphasizing on the vulnerability indices of inter­
acting society. These studies focus only on a single dimension of 
resilience, either vulnerability or recoverability. Yet, considering the 
entirety of the resilience process is critical [55]. More recently, 
Ghorbani-Renani et al. [56] balance vulnerability and recoverability 
before and after a disruption for interdependent infrastructure networks 
through a tri-level protection-interdiction-restoration problem. How­
ever, the inherent social vulnerability of the served community is not 
accounted for in this study, and the tradeoff among protection and 
restoration investments across multiple budget and time limitations is 
not formalized. Thus, the methodology proposed in this work is a natural 
next step that would close the gaps among these studies. 
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on interdependent 
infrastructure networks that captures the entirety of the resilience pro­
cess (both vulnerability and recoverability) with (i) analyzing the 
tradeoff between pre- and post-disruption activities through multiple 
time and budget resources, and finally (ii) considering the social 
vulnerability of the served community. 
3. Proposed approach 
In this study, the tradeoff between pre- and post-disruption in­
vestments for interdependent infrastructure networks is analyzed 
through our extended resilience-driven multi-objective mixed-integer 
programming model [54]. Under each desired level of system resil­
ience and budget constraint, our model provides a unique optimum 
solution for the preparedness and restoration activities on system com­
ponents. The research questions “how much strengthening investments 
should be assigned and to which components before a disruption”, and 
“how to order the restoration of each component after a disruption” are 
addressed through our decision variables. Further, system performance 
is optimized with an emphasis on social vulnerability through our 
model. Thus, disrupted components that serve more socially vulnerable 
communities are (i) strengthened beforehand to reduce vulnerability, 
and (ii) scheduled earlier during the restoration activities. This priori­
tization is also common for physically interdependent components 
among infrastructure networks. To enhance the overall system 
performance more efficiently, interdependent components are also 
assigned for hardening and restoration earlier. 
Our novel approach enables better decision-making for resource 
allocation (i.e., work crews, investment budget, and time) on interde­
pendent infrastructure networks, as well as on surrounding commu­
nities. By implementing our novel methodology on various 
interdependent infrastructure networks, decision-makers can obtain a 
better understanding of the overall system behavior. In the future, more 
effective system resilience strategies can be introduced based on past 
and simulation data. Thus, our tradeoff analysis between pre- and post- 
disruption investments can be useful to derive more comprehensive 
managerial insights on the efficient use of available resources, interde­
pendent infrastructure system design, and community response. 
A more detailed explanation and formulation of the model assump­
tions, notation, objectives, and constraints are listed below. 
3.1. Model assumptions 
The first set of assumptions relates to the model: (i) infrastructure 
networks are formed by components (i.e. nodes and links), (ii) compo­
nents of each network are subject to complete or partial disruption, (iii) 
components could be partially or fully restored, (iv) components can be 
assigned to multiple work crews during different time periods for re­
covery, (v) partial dependencies and partial functionalities are allowed 
through the system, and (vi) due to physical interdependencies, a 
dependent node can be operational if another specific node in another 
network is also operational. These are generally more relaxed assump­
tions (i.e., allowing for partial rather than binary disruptions, restora­
tions, mitigations, functionalities, and dependencies). Hence, a more 
efficient preparedness and restoration process for the interdependent 
infrastructure networks is provided. Also, full system performance can 
be achieved faster with less cost. 
The second set of assumptions relates to the parameters in our 
approach: for each component there exists a different, but constant and 
known (i) restoration rate and cost through the restoration process, (ii) 
supply, demand, and flow capacities, (iii) unitary unmet demand pen­
alty cost, and (iv) strengthening cost through the preparedness process. 
Also, the number of available work crews through the restoration pro­
cess of each network is known and fixed. These are notably more relaxed 
assumptions regarding model parameters, as they do not include any 
uncertainties within the rates, capacities, and costs. However, formu­
lating a deterministic interdependent infrastructure network resilience 
model for the tradeoff analysis between pre- and post-disruption in­
vestments is a natural first step to close the current gap in the literature.

conflicting goals: (i) maximizing the resilience of the overall system of 
interdependent infrastructure networks through the preparedness and 
restoration horizon, and (ii) minimizing the total cost of the mitigation 
investments and recovery activities from the community resilience 
perspective.

3.5. Tradeoff analysis 
Our novel approach is a multi-objective optimization model due to 
the contradicting nature of cost minimization and resilience maximi­
zation objectives. To maximize resilience, the performance of the 
interdependent infrastructure networks must be enhanced by pre- and 
post-disruption investments. Thus, some amount of expense is required. 
On the contrary, to minimize cost, no expenses should be made to the

these contradicting objectives simultaneously, a multi-criteria decision- 
making technique must be implemented [59].

4.1. Measuring social vulnerability in Shelby County, TN 
In this study, we adopt the SoVI-Lite algorithm [52] to account for 
the various social vulnerability indices of the demand nodes in the two 
networks. The appropriate social vulnerability variables that are avail­
able in the block-group level for Shelby County, TN are listed in Table 5, 
which account for most of the 11-factor groups in Table 1. 
To calculate the social vulnerability indices by SoVI-Lite algorithm 
and assign these indices to demand nodes in the water and power net­
works, the following steps are used: (i) calculate the population or 
household percentage for each social vulnerability variable in Table 5, 
(ii) normalize these percentages into z-scores for each variable, (iii) sum 
the z-scores of the social vulnerability variables for each block-group, 
and finally (iv) scale the block-group level social vulnerability index 
sums such that they range between 0 and 1. Thus, 0 stands for the least 
socially vulnerable and 1 stands for the most socially vulnerable com­
munity. Then, we assign the average block-group level social vulnera­
bility score to each demand node at water and power networks 
according to their coverage area. The distribution of the block-group 
social vulnerability indices among the demand node coverage areas is 
represented in Fig. 4. Similarly, the final social vulnerability scores of 
the demand nodes, Vk
i , are illustrated in Fig. 5.

5. Concluding remarks 
Critical infrastructures are the backbone of modern societies as they 
are utilized regularly to ensure the existence and functionality of daily 
activities. However, due to the continuous developments in technology 
and the globalization of societies, infrastructure networks become more 
interdependent in terms of operability. Hence, these networks become 
more vulnerable to external stressors since they are subject to chain 
reactions of dysfunctionality. 
To ensure secure infrastructure systems that are resilient against 
disruptions, many studies have separately considered infrastructure 
preparedness and restoration problems. However, considering the en­
tirety of interdependent infrastructure network resilience – both the 
vulnerability and recoverability aspects – become an important topic 
[62]. In addition, analyzing the tradeoff between the pre- and post-event 
investments across multiple budget and performance constraints to 
enhance the resilience of interdependent infrastructure networks is a 
relatively unexplored subject that has room for important contributions. 
In this study, we propose a multi-objective mixed-integer program­
ming method that allows the co-existence of pre- and post-event activ­
ities to enhance the resilience of physically interdependent 
infrastructure networks while accounting for the inherent vulnerability 
of the served community. The developed model aims to understand the 
relationship between the pre, and post-disruption activities based on the 
desired level of resilience and budgeting constraints. The pre-event ac­
tivities for strengthening the components prior to the occurrence of an 
event reduce their vulnerability levels. In case of a disruption, these 
hardened components would function with higher capacities. Hence, the 
required post-disruption resources for recovery, such as the number of 
available work crews and recovery time horizon for the restoration ac­
tivities, would also be reduced without making any sacrifices from the 
network performance and resilience levels. Furthermore, the proposed 
approach allows decision-makers to arrange the parameters of pre- and 
post-event resources so that the desired level of system resilience could 
be achieved in various scenarios of time and money based on their 
mindset. 
In addition to considering the physical interdependencies between 
critical infrastructures and representing the tradeoff between the pre- 
and post-disruption activities, the proposed mathematical model ac­
counts for community resilience. The importance of integrating com­
munity resilience with physical infrastructure systems has also gained 
momentum [63]. In this study, network resilience is integrated with the 
geographically distributed social vulnerability index that quantifies 
behavioral responses and social expectations against external stressors 
based on the various demographics in the society. Hence, the network 
performance is weighted with social vulnerability indices of the com­
ponents while the system resilience is measured. Therefore, the pre­
paredness and restoration activities for the disrupted components that 
represent more socially vulnerable regions would be prioritized. 
5.1. Future work 
In its current form, our study takes uncertainties within the system of 
interdependent infrastructure networks into account indirectly. Even 
though we introduce a deterministic model as a first step to close the gap 
in the interdependent infrastructure network resilience literature, our 
case study is conducted with multiple disruption scenarios rather than a 
single one. The data for these disruption scenarios are adopted from a 
previous simulation study which probabilistically determined each 
disruption scenario (i.e., which components were to be disrupted) with 
varying parameter values (i.e., the functionality of each component). We 
also include a sensitivity analysis to capture how changes in the unit cost 
of preparedness activities affect the tradeoff between pre- and post- 
disruption investments. Therefore, in our case study uncertainties 
within the disruption scenarios and parameter values are addressed to a 
certain degree. 
However, we anticipate that the obvious future work will center on 
addressing uncertainties that would occur in system parameters by 
extending the modeling. Such uncertainties could be within the (i) 
strengthening and restoration rates, (ii) supply, demand, and flow ca­
pacities, (iii) unmet demand penalty costs, strengthening activity costs, 
restoration activity costs, and finally (iv) the number of available work 
crews at each time period. Additionally, with consideration of risk, 
capturing the disruption probability of each component could be 
introduced as an extension of our currently proposed methodology. 
Further, the possibility of cascading failures where pre-investment may 
be of more extreme importance to prevent cascades that require more 
restoration resources could be addressed. Thus, we plan to explore sto­
chastic approaches and robust optimization models in the future to 
enhance the practicality and realism of our tradeoff analysis.

infrastructure networks data, we could implement it on more complex
