The treatment, monitoring and distribution of drinking water is an integral component of critical national infrastructure and
therefore places continually increasing demands on Water Distribution Networks (WDNs). This domain and its sub-sectors
face several major problems, namely climate change and drought-induced rises in water consumption from surface and
underground reservoirs, in addition to the existence of signiicant water leaks during transmission to end users. These
problems can be addressed by deploying Internet of Things (IoT) systems and smart distribution grids to improve the eiciency
and safety of water distribution and to easily detect leaks or unauthorised consumption. This type of smart grid is referred
to as Smart Water-IoT (SW-IoT), a novel, comprehensive water management concept. This review article discusses the
application of IoT components and artiicial intelligence (AI) in ive basic categories (agriculture, water treatment, security,
water distribution networks and wastewater). Relevant legislation in the EU, USA, Canada, Australia, China, Japan and India
is also reviewed. In this context, the mandatory implementation of smart remote data reading solutions into the critical
infrastructure of EU member states is outlined to highlight the importance of responsible water handling. The article provides
a detailed analysis of the current research in SW-IoT and deines the main research challenges for future investigation.

Introduction
Water is essential to life, and for this reason, the treatment, monitoring, and distribution of drinking water
forms a part of critical national infrastructure which is indispensable to economic and even social development.
Continuous urbanisation and migration of populations to large agglomerations place enormous demands on Water
Distribution Networks (WDNs). According to Eurostat [1], approximately 40 % of the EU’s population currently
live in large cities, while a United Nations report suggests that by 2050, nearly 84 % of the population will live in
urban environments (cities and towns/suburbs) [2]. Previously published data indicate that the requirements for
transmitting, storing, and treating potable water will change fundamentally. Eurostat [3] reports that there is no
current increase in water consumption per resident in the European Union (EU); however, climate change and
the increasing incidence of drought on the continent have led to greater exploitation of surface and underground
reservoirs. In this context and in relation to water handling procedures, the problem of leaks and the loss of
signiicant volumes of water during the supply to the end user must be corrected. The average leakage rates vary
signiicantly between EU countries and depend largely on the condition of the infrastructure and the type of
technologies applied. European Federation of National Associations of Water Services (EurEau) [4] states that the
average volume of water leaked in the EU during transmission amounts to approximately 25 %, the lowest losses
reported from Germany (approximately 5 %) and the highest in Bulgaria (approx. 60 %). These statistics can be
juxtaposed with the fact that roughly 95 % of the EU population has access to potable water. These losses arise
mainly from unauthorized consumption, metering inaccuracies, leakage during transmission or at the distribution
mains, leakage and overlow at utility storage tanks, and leakage at service connections up to the measurement
point.
Already today, the Smart City concept is supporting the large scale deployment of Internet-of-Things (IoT)
systems into almost all the functional areas and subdomains that will be embedded in or controlled in cities of
the future. This concept is becoming a standard for building smart distribution networks [5, 6], in which the goal
is not only to improve the eiciency of resource distribution, but also to increase security and detect possible
leaks or unauthorized consumption from distribution systems. A shift can be seen from conventional approaches
towards smart IoT technologies for collecting grid data and Artiicial Intelligence (AI) tools for evaluating and
detecting possible water leaks. The huge growth of these technologies has enabled a new branch of the IoT to
emerge, a subdomain that could be referred to as the Smart Water Internet of Things (SW-IoT).

in the current article are summarised in Table 1. The table lists details of the authors and main topics discussed
and compares the studies to the work explored here. Most of the studies focused on concrete applications of the

By contrast, the current review investigates the deployment of IoT systems and use of AI in ive basic water

IoT-enabled Smart Water Systems with integrated AI: SW-IoT
The adoption of advanced technologies in water management has become imperative in response to the growing
global demand for water. This demand is primarily driven by dynamic climatic conditions, population growth,
urbanisation, and increasing water requirements from the agricultural and industrial sectors.
IoT-enabled Smart Water Systems, integrated with artiicial intelligence (referred to in this study as SW-IoT),
ofer a viable solution to address these escalating consumption pressures by enabling more sustainable and
eicient management of this critical resource, thereby reducing unnecessary water waste. Such innovations are
vital for progressing toward sustainable water resource management.
The application of IoT technology is especially beneicial for monitoring essential parameters within water
supply networks, including water quality, consumption levels, and network pressure. Leveraging this data,
predictive maintenance can be implemented to mitigate water losses resulting from system malfunctions, and
responses to emergency situations can be optimized. [7, 19, 20]
AI in water management facilitates advanced real-time data analysis and enables the prediction of potential
issues within water supply networks. Machine Learning (ML) algorithms, trained on historical datasets, enhance
the accuracy of these predictive capabilities, allowing for the implementation of preventive measures to avert
potential problems and system failures. Additionally, these algorithms provide robust predictions of water
consumption patterns, which subsequently supports the optimization of processes across the entire water supply
network. [7, 21]
The deployment of IoT and AI technologies in water management introduces several challenges. Among the
most prominent are the high initial capital costs, security vulnerabilities, and the need to ensure interoperability
between devices produced by various manufacturers [19, 20]. Reliable data transmission presents another critical
challenge, as current connectivity solutionsÐsuch as Low Power Wide Area Network (LPWAN) technologies (e.g.,
SigFox, LoRaWAN, and NB-IoT) or LTE networks, which are constrained by relatively high power consumption,
which limits their applicability to locations without consistent access to the power grid [22, 23]. In addition,
deploying these technologies in extreme environments, such as underground water utilities, poses additional
complexities. In such conditions, it is essential to address not only adverse microclimatic factors but also the
challenge of maintaining suicient signal strength [24].

Legislation
Legislation has a signiicant impact on water management at both national and international levels. Legal
regulations in this ield focus on water consumption measurement, water management practices, and establish
requirements for water quality and the protection of water resources. With recent advancements in artiicial
intelligence, regulations are also emerging for its application across various sectors. For instance, the EU AI
Act: First Regulation on Artiicial Intelligence [25] includes critical infrastructure among regulated areas, which
encompasses water infrastructure.
3.1
International standards
Currently, there are two international standards from International Organization of Legal Metrology (OIML) and
International Organization for Standardization (ISO), that align with the national legislation of most countries.
Adhering to these standards ensures compliance with the legislative requirements of the majority of nations,
facilitating export and enabling broad standardization across continents.
3.1.1
OIML R49. Like water meters themselves, global approvals for water meters have undergone signiicant
changes over the past 10 years, primarily aimed at unifying the requirements of independent countries within
a single certiication process. As a result, OIML R49 has now become the internationally recognized standard
for measuring cold potable water. The International Recommendations (OIML R) are model regulations that
establish the metrological characteristics required of certain measuring instruments and specify methods and
equipment for verifying their compliance. The OIML R49 standard is an international recommendation outlining
the requirements and testing procedures used to evaluate water meter models.
The OIML R49 standard has been legally adopted for measuring cold potable water and represents a synthesis
of BS 5728, ISO 4064, EN 14154, and numerous other standards from around the world, encompassing regulations
for each water measurement criterion.
3.1.2
ISO 4064. is a set of international standards developed by the International Organization for Standardization
(ISO) that speciies requirements and testing methods for water meters intended for residential, commercial,
and industrial use. These standards are designed to ensure accurate water consumption measurement, promote
consistency in meter design and performance, and facilitate interoperability between products from diferent
manufacturers.

Agriculture
The use of water in agriculture is a key problem in the entire water resources management sector. According to
[27], the proportion of agricultural water consumption in the EU is almost 30%, with only approximately one-third

further handling. Reducing consumption in this sector thus constitutes a critical precondition for sustainable
planting and responsible management of natural resources. This goal currently appears more optimistic, mainly
because the deployment of IoT systems and AI in agriculture has simpliied water care and economisation.

5.1
Smart Irrigation
Irrigation is fundamental to agricultural production in numerous countries, particularly in response to rapidly
changing climatic conditions, where natural rainfall is often insuicient to sustain healthy crop growth. In this
context, irrigation is vital for stabilizing agricultural output and supporting food self-suiciency across various
regions.
In described SW-IoT scheme in agriculture, IoT devices collect data and artiicial intelligence algorithms analyse

Discussion of Applied AI Algorithms in Agriculture
AI in agricultural water management is used to optimize irrigation timing, evapotranspiration prediction, water
resource management and environmental monitoring. Support Vector Machines (SVM) and k-Nearest Neighbors
(kNN) are used in soil moisture classiication, while Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks support time-
series forecasting of weather and water needs. For evapotranspiration, models such as Support Vector Regression
(SVR), Artiicial Neural Network (ANN), and hybrid approaches like AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average
(ARIMA) capture complex environmental relationships. DNNs (Deep Neural Networks) are used for soil moisture
prediction and Decision Support System (DSS) for Real-Time Water Management. AI algorithms are used in
environmental monitoring for soil salinity mapping (ANN) and for soil moisture prediction with diferent
algorithms, such as ARIMA, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Random Forest (RF) or Support Vector
Regression (SVR).

7.5
Discussion of Applied AI Algorithms in Wastewater Management
AI in wastewater treatment focuses on process optimization, pollutant prediction, and intelligent control. Algo-
rithms such as ANN, SVM, XGBoost, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) are applied in advanced
tasks including pollutant control, anomaly detection, and wastewater quality prediction. These algorithms are
also used in smart puriication systems to optimize the removal of persistent pollutants such as PFAS, steroid
hormones, and heavy metals, with applied models including ANN, SVM, Random Forest, Gaussian Processes
(GP), and XGBoost. Furthermore, AI integrated with IoT and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)
platforms enables real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance across wastewater treatment networks.

The IoT and security
Any device connected to a network has an inherent potential risk, especially with inadequate or unreliable
cybersecurity. Cyberattacks occur frequently, and are often designed to harm operational technology and IoT
systems or to steal data. IoT honeypots operated by Symantec, for example, discovered approximately 5200 attacks
every month in 2018 [158]. An attack which impacts an organisation is classiied as a cybersecurity incident
and should be oicially reported. However, most incidents remain unreported [159], and in many cases even
undetected. An estimated 43% of cyberattacks are communicated to authorities, and the remainder are either
concealed or disclosed through the news media [160].
Details of some major security incidents in the past have been released through government reports and
specialised institutions, also in the mass media. Incidents which afect drinking water generally classify as
high-proile events. In 2016, a chemical attack targeted a water treatment plant serving several regions [161].
In this incident, the attacker progressively altered the quantity of chemicals difused in the water. The attack
was discovered and eventually terminated by applying secondary measures. In 2019, the U.S. Attorney Oice for
Kansas prosecuted a case [162] in which a former water treatment plant employee had logged remotely into the
plant’s operating software and shut down its processes. A similar and more serious ofence was committed by
an attacker who gained remote access to a water treatment facility’s systems and deleted all its programs [163].
Not long after, in February 2021, a hacker with stolen credentials attempted to remotely increase the amount of
sodium hydroxide used at another treatment plant [164]. If successful, this would have resulted in poisoning of
the drinking water. The process was reversed by an operator who reduced the chemical’s amount to a normal
level.
An incident involving illicit manipulation with systems at a small reservoir occurred in New York state in 2013
[165]. In recent years, several water treatment plants [166] have been targeted with ransomware attacks, causing
temporary data loss which required manual operation of the facility’s systems. Hssanyadeh et al. [167] presented
an important study which analysed security incidents in the water sector. The article discussed incidents which
occurred before May 2019. Although most of the attacks exhibited the potential to jeopardise human lives, they
ended harmlessly.
Nevertheless, the rising number of attacks in this sector presents a concerning outlook. The growth in IoT
devices connected to networks has paralleled an increase in security incidents, fueling greater interest in IoT
security research. Aslam et al. [168] conducted a survey focused on cyber attacks targeting potable water
systems, while Bello et al. [169] provided a systematic review of cybersecurity challenges in water infrastructure
management, particularly in Australia.

Research challenges, Future Trends and Emerging Technologies
The most current trends in the application of IoT and AI across various domains focus primarily on maximizing
management eiciency, sustainability, and the precision of monitoring selected water resources or key areas.
This efort aims to optimize the entire water management process in these areas, making the handling of this
valuable natural resource sustainable for future generations.
In the ield of water management, there is an observable shift toward the complete digitization of processes, a
paradigm commonly termed "Digital Water Management." This approach relies fundamentally on IoT for data
acquisition and on AI algorithms for advanced data analysis. The concept is further strengthened through the
integration of digital twin technology, Geographic Information Systems (GIS), and remote sensing [185ś187].
Another highly relevant topic is the use of blockchain, whose beneits primarily lie in ensuring data reliability,
increasing transparency and trust among stakeholders, and supporting resource management through decen-
tralized systems. Currently, however, gaps remain between theoretical potential and practical implementation,
particularly in terms of scalability and interoperability. [188, 189].
9.1
Monitoring and Metering
Among the most current topics in this ield is the detection of water leaks throughout the water distribution
network using smart IoT water meters [88, 89, 94] and the deployment of artiicial intelligence methods for
analyzing and processing the data collected in this way [90, 98, 100].
The data collected in this way must be transmitted over long distances with optimized power consumption.
For instance, Boccadoro et al. [190] describe the use of IoT and Sigfox technology for water quality monitoring,
designed for continuous tracking of parameters such as pH, conductivity, oxygen content, and temperature. The
dataset from the Tiziano project is used to train a predictive model based on LSTM neural networks, aiming

As indicated by current trends, IoT technologies are anticipated to play a central role in future water manage-

9.2
Agriculture
Agriculture is characterised by large-scale water consumption for crop growing and supporting livestock (es-
timated at approx. 1/3 of the total volume of drinking water consumed). Due to changing climatic conditions,
current trends focus on eiciency, sustainability, and digital transformation to increase productivity, reduce water
waste, and optimize consumption by directing water precisely where it is needed.
In this context, one of the main challenges will be adapting to dry periods and potential climate extremes.
Future irrigation systems will incorporate predictive AI models that forecast water consumption and determine
optimal irrigation times based on meteorological data and current ield conditions, monitored via IoT sensors.
This approach will allow for targeted irrigation, such as watering only speciic ield areas or precisely designated
spots. [198, 199]
With the continually growing population, increasing crop yields is essential, which often necessitates the
use of agrochemicals (such as pesticides, fertilizers, and others). However, these substances iniltrate the water
cycle, leading to signiicant pollution. Currently, there is increasing pressure to reduce the quantity of chemicals
used, achievable only through highly precise monitoring of plant health (e.g., via machine vision and monitoring
of surrounding climatic conditions with IoT sensors) and highly targeted sprayingÐsuch as with drones. The
number of these autonomous robotic systems in agriculture is expected to grow each year. [11, 17]
Another signiicant emerging trend is the deployment of optical inspection with artiicial intelligence methods
using remote sensing. With advancements in remote sensing technologies and imaging techniques, these tech-
nologies are already being applied in agriculture, not only for monitoring water conditions in landscapes but also
for other agricultural applications. This is achieved through both standard methods and multispectral cameras.
[200, 201].
9.3
Drinking and Wastewater Treatment
A primary research challenge in this ield is the development of advanced wastewater treatment systems with
capabilities for real-time monitoring and control. Smart technologies enable continuous data acquisition on
parameters such as water quality and volume, facilitating the optimization of the entire water treatment process.
Additionally, there is an increasing emphasis on the potential of wastewater as a valuable resource, focusing on
the recovery of essential raw materials for subsequent use [154, 202]. Given the growing importance of resource
recycling and reuse, this trend is expected to intensify.

With advances in materials engineering, nanotechnology, and the development of increasingly sophisticated
advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), these technologies are expected to be used more frequently for mem-
brane iltration or nanoiltration, enhancing the removal of small and currently challenging pollutants, such
as microplastics [203]. AOPs, in turn, enable the removal of emerging contaminants, such as pharmaceutical
compounds, PFAS and other persistent organic pollutants [204].
New methods, such as Wastewater-based Epidemiology (WBE), are expected to be increasingly deployed. WBE
is an innovative approach for monitoring public health through the analysis of biological markers (e.g., viruses)
and chemical markers (e.g., medications, drugs, and others) present in wastewater. Recently, this method has
been used for advanced analysis of viruses and their mutations during the Covid-19 pandemic, as well as for
detecting Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) [205, 206].
Widespread implementation of these methods will enable the development of early warning systems that can
detect pathogens and predict their spread, allowing authorities to respond more promptly to potential public
health threats without the need for individual testing.
Beyond infectious diseases and AMR, authors focus on environmental factors, such as pesticides, microplastics,
and heavy metals, enabling to monitor ecological burdens and their impacts on public health [206].
9.4
Legislation
Smart water management technologies are relatively new tools, therefore the regulations for their use must be
properly established to ensure safe and eicient operation. Since these technologies collect and process data
which contain personal and sensitive information, a key area for legislation is privacy protection and data security.
A legal framework to protect user privacy is therefore an essential ś the collection, storage and use of data
requires legislative instruments to protect it from unauthorised access and misuse.
New legislation addressing the deployment of AI systems across various societal sectors is already emerging.
Among the latest directives is the Artiicial Intelligence Act (AI Act) [25], which regulates the use of these systems
in multiple industries. Within this framework, smart water management is classiied as a high-risk AI system,
which may signiicantly impact the health, safety, or fundamental rights of individuals in the EU. To ensure the
safe and ethically responsible use of these systems, strict requirements are established, including provisions for
human oversight to enable intervention by a human operator when necessary.
Similar legislation broadly governing the use of AIÐthough typically in a less restrictive formÐis also being
developed in other countries. Examples include the Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights [207] in the USA, the Digital
Charter Implementation Act (Artiicial Intelligence and Data Act) [208] in Canada, and the White Paper on AI
Regulation [209] in the United Kingdom. In all cases, these laws and guidelines are responses to rapidly advancing
AI technologies, having been introduced after 2022.
9.5
Security
With the increasing integration of smart technologies in water management systems and critical infrastructures
(e.g., reservoirs, water treatment plants, pumping stations), the number of cybersecurity threats also rises. Need
for comprehensive security solutions across all aspects of these systems is a constant requirement [173] and
the especially for water, where such solutions are life-critical [168]. Current systems address many security
challenges, including conidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, access control, and more [170, 177, 210].
However, future solutions will need to incorporate advanced methods for cybersecurity incident detection, novel
authentication techniques, and enhanced data validation. The anticipated arrival of quantum computing will pose
signiicant challenges for existing cryptographic standards, especially given the limited computational power
of most IoT devices. Furthermore, the trend toward increased automation and minimal human involvement
emphasizes the need for scalable, automated processes for IoT device deployment and security.

Today, AI plays a vital role in IoT security, and the use of machine and deep learning in next-generation IoT
networks is expected to grow [211]. One of the primary beneits is cybersecurity incident detection, which relies
on attack signatures and anomaly detection [212ś214], with comprehensive datasets being developed to support
these applications [215]. However, future challenges include resource limitations for these computations, where
edge computing advancements may help to mitigate [216, 217].
The ongoing proliferation of IoT devices increases the volume of data generated by end devices, creating
challenges in data veriication and authentication. Novel systems utilize blockchain mechanisms to verify data

authentication, where Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs) are currently deployed [220].
While current cryptographic algorithms provide adequate security for today’s computational capabilities,
the advent of quantum computers is poised to transform the ield of cryptography. Preparing IoT devices for a
quantum-resistant cryptographic future is therefore a crucial upcoming challenge [210, 221].
The widespread availability and ubiquity of IoT devices drive the need for automated deployment processes
with minimal human intervention. This necessitates touchless deployment methods [216], which emphasize
well-designed coniguration frameworks based on established security protocols and avoid human errors [177].

Conclusion
The article discussed current trends in the use of the IoT and AI in smart water management. The irst section
introduced relevant areas (drinking water and wastewater treatment, agriculture, monitoring and metering,
legislation and security) and a comparative review of recent related research articles and other related works
which explored and addressed the issues discussed in this article. A novel concept, the SW-IoT (Smart Water ś
Internet of Things), and its individual areas were deined and elaborated.
Future directions and challenges for the current research were outlined and proposed. These research challenges
were classiied into several smart water management areas, with a special emphasis on the application of
IoT systems and AI. For monitoring and metering, the main research area is in developing IoT and LPWAN
technologies in monitoring systems for deployment in locations without any signal coverage or connection
to a power grid. Remote monitoring systems can greatly aid in monitoring water distribution network nodes,
responding to emergencies or other hazardous conditions, remote ground sensing, and real-time monitoring.
Real-time monitoring and control also enables rapid acquisition of water quantity and quality data, which is
useful for optimising water treatment procedures and energy eiciency since drinking water and wastewater
treatment is an energy-intensive process.
From a security perspective, smart technologies deployed for monitoring are not without inherent risks and
may be misused for illegal purposes. It is therefore necessary to develop cybersecurity and early warning systems
to protect water infrastructure. In agriculture, research should aim for irrigation eiciency and water-saving
techniques to make the most efective use of water resources. Smart technologies cold be deployed, for example,
to provide accurate weather forecasting to support efective water management. An especially important future
research area is the establishment of a legal framework to regulate smart water management and the collection,
storage and processing of data obtained through deployed water monitoring systems. The primary aim here
would be protection of data from unauthorised access and handling. Liability for injury and damage to health or
property due to malfunction of these smart systems is also major area for research to address.
