A B S T R A C T  
Water Supply Systems are recognized as significant energy consumers. Consequently, numerous studies have 
concentrated on optimizing and designing energy-efficient water systems. Nonetheless, despite the increasing 
awareness of renewable energy sources, less emphasis was given to the optimized integration of sustainable 
green energy systems in the design and operation of water supply systems. The proposed model presents an 
innovative approach by introducing an optimization model for water supply systems that integrates solar plants 
and battery storage systems, transforming conventional water supply systems design and operation methods. The 
optimization model addresses both the design and operation of these systems, with the primary objective of 
minimizing total costs, including capital and operational expenditures. This novel model not only determines the 
optimal sizing of essential components and daily operations, encompassing power and water flow regulation 
across different seasons, but also signifies a paradigm shift in the planning and management of integrated water 
and energy systems. The comparative analysis between conventional water supply systems and our combined 
water and power systems highlights their compelling advantages. The results show how the utilization of clean 
energy resources can facilitate remarkable improvements in energy cost efficiency. Furthermore, the approach 
not only reshapes conventional operational strategies, but also redefines traditional design guidelines for water 
supply systems. The study also extends to the sensitivity analysis, demonstrating that the advantages of solar and 
battery integration are contingent on the size of the water supply systems, with larger systems experiencing more 
significant benefits. The results in this study advance our understanding of integrated systems, providing a clear 
roadmap toward a cleaner, more efficient, and sustainable future for water and energy infrastructure.   
1. Introduction 
Integrating renewable energy sources with conventional infrastruc­
ture, such as Power Supply Systems (PSSs) and Water Supply Systems 
(WSSs), has indeed become a crucial aspect of sustainable and efficient 
infrastructure design [1]. Coordinating the design and operation of PSSs 
with WSSs can offer several benefits in balancing supply and demand 
and reducing energy utilization costs. WSSs are responsible for deliv­
ering clean water to communities. As such, their design and operation 
require providing an adequate quantity of water for domestic, industrial, 
and agricultural consumers while optimizing the infrastructure in­
vestments (pipes, tanks, and pumps sizing), and optimizing the opera­
tional costs, such as the energy cost associated with pumping [2]. Water 
pumping stations play an important role in many WSSs by providing the 
necessary water pressure to move water from the water sources to 
consumers. These pumps are responsible for a significant portion of 
electrical energy utilization in WSSs, as these costs are estimated to 
account for up to 20% of the electricity consumed by water facilities

In the realm of WSSs optimization, several researchers have pro­
posed innovative approaches to tackle the complex challenges associ­
ated with their optimal design and operation [4–9]. Furthermore, 
several studies have also focused on developing optimization method­
ologies that use metrics based on the energy content in the water systems 
for various tasks, such as leakage minimization [10] and energy recov­
ery from pressurized water networks [11]. These approaches showcase 
the growing interest in advanced optimization techniques for addressing 
the complexities of WSSs design and operation, focusing on energy ef­
ficiency and cost-effectiveness. 
Similarly, PSSs are critical infrastructure for any modern society. 
Nowadays, power systems are evolving due to the impact of Renewable 
Energy Sources (RESs) integration. RESs, such as solar and wind power, 
have become a great alternative to fossil fuel consumption in response to 
global warming mitigation policies and increasing energy demand [12]. 
The integration of RESs into PSSs within a microgrid constellation has 
raised the attention of several researchers due to environmental ad­
vantages and the need for sustainable energy solutions. Despite their 
advantage, renewable sources like solar and wind are intermittent in 
nature, creating fluctuations in voltage and frequency. Hence, the 
microgrid design can include battery storage as an effective solution to 
maintain the balance of voltage and frequency regulation [13]. This area 
of research mainly focuses on optimal microgrid design and operation 
that involve the optimal sizing of RESs, power flow management, grid 
stability, energy storage, and demand-side management. In the quest to 
minimize both the design and operational costs of microgrids, numerous 
optimization models have emerged over the past years, particularly on 
the PV-integrated battery storage systems [14–21]. Nonetheless, all 
these studies focused solely on the power microgrid design and opera­
tion without considering WSSs. 
Traditionally, WSSs and PSSs have been treated as separate entities, 
in which they are managed and optimized independently [22]. Hence, 
the aforementioned research has extensively addressed the optimal 
operation, design, and cost minimization of WSSs using variable market 
price tariffs and also explored the optimal operation and design of PSSs 
with battery integration for voltage and frequency regulation. Although 
significant advancements in both water and power supply systems 
research, the combined integrated design and operation of these two 
critical infrastructures have not received equal attention. While there 
are studies that focused on the operation of combined water and power 
supply systems (e.g., [23–29]), these studies primarily focused on the 
operation aspects of combined systems. They often assumed the exis­
tence of PSSs and WSSs without consideration for design elements or 
special characteristics related to RESs design, such as seasonal varia­
tions. Despite the potential advantages, such as reduced greenhouse gas 
emissions, energy cost savings, and sustainability associated with the 
joint planning and management of water and energy systems [23], there 
remains a significant gap in the literature for the joint optimal design 
and operation of the two systems. Specifically, no prior studies have 
considered the combined design and operation of the water-energy 
systems, considering the variable time-of-day price of electricity. 
Considering the above, recent efforts have been made to address 
these limitations [30,31]. These studies included some of the design 
aspects of the combined systems, focusing on the development of a solar 
energy system integrated with a WSS located on the consumer’s side 
(behind the meter). Yet, the studies did not consider crucial aspects of 
the problem. For example, the developed models did not account for: (a) 
variable time-of-day price (b) battery integration along with operation 
and sizing (c) tanks operation and sizing (d) seasonal variation in solar 
irradiance, and (e) selling excess energy. 
This paper fills a significant gap in the existing literature by offering 
an innovative approach to the integrated design and operation of water 
and power supply system networks, considering renewable energy 
sources, cost optimization, and the specific requirements of each system. 
By combining WSSs and PSSs and considering daily and seasonal 
variable pricing, the study contributes to a more holistic and sustainable 
approach to infrastructure management, taking advantage of energy 
cost savings and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. The holistic nature 
of this research represents a significant step toward more efficient and 
sustainable infrastructure design. To summarize, the main contributions 
of the paper are listed as follows:  
• Existing studies have predominantly focused on optimizing the 
operation of WSS and PSSs separately. This research extends the 
scope to determine the optimal sizing and daily operation of both 
systems simultaneously. By considering joint optimization, the study 
aims to minimize capital expenditure (CapEx) by sizing infrastruc­
ture components while minimizing operational expenditure (OpEx) 
by regulating power and water flows.  
• Unlike previous studies, the developed model includes the operation 
and sizing considerations for water storage tanks and battery storage. 
These components are critical in ensuring stability, reliability, and 
energy storage balance in the system, enabling more efficient utili­
zation of stored energy.  
• The model integrates solar power plants and battery storage into the 
combined water and power system. Addressing the intermittent 
operation of renewable energy sources, this integration seeks to 
leverage the benefits of clean energy in both networks, enhancing 
resilience and reducing reliance on grid-supplied electricity.  
• The model considers design and operation aspects related to 
renewable energy sources, such as seasonal variations in solar irra­
diance and selling excess energy back to the power grid. By consid­
ering dynamic electricity tariffs that vary with time and seasons, the 
model can smoothly transition between solar power, battery storage, 
and grid supply, thus optimizing energy expenses and operational 
efficiency accordingly.  
• The study conducts a comprehensive sensitivity analysis of key 
model parameters. This analysis is crucial for understanding how 
parameter variations impact the performance and cost-effectiveness 
of the integrated system, providing valuable insights for real-world 
implementations. 
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 pre­
sents the methodology, which details the combined WSS and PSS 
mathematical model involving the cost functions of each component and 
the modeling constraints. Section 3 describes the case study of the 
combined network and the conventional WSS case (without solar and 
battery storage sources) with obtained results and discussions. Section 4 
describes a sensitivity analysis for some of the model parameters, and 
the conclusion is drawn in Section 5. 
2. Methodology 
The formulated model pertains to the least-cost design and operation 
of a WSS combined with a PSS, hereinafter denoted as Combined Water 
and Power Systems (CWPS). The whole network consists of the 
following components: pumping units, tanks, pipes, and energy sources 
like solar power, battery storage, and the power grid (Fig. 1). The 
objective is to achieve an optimized, least-cost design and operation of 
the CWPS, while fulfilling the CWPS’s consumer demands, subject to 
technological, operational, and physical constraints.

and a power capacity of Pp(kW). The water is pumped via a pipeline with 
a diameter of d (m) and a length of l (m) to an elevated water tank with a

The solar power plant is a renewable energy source that utilizes solar 
panels to convert sunlight into electrical energy. The design process 
involves determining the optimal capacity and configuration of solar 
panels to maximize the system’s energy generation and power supply. A 
battery storage system is integrated into the overall design for a 
consistent and reliable power supply. This battery system plays a crucial 
role in storing excess energy generated by the solar plant during periods 
of ample sunlight. Importantly, the battery’s function is not limited to 
storing solar energy or supplying the pump. It can also be designed to 
store energy from the grid during times of low electricity prices. This 
design feature allows the battery to take advantage of varying electricity 
costs such that during peak tariff hours, when electricity prices are high, 
the battery can release stored energy back to the grid to generate rev­
enues. This strategic use of the battery’s capacity further optimizes the 
overall economic performance of the integrated system. It is noteworthy 
that the model can also choose not to install a battery if its costs exceed 
its benefits. 
The battery storage system shown in Fig. 1 receives energy from both 
the solar plant (ESB) and the grid (EGB). For clarity, we denote the energy 
flow by E, while the sub-script letters denote the source and target of the 
energy. For example, EGB is the energy supplied from the Grid to the 
Battery. The energy required by the pump is sourced from solar (ESP), 
battery (EBP), and grid (EGP). The power generated by the solar plant can 
be sent to the grid as ESG, and power from the battery can also be 
directed to the grid as EBG. The energy sent to the grid is considered to be 
the selling energy. 
The integrated system interacts with the power grid by considering 
dynamic electricity tariffs that change between the hours of the day, 
days of the week, and seasons. Time-based energy pricing influences the 
system’s decision-making process, enabling seamless switching between 
solar power, battery storage systems, and grid supply. When solar power 
is inadequate, or grid electricity costs are low, the system can utilize grid 
energy to optimize energy costs and operational efficiency. 
The goal is to minimize the overall cost of designing and operating 
the system. This entails selecting the optimal sizes of the tank, pumping 
station, pipes, battery, solar plant, and determining the best time-steps 
for their operation while adhering to water and power network supply 
constraints and system dynamics. Below, we present a comprehensive 
overview of the problem formulation for the WSS and the PSS, including 
the constraints, cost functions, and considerations that guide their 
design and operation. To capture the dynamic behavior of the systems 
over time, in terms of water demands, energy prices, and solar radiation, 
we consider four seasons (s), different categories of days (w), and hourly 
time steps (t). In total, the optimization process simultaneously covers 
eight 24-h periods, corresponding to the four seasons with two cate­

The formulation of the CWPS introduces a challenging optimization 
problem characterized by nonlinearity and nonconvexity. To address 
this complexity, a discretization technique was applied as described in 
[44]. This method restricts continuous decision variables, such as pipe 
sizes, pumping station capacities, and tank sizes, to discrete options 
within their defined ranges. Consequently, binary variables are intro­
duced to mathematically represent the selection process from these 
discrete options. This transformation converts the optimization problem 
into a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problem. We 
employed the Gurobi solver to solve the resulting MILP formulation 
[45]. All runs were conducted within the MATLAB R2023a environ­
ment, utilizing a 12th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i7-12700H processor 
running at 2.30 GHz, and equipped with 64 GB of memory. The models 
are formulated using the Yalmip toolbox for optimization modeling 
[46]. The code and data are available at https://github.com/SWS 
LAB/CWDN/.

considers solar energy generation. If the pump schedule is performed 
based on energy tariffs only (as in the conventional WSS operation, Fig. 
S3 and Fig. S4), the pump will be mostly operated with two modes i.e., 
zero flow and maximum flow. However, when solar power availability is 
a limiting factor, the pump operation is synchronized to match the 
available energy in a way that optimizes the overall cost of the system, as 
discussed in the next section. Fig. S6 in the Supplementary Material 
shows the pump operation and the demand during the weekends for the 
different seasons. Compared to the weekdays, the pump operation 
during the weekends in the summer and the spring seasons shows less 
utilization of the pump. We also note that during the weekends of these 
two seasons, the electricity tariff has a flat rate, as such there is no 
incentive for shifting the pump operation from one period to another.

3.4. Selling energy analysis of the CWPS 
Fig. 8 shows the energy sold to the grid from the solar plant and the 
battery during weekdays (Fig. S9 in the Supplementary Material shows 
the weekend results). As can be seen in Fig. 8b, energy is sold to the grid 
only in the winter. During the winter, solar-generated energy is stored in 
the battery and sold at the evening hours (i.e., on-peak, 18:00 to 21:00). 
During the weekdays of summer and winter, the difference in electricity 
tariff between off- and on-peak periods is relatively high (Table 3), this 
difference incentives storing energy in the battery to be sold during the 
on-peak period. However, in summer, the maximum energy generated 
from the solar panels is utilized by the pump, and the remaining is stored 
in the battery to be used for pumping during the on-peak hours without 
selling it to the grid. On the other hand, in winter, the solar energy is 
utilized by the pump and the remaining is stored in the battery and then 
sold to the grid. During the weekdays of spring and autumn, the dif­
ference in electricity tariff between off- and on-peak periods is relatively 
small (Table 3), thus there is no economic benefit for storing the energy 
between periods and selling energy to the grid (recalling that some of the 
energy will be lost due to battery and inverter efficiencies). During the 
weekends (Fig. S9 in the Supplementary Material), the energy is sold 
from the battery to the grid only in the winter because in the other 
seasons, the weekends’ electricity tariff is a flat rate regardless of the 
time of day (Table 3). 
We note that there are many models for trading energy in different 
markets, such as the energy spot market with near-real-time prices, day- 
head prices, contract-based with time-of-day variable prices, and, of 
course, fixed prices. In this study, we considered half of the price of the 
time-of-day electricity tariff in Israel. We acknowledge that this 
assumption might be too optimistic compared to scientific literature 
[50]. Nonetheless, we reason that large water utilities can bargain on a 
good contract with the power grid manager. To examine the impact of a 
constant selling price, in the Supplementary Material we provide a

average. Interestingly, the results show that the optimal system design

average electricity tariff. Yet, a solar plant that utilizes the entire 
available area is included in the optimal design even when the selling 
price is at 80% of the average tariff. Furthermore, when the selling price

4. Sensitivity analysis 
Sensitivity analysis provides a detailed description of how parame­
ters and their interactions impact model performance. Fig. 9 shows the 
sensitivity of the total cost to changes in the water demand and the 
maximum area allowed for the solar panels. The change in the demand 
on the y-axis ranges from 0.5 to 1.5 of the base run demand in Section 3, 
while the change in the area available for solar plants on the x-axis 
ranges from 0 to 1 ha (10,000 m2). As such, the result of the base run 
presented in Sections 3.1–3.3 is represented by a demand factor of 1 and 
an area of 5000 m2, highlighted with a bold box in the figure. The 
analysis indicates that the parameters exhibit a significant influence 
over the total cost of the network. The highest cost of the system occurs 
at the high demand (i.e., demand factor of 1.5) and no area for solar 
panels (0 m2). The lowest cost occurs when the demand is low (i.e., 0.5 
demand factor) and a large area of solar plant (10,000 m2). This result 
shows that integrating renewable energy sources with WSS has an eco­
nomic advantage. Specifically, analyzing the change of colors in each 
column of Fig. 9 provides insights into the nonlinearity of the cost 
function for different sizes of solar plants. For the first column, which 
represents a system with no solar panels, we can see a rapidly increasing 
function, indicating that the system is sensitive to increasing demand. In 
the last column, which represents the largest solar plant, we can see a 
mildly increasing function, indicating that the system is less sensitive to 
increasing demand with the presence of a large solar plant. Furthermore, 
analyzing the change in colors in each row of Fig. 9 provides insights 
into the advantages of solar plants for WSSs (which have different water 
demands). Comparing the first and last rows shows a more rapid 
decrease in the cost function for a large system with a demand factor of 
1.5. This indicates that integrating solar plants is more advantageous in 
large systems. A detailed explanation with appropriate figures is pro­
vided in Supplementary Material to show how the demand factor and 
the maximum area of the solar plant influence the capital cost, the 
operational cost, the cost of the PSS, and the cost of the WSS. 
Interestingly, the sensitivity analysis shows that even for systems 
without solar panels, it could be beneficial to install a battery storage 
system that stores energy from the grid during off-peak hours to be used 
for pumping during the on-peak hours. 
5. Conclusion 
In this study, we introduced an optimization model to enhance the 
efficiency of WSSs integrated with solar plant and battery storage sys­
tems. Our optimization model simultaneously addresses the design and 
operation of these systems, with the goal of minimizing the total cost, 
encompassing both capital and operational expenditures (CapEx and 
OpEx). The model is designed to determine the optimal sizing of 
essential components and the optimal daily operations, encompassing 
both power and water flow regulation across different seasons. 
To comprehensively evaluate the advantages of integrating solar 
plant and battery storage systems within WSSs, the study contrasts the 
optimal designs of a conventional WSS against a CWPS. This comparison 
underscores several compelling benefits, primarily highlighted around 
using clean energy resources and the consequent enhancement of energy 
cost efficiency. These advantages firmly establish the viability of inte­
grating renewable energies into the WSS, showcasing a transformative 
leap toward a more sustainable, reliable, and cost-efficient infrastruc­
ture. It not only aligns with global efforts to combat climate change but 
also offers tangible economic benefits. The CWPS optimal design un­
derscores the promise of renewable energies in meeting the energy de­
mands of critical infrastructure while contributing to a greener and more 
sustainable future. Considering our case study, the incorporation of solar 
and battery storage within the WSS significantly reduces operational 
Greenhouse Gases (GHGs) emissions, demonstrating a shift from 2.2835 
kt (without solar) to 0.9625 kt (with solar) when integrating solar and 
battery storage system with WSSs (detailed calculation for the GHSs 
estimates is provided in the Supplementary Materials). 
Our results demonstrate that integrating solar and battery storage 
technologies leads to reductions in the size of water infrastructure (such 
as pumps and tanks) and the overall operating costs. We also show that 
traditional operational strategies that are widely adopted in

conventional WSSs operation might not be suitable for WSSs that

WSS, the operator is mainly attempting to maximize the pumping during 
the off-peak period, while in the CWPS, other factors, such as sunlight 
availability and the potential of selling energy to the grid, play an 
important role. Furthermore, in the design aspects, the conventional 
design guidelines for WSSs face novel challenges when applied to 
CWPSs. A key example of this pertains to the role of energy storage fa­
cilities. In traditional WSS design, the primary means of energy transfer 
between different time periods is through a water tank. However, in the 
presence of battery systems, energy storage is distributed between a 
smaller tank and a battery. This shift in energy storage capabilities opens 
up an opportunity to reconsider the size of water tanks in WSSs, 
potentially reducing their volume compared to classical guidelines. This, 
in turn, opens the door for the exploration of new engineering principles 
and strategies for the design and operation of CWPSs. 
Furthermore, the research conducts a sensitivity analysis of the 
CWPS system, analyzing the impact of variations in water system size 
and the availability of area for solar plant installations on the costs of the 
CWPS. Notably, we demonstrate that the integration of solar and battery 
technologies can have different effects on WSSs depending on their size, 
with larger water systems experiencing more significant advantages 
from the inclusion of solar plants. The sensitivity analysis also reveals 
that CWPSs equipped with substantial solar facilities are better equipped 
to meet increasing water demands within the WSSs. Additionally, the 
results indicate that there exists a threshold beyond which the size of the 
solar plant can generate operational profits, which can offset a portion of 
the capital costs. This threshold depends on the water system’s size, with 
larger water systems requiring larger solar plants to generate opera­
tional profits. 
The development of the CWPS optimization model provides signifi­
cant opportunities for examining the performance and characteristics of 
CWPS systems by investigating variations in parameters and various 
scenarios related to technology advancements and policies. Future 
research can leverage this model to assess how improvements in battery 
systems and solar energy generation technology will impact CWPS sys­
tems. Policy makers can use such analyses to design economic incentives 
for water utilities to enhance the transition toward CWPS systems. 
Moreover, future research can delve into the effects of diverse electricity 
tariff structures and electricity purchasing agreements, allowing deci­
sion makers to formulate optimal mechanisms for the coordinated 
management of energy and water systems. These future research di­
rections promise to offer novel insights and a broader perspective, 
deepen our understanding of the complicated dynamics inherent in in­
tegrated systems, and further illuminate the path toward cleaner, more 
efficient, and sustainable water and energy systems.
