A B S T R A C T
In arid regions worldwide, sea and brackish water desalination is the primary source of potable water. In Kuwait
and the Gulf countries, co-generation plants combine seawater desalination and power generation. Thermal
desalination is found on a large scale in these plants, with more than 70 % share. Similarly, electric power
generation is dominated by fossil fuels that operate steam and combined cycle turbines. This research focuses on
the design and performance analysis of large-scale concentrated solar power (CSP) plants with a capacity of 111
MWe. The system utilizes parabolic trough solar collector (PT) and molten salt energy storage tanks. The system
is combined with a direct contact membrane distillation system DCMD), with a capacity of 100,000 m3/d. Design
and analysis of the CSP system are done using the System Advisory Model (SAM), which utilizes daily average
data on ambient temperature and solar radiation in Kuwait to design and size the PT-CSP system. The model
generates the thermal energy load and the amount of electric power produced by the PT-CSP system. This data is
used to design a large-scale DCMD plant that operates on feed and cooling water temperatures of 60 ◦C and 20 ◦C,
respectively. The feed stream in the DCMD system flows across four elements in series, with a 5 ◦C drop in each
component, and the cooling water flows in a parallel configuration. The system’s design characteristics and
model predictions are well within the data reported in previous literature studies. Estimates of the levelized cost
for both systems are $0.83/m3 for desalinated water and $0.11/kWh for electric power. Both values agree with
the literature data. The model and results presented in this research provide valuable insight into the design
elements and characteristics of large-scale renewable energy plants and non-conventional desalination systems
that utilize, in part, low-grade energy and heating steam from low-pressure turbines. This proposed configuration
addresses the pressing need for sustainable, renewable, non-fossil energy sources combined with seawater
desalination.
1. Introduction
Water and energy are essential elements of humane life and welfare.
The rapid growth of the world population, especially in developing
countries, has resulted in a lack of adequate freshwater resources and
electric power availability. In 2019, WHO reported that 1 in 3 people
worldwide cannot access safe drinking water or adequate electricity
supply [60]. Therefore, due to limitations of natural resources, desali-
nation has become a significant source of fresh water in arid countries
[35,18]. In Kuwait, the Gulf countries, and several other arid regions
around the world, the primary source of fresh water is desalination,
either by thermal methods, which include multistage flash (MSF) and
the multiple effect (MED) processes, or by membrane desalination
(reverse osmosis (RO)). Desalination plants operate on seawater and low
to high-salinity brackish water aquifers [4,28] gave a review of various
configurations of membrane distillation (MD) water production cost,
energy efficiency, and gain output ratio (GOR). The review showed three
orders of magnitude variation in energy consumption and four orders of
magnitude in water cost. Several factors, including unit size, energy
source, system efficiency, cost of energy, etc., cause this widespread.
Further analysis of these values shows that size scale and lack of com-
mercial scale units cause widespread.
The main drawback of desalination is its high rate of energy con-
sumption. Therefore, most desalination plants are constructed in fossil
fuel co-generation configuration, where electricity and heating steam
are available for thermal and membrane desalination [8]. Research
studies continue to seek sustainable and renewable energy resources in
place of fossil fuels and development of more energy-efficient desali-
nation processes [48,1,61] [45]. demonstrated that solar energy can be

systems increased drastically during the past decade [20,1]. The
research covered most of the known configurations, including direct

temperature and membrane porosity [9]. compared experimental mea-

mance predictions, while the lumped parameter model predictions are

2. System description and ambient conditions
The proposed system consists of a PT-CSP plant and a DCMD system,
as shown in Fig. 1. The PT-CSP plant consists of a PT solar collector,
thermal storage tanks, and a power block. A two-tank system with
molten salt as the storage medium is chosen for the thermal energy
storage (TES) system. The heat transfer fluid (HTF) circulates through
the PT solar collector, receives thermal energy, and transfers the energy
into the TES hot tank at point (1), transfers the energy into the heat
exchanger of the block at point (2), returns to the TES cold tank at point
(3), and finally enters the PT solar collector at point (4). The PT col-
lector, TES storage tanks, and HTF design and operation allow for
continuous power generation during the daylight and night hours [65].
In the power block, the HTF evaporates the water stream, generates
saturated steam, and changes into superheated steam, which is used to
drive the power turbines at point (5). The turbines drive the electricity
generators, and superheated steam leaves the turbines as low-pressure
steam at point (6). The low-pressure steam is condensed by a cold
seawater stream at point (7) and leaves the condenser to enter the heat
exchanger at point (8).
The DCMD system is connected to the power unit at points (7) and
(9). The cold feed seawater is heated in the condenser to the desired
operating temperature of the MD system. The system design allows the
heating of 3000 L of feed seawater per one MWh generated in the power
unit [54]. The average seawater temperature in Kuwait, used as cooling
water in the power plant condenser, is between 25 ◦C to 43 ◦C as
depicted in Fig. 2 [46],. The brine discharge leaves the feed channel of
the DCMD at point 12, and the distillate water leaves the permeate tank
of the DCMD system, and part of the cold distillate product circulates
through the permeate channel, and points (10) and (11).
Abdali, Kuwait, is chosen as the location of the PT-CSP-MD plant.
Abdali has high direct normal irradiance (DNI) near the Gulf. CSP plants
are economically viable in locations where the DNI exceeds 5.5 kWh/
m2/day (1800 kWh/m2//year) (H. L [63,64]). Fig. 3 shows the DNI
hourly average data for each month in Abdali, Kuwait. The data is part of
the SAM database [15]. As shown, the site has DNI levels greater than
5.5 kWh/ m2/day throughout the year; the highest daily DNI of 7.855
kWh/ m2/day is recorded in August, while the lowest value of 5.74
kWh/ m2/day is recorded in November. The monthly average for the
data shown in Fig. 3 is used in the simulation and calculations of the
power generated by the CSP system.

The DCMD model is based on the following assumptions:
• Steady state operation is assumed to give the system design and
operating features at the desired design value. In actual operation, all
industrial systems go through a transient decline from the design
point due to changes in operating conditions, such as membrane
fouling.
• Laminar and one-dimensional flow in the feed and permeate chan-
nels; this is because, in actual operation, the Reynolds number in
both channels is less than 2300 [30]
• Negligible heat losses to the surroundings because of the low-
temperature difference between the membrane cells and the
ambient air.
• Constant total pressure in the feed and permeate channels; this is
because of the smooth membrane surface and the low Reynolds
number [47],
• Complete salt rejection, which is the main hydrophobic feature of the
MD membranes [16]
• Water vapor is transported via a tortuous pass across the membrane
[16],
• The air entrapped within the membrane pores is stationary, which
enables the water vapor to permeate through it (Noor A Mohammad
[10,11]).
• Complex transport mechanisms, including simultaneous mass and
heat transfer, occur across the MD membrane. The mass transfer
mechanisms include (i) Knudsen diffusion, (ii) molecular diffusion,
and (iii) Poiseuille (viscous) flow (A [26,27]), (M. I [53]).

The characteristics of the PT-CSP system are calculated by SAM and
are used to generate variations in daily and hourly averages for each
month of the year. These results include thermal/electrical power
(Fig. 4), cycle efficiency (Fig. 5), input/output power of HTF (Fig. 6),
incident power and power transmitted to the power block (Fig. 7), gross
electrical power (Fig. 8), and cooling water flow rate (Fig. 9). Fig. 4 il-
lustrates the hourly incident solar power, the hourly thermal energy
supplied to the power block, and the gross electrical output.
Fig. 4 shows the highest incident power was in June and August, with
values of 761.1 and 762.6 MWt, respectively. The largest thermal energy
supplied to the power block was 291.5, 290.9, and 288.8 MWt in June,

9 a.m., from January to March and September to December. The nega-

Therefore, under these conditions, the desalination system will be
maintained idle. Another option is to provide the required power

cloud cover and the low angle of the incident solar radiation are not
sufficient to generate power. The average monthly thermal energy of
HTF and input energy to the power block energy are relatively similar

The monthly incident solar power and thermal energy supplied to the
power block are shown in Fig. 7. Monthly incident solar power peaks in

GWh, respectively. The thermal energy supplied to the power block
peaks in June, July, and August, with 52.5, 52.4, and 52 GWh, respec-

Fig. 8 shows the monthly energy production, which peaks in June,
July, and August, with values equal to 20.5, 20.3, and 20.1 GWh,

Fig. 8 shows that energy generation was lowest in January, November,
and December, with values of 11.5, 12.6, and 10.7 GWh, respectively. In
the remaining part of the year, characterized by high DNI, the electric
power generation ranged between 18 and 20.5 GWh. During the winter,
the energy produced is reduced to below 16 GWh. This is because the
performance of a PT plant is affected by DNI, ambient temperatures, and
relative humidity.
Fig. 9 shows variations in the cooling water consumption predicted
by SAM. The consumption rate follows a similar pattern to DNI and
electric power generation. The highest demand for cooling water was
24,968, 25,064, and 24,847 m3 for June, July, and August, respectively,
while the minimum demand was 13,070, 14,989, and 12,461 m3 for
January, November, and December, respectively.
The proposed CSP plant design in Kuwait generates 828.5 GWh of
annual energy, with a capacity factor of 85.80 % and a gross-to-net
conversion of 90.60 %. Table 2 summarizes the PT-CSP plant’s annual
energy production and performance indicators estimated by SAM. It
should be stressed that maintaining these efficiencies in the arid con-
ditions of the Kuwaiti environment will require close monitoring of dust
and soiling of the collector tubes and reflecting mirrors [2]. The studies
of [12,2,34] show a 2 % daily generated power reduction if soiling is
unchecked. An optimized cleaning scheme is proposed by [12] to
maintain high power generation efficiencies and to limit the water used
in cleaning.
Fig. 10 shows the effect of the pressure difference across the mem-
brane on the permeate flux. Calculations are made for 30 and 45 ◦C feed
temperatures and permeate temperatures of 15 and 20 ◦C. As shown, the
permeate flux increases with the pressure difference across the mem-
brane. This result is consistent with previous literature data reported [5,
22]. The more significant pressure difference across the membrane oc-
curs at higher feed and lower permeate temperatures. (A [26,27]) They
operated an experimental unit with PTFE membranes at feed tempera-
tures varying between 40 and 90 ◦C. Moreover, permeate temperatures
between 5 and 25 ◦C. They reported permeate fluxes close to 100 kg/m2
h at feed temperatures of 90 ◦C. Choice of such conditions would require
staging the DCMD elements, where the temperature of the feed stream
will decrease across the elements from 90 ◦C to a temperature higher
than the surroundings by 5–10 ◦C, which is the standard practice in
thermal desalination processes. This implies that the DCMD elements
will operate along the line shown in Fig. 10, where the high-temperature
elements operate at the most significant pressure difference, and the
low-temperature elements operate at the smallest pressure difference. A
schematic for such a configuration is shown in Fig. 11, where the feed
cascades across the elements while the cold permeate stream enters the
elements in parallel. The temperature of the product permeate is
reduced to a temperature of 5 ◦C lower than the brine temperature
leaving the last MD module. Cooling of the permeate stream takes place
in a cooling tower by ambient air. The amount of heating steam needed
to increase the temperature of the feed seawater by 40 ◦C is 170 kg/s,
which is based on an average latent heat of 2200 kJ/kg, total product
equal to 100,000 m3/d, and a recovery ratio equal to 0.65. Fig. 12 shows
the solubility limits of calcium sulfate as a function of temperature and
concentration and the operating line of the DCMD. As shown, the feed
enters the first element at a temperature of 60 ◦C and salinity of 42,

Fig. 13 shows the effect of Reynolds number of the feed and permeate
sides on the permeate flux. These results are obtained at feed and
permeate temperatures equal to 60 ◦C and 20 ◦C, respectively. As
shown, the permeate flux increases with the Reynolds number because
of the reduction of the resistances in the mass and heat transfer
boundary layers on both sides of the membrane. This increases the heat
and mass transfer coefficient and consequently increases the driving
force for permeation and the permeate flux. Similar findings were pre-
viously reported [31], where the Reynolds number increased in the
turbulent regime from 5000 to 7000, and the permeate flux increased by
50 %.

50 g/L, which caused a 20 % reduction in the permeate flux by 20 %,

feed side. This increases the magnitude of concentration polarization,
which hinders the vapor motion. Similar levels of reduction are reported
(Noor A Mohammad [10,11]) for feed temperatures equal to 60 ◦C and
feed concentrations ranging between 25 and 50 g/L and (A [26,27]) at
temperatures equal to 50–70 ◦C and feed concentration ranging between
0 and 100 g/L.

6. Conclusions
This study presented the design, analysis, and economics of a com-
bined system of 111 MWe PT-CSP and 100,000 m3/d DCMD that oper-
ates in Kuwait’s ambient conditions. The SAM software was used to
design and determine the economics of the PT-CSP plant. The solar
collectors produced sufficient thermal energy to operate the power block
during daylight hours and to store energy for nighttime operation. Two
storage tanks (hot/cold) with molten salt are used for energy storage.
The PT-CSP plant uses part of the feed seawater of the DCMD system as a
cooling stream in the heat exchanger unit. This accounts for approxi-
mately 10 % of energy saving in heating the DCMD feed stream. The
remaining thermal load for heating the feed stream is obtained from part
of the low-pressure steam in the steam turbine system. The feed seawater
temperature is increased to 60 ◦C and is fed to a series of four membrane
elements. The use of the multi-stage MD system results in GOR values
greater than one, which is achieved through the efficient use of low-
