H I G H L I G H T S
• Energy data can function as an observatory for water system performance.
• Observatories emphasize trajectories and response, not static efficiency metrics.
• Energy signals integrate hydraulics, quality, climate, and infrastructure condition.
• Observational energy data reveal system drift before failure occurs.
• The energy observatory is orthogonal to modeling, optimization, and AI approaches.

A B S T R A C T
Water utilities are strained by climate variability, aging infrastructure, workforce changes, funding, and 
dependence on the electric grid. Although utilities routinely collect detailed energy data, they usually treat the 
data narrowly for billing, compliance, efficiency reporting, or dashboards. What remains surprisingly absent is 
something more fundamental: a continuous way of seeing how systems behave over time. In this Making Waves 
article we argue that operational energy data can function as an observatory for water system performance: a 
continuous signal that integrates hydraulic behavior, water quality management, infrastructure condition, 
climate exposure, and institutional capacity. The argument reframes energy data from a static efficiency metric 
to a diagnostic, long-term record of system performance. The observatory emphasizes sensitivity over causal 
attribution and remains deliberately orthogonal to digital twins and artificial intelligence applications; it em­
phasizes interpretation over prediction. By articulating this new perspective we aim to improve how the water 
sector observes, analyzes, and manages the systems it already operates.
1. The observatory water systems already have
Modern water treatment and distribution systems operate 
quietly—until they do not. For decades, they have delivered water 
reliably while absorbing gradual changes in demand, climate, infra­
structure, and regulation. Today, those changes accumulate faster and 
interact more often. In response, the water sector has produced an 
expanding collection of metrics, dashboards, and tools meant to improve 
performance and resilience (Sakai, 2024).
What remains surprisingly absent is something more fundamental: a 
continuous way of seeing how systems behave over time.
Other environmental fields rely on observatories. Stream gauges 
show hydrologic variability long before rivers run dry; atmospheric 
monitoring networks detect slow shifts that no single storm could 
explain. Water utilities, by contrast, tend to observe themselves 
intermittently—through audits, inspections, master plans, and regula­
tory reports produced years apart. These snapshots offer detail but rarely 
reveal motion.
Yet most water utilities already collect observatory-grade data. En­
ergy data associated with pumping, treatment, storage, and distribution 
are continuous, system-wide, and archived. Utilities collect them for 
billing and compliance, and researchers use them to estimate efficiency 
or emissions (Zib et al., 2021). What remains underdeveloped is the idea 
that energy data can function as an observatory for water system

Although research has demonstrated using energy trend data espe­
cially for pump diagnostics and optimization (Luna et al., 2019; Zhou 
et al., 2024), systematic programs for long-term water system perfor­
mance are not widespread. Water utilities often struggle to find their 
own energy data (Chini and Stillwell, 2017; Sowby et al., 2019), 
complicating the development of utility-level energy analytics con­
nected to water system performance.
We argue for a shift in perspective. Rather than treating energy as a 
narrow efficiency metric, we propose treating energy as an interpretive 
lens, a way to observe the hidden state of long-term system performance. 
Doing so does not require new technology; rather, it requires learning to 
see differently. By naming energy data as an operational observatory, we 
seek to shift how the water sector understands system performance.
2. What the observatory is (and is not)
With the water–energy literature already crowded (Fayiah et al., 
2020; Helerea et al., 2023), the observatory is a reframing of the 
issue. It treats energy data as a proxy signal that integrates many di­
mensions of system performance, emphasizing time, variability, and 
response: how systems behave, not just how they score.
The observatory is not a proposal for a new metric. It does not require 
widespread deployment of new sensors or artificial intelligence, though 
such improvements are complementary and rapidly developing (Sela 
et al., 2024). Nor does it claim that energy data alone can diagnose 
causality or replace detailed hydraulic or water quality analysis.
The value of an observatory lies in its sensitivity to change. Moni­
toring of energy trends can reveal unintended behavior that might not be 
detected until later, if at all. It can suggest which kinds of analyses are 
warranted (e.g., sensor calibration, hydraulic modeling, water quality 
testing) and shape the next diagnostic steps where staff can determine 
the severity, cause, or solution. Unlike efficiency frameworks, which 
compress behavior into a static ratio, the observatory treats energy use 
as a record of motion overlain on varying conditions (Fig. 1).
3. What energy data say about water systems
Mix et al. (2020) showed how anomalies in flow and pressure time 
series can indicate burst leaks, continuous leaks, backflows, and pump 
failures. The point is that the data stream, when studied, shows more 
than the variable it is tracking, and paying attention to the signals can 
lead to early interventions and prompt improvement in system 
performance.
Likewise, energy use reflects far more than resource consumption. It 
records how systems move water, manage quality, maintain assets, and 
organize people. Pumping energy, for example, responds to many con­
ditions. Gradual increases in energy use at constant demand often signal 
mechanical decline like fouling, friction, or leaks, while variable energy 
use can indicate unstable control. Energy aggregates these behaviors 
across time and space in ways few other signals can.
Energy also reflects water quality management. Mixing, recircula­
tion, treatment intensity, and storage turnover all leave energetic traces. 
Systems that rely on operational intervention to maintain quality exhibit 
different energy patterns than those that rely on infrastructure design. 
Changes in treatment strategy, whether intentional or not, often appear 
first as changes in energy use.
Climate and hydrology leave their imprint as well. Drought, seasonal 
variability, and source switching alter pumping regimes and treatment 
requirements. A declining water table corresponds to higher energy 
demand at the well; wetter conditions correspond to lower energy de­
mand system-wide. Drinking water systems’ energy footprints depend 
on source, quality, and volume, but also on local temperature and pre­
cipitation patterns—factors tied to a changing climate (Sowby and 
Hales, 2022). Energy data capture such shifts directly, even when the 
drivers are not precisely known.
Infrastructure condition appears quietly in energy records. Condition 
assessment rests on integrating data streams such as flow, pressure, and 
energy use to infer the failure risk of buried infrastructure (Delnaz et al., 
2023). Aging pumps, fouled membranes, corroded pipes, and failing 
valves rarely announce themselves, but changes in energy can point to 
such problems. Energy intensity drift—slow increases in energy use per 
unit volume of water—often precedes alarms or failures.
Finally, energy data reflect institutional capacity (Andrews and 
Johnson, 2016; Ingram et al., 2023). Staffing levels, automation matu­
rity, and operational discipline shape how systems respond to distur­
bances. Stable institutions tend to produce stable energy patterns; 
institutions under strain often leave energetic fingerprints: variance, 
inefficiency, increased operational costs, and reactive operation.
Most energy–water research treats energy as something to minimize. 
That framing has value, but an observatory asks different questions. 
How does energy use respond to a heat wave? How quickly does the 
system recover from a power disruption? After a drought, does the 
system return to its prior state, or does it settle into a new one? Seen this 
way, energy time series are more like hydrographs than scorecards. 
Mean values matter, but so do shapes. The observatory provides context 
for existing metrics.
The observatory perspective is orthogonal to other approaches. 
Research on the clean-energy transition treats energy as an external 
system to which water utilities must adapt (Sowby, 2023) while digital 
twins and artificial intelligence approaches treat data as inputs to 
simulation (Ahmed et al., 2024). The observatory reverses the direction: 
it treats energy data as the primary observational signal, not merely an 
input or outcome. Models seek to simulate system behavior; observa­
tories seek to interpret behavior.
4. Observatory framework
Incorporating energy observation into water system performance 
would provide a more holistic and sustainable approach to system 
management. For example, developing the observatory outlined in Fig. 1
for planning, operation, and management could provide value. As dis­
cussed in Section 3, review of water system energy behavior can provide 
substantiable benefits, but frameworks to incorporate the data into each 
practice are necessary.
Energy information is available, often through a SCADA system or 
power utility database, but its direct implementation into management 
and operations of water systems is not. The observatory is not so much a 
technology as it is a program, extending a data system into a human

Here we outline several levels of frameworks to implement the en­

5. Implications for research, practice, and system analysis
Viewing energy data as an operational observatory exposes gaps that 
metrics often conceal. The field lacks shared expectations for what 
“normal” energy behavior looks like. Variability and recovery receive 
less attention than averages. Energy data rarely couple with hydraulic 
models in ways that support interpretation rather than calibration. 
Institutional factors such as staffing, policies, operational culture remain 
largely invisible in technical analyses, despite their clear energetic 
signatures.
Our paper does not resolve these gaps. But it does name them. It 
defines a concept intended to motivate empirical work in an already 
data-rich industry.
Treating energy data as an operational observatory has several key 
features. First, energy data act as integrative observables, reflecting 
hydraulics, water quality management, infrastructure condition, climate 
exposure, and institutional capacity. Second, the observatory differs 
fundamentally from efficiency metrics, emphasizing trajectories and 
responses rather than static ratios. Third, the observatory prioritizes 
sensitivity over attribution, offering early insight even when deeper 
analysis remains necessary. Finally, the approach is orthogonal to 
modeling frameworks, focusing on interpretation rather than prediction.
To advance the development of energy observatories, we suggest 
work toward the following research questions. They address both 
technical and organizational practices. 
• What patterns in utility energy time series distinguish normal oper­
ational variability from early signs of hydrologic, hydraulic, me­
chanical, or institutional stress?
• How can energy data be coupled with flow, pressure, storage, and 
water quality records to interpret system behavior without over­
claiming causal attribution?
• What

• What organizational practices, staffing capacities, and data man­

Reframing energy data as an operational observatory does not solve 
every challenge facing water systems. It does, however, offer a quieter 
and more durable advance: a way to see systems as they change, before 
change becomes failure. The opportunity lies not in collecting more 
data, but in recognizing what existing data already reveal.
