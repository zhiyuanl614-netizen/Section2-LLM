Abstract—The occurrence of large-scale outages in power dis-
tribution systems (PDSs) caused, for instance, by extreme weather
events has raised concerns on the cascading impact of the power
outages on the resilience of other critical infrastructures, includ-
ing water distribution systems (WDSs), whose healthy operation
highly relies on the availability of power from PDSs. This paper
proposes an analytics model for quantifying the interdependence
between the resilience of power and water distribution systems. The
proposed model ﬁrst performs spatio-temporal outage analysis on
the PDS and WDS to determine the PDS power serving capability
and the resulting cascading impact on the WDS water serving
capability during the occurrence of disruptions. The resilience
interdependence is then determined by a proposed set of six metrics
calculated using the power and water serving capability proﬁles,
which trace and quantify the cascading impacts of power outages in
the operation of WDSs during the degradation and recovery stages.
The proposed analytics model is implemented on the IEEE 33-bus
power distribution system supplying power for pumping stations
of a 15-node test WDS, considering multiple cases of water storage
capacity, single and multiple outage scenarios, and availability of
distributed generation. The numerical results demonstrate that the
proposed metrics track the temporal and spatial interdependence
between the resilience of power and water distribution systems,
providing a mechanism to identify weak spots, and assess options
to enhance the resilience of the critical water infrastructure.
Index Terms—Water-power nexus, water distribution system,
power distribution system, resilience metric, outage analysis.

I. INTRODUCTION
A. Background and Literature Review
The interdependence between power distribution systems
(PDSs) and water distribution systems (WDSs) has received
growing attention in recent years, focusing on opportunities
for coordinated operation to improve the performance of both
systems[1],[2].Theothersideofthisinterdependence,however,
is related to the vulnerability to outages that cascade from power
grid into the water infrastructure. The PDSs are exposed to a
wide variety of faults caused by equipment failures or external
events [3]–[6], which can cause subsequent cascading outages in
WDSs that are powered by the PDS. The vulnerability of PDSs
to such events has been experienced during multiple, recent
large-scale catastrophic events, Superstorm Sandy in 2012 and
Hurricane Maria in 2017, which have disrupted the operation
of other infrastructures dependent on power supply, including
water and transportation systems [7]–[10]. In particular, the
interaction between power and water distribution systems is
critical in case of disasters, since their extended interruption can
represent a life-threatening risk for vulnerable communities, as
occurred in Texas during the 2021 winter storm [11].
Despite the vulnerabilities of power and water infrastructures
being well known, actions aimed at enhancing the resilience of
PDSs and WDSs are based on metrics that treat these systems
as independent, neglecting the effect of their interdependent
operation. Strategies for enhancing resilience of PDSs include
splitting the grid into microgrids for self-healing operation [5],
utilizing distributed resources for local load restoration [12],
[13], distribution line hardening [14], and grid reconﬁguration to
maximize the power supply to the affected demand [15], where
resilience is associated with the amount of load shed during
recovery. Alternatively, the resilience assessment is achieved
based on a performance curve that assesses the evolution of a
resilience indicator in time, the triangle curve or the trapezoid
curve [16], [17]. The trapezoid curve is utilized in [18] to
formulate ﬁve metrics which describe the phases experienced
by a critical infrastructure during the disaster and its restoration.
In addition, six probabilistic metrics are introduced in [19] to
capture the response of automated devices in the grid during
the fault isolation and restoration. These metrics are then used
in [20] to assess the resilience of the grid against a speciﬁc
type of natural disaster. Furthermore, a metric to quantify the
system resilience to a time-evolving event is presented in [21]
to consider the risk to extreme temperatures and water scarcity.
Besides, the quantiﬁcation of resilience based on load shedding
is extended to involve a component criticality ranking in [22] to

The resilience quantiﬁcation in WDSs has followed two major
methods. On one side, the metrics use the deﬁnition introduced
in [34], where resilience is quantiﬁed as the ability to return to a
non-failure state after a failure has occurred. On the other hand,
resilience is quantiﬁed by the ability of WDS to deliver water
based on the available pressure head surplus at each node,the
amount of pressure head above the minimum required for water
distribution [35]. Additional metrics have been proposed in the
literature based on these methods [29], [30], which are solely
developed for a stand-alone WDS.
In general, the resilience quantiﬁcation in critical infrastruc-
tures is associated with the amount of demand supplied during
and after the event. A number of resilience metrics is developed
by modeling the infrastructures as graphs, where resilience is
deﬁned using the ratio between demand satisfaction and total
demand [36] or the weighted sum of the individual perfor-
mance of each infrastructure [37]. While this approach offers
a modeling alternative for any interdependent infrastructure, the
resilience assessment requires developing methods that consider
speciﬁc constraints of each system, which cause inaccuracies
when neglected [38]. In particular, metrics to quantify resilience
of interdependent power-gas networks are formulated in [39],
[40], which are respectively obtained based on the cost of the
curtailed energy demand and the convex linear combination of
each infrastructure performance based on budget. In power and
transportation infrastructures, where power outages cause trafﬁc
light de-energization, resilience is quantiﬁed as a function of the
cost of power curtailed and the cost of travel times after power
interruptions for trafﬁc lights [10] or as the ratio between the
travel time before and after the event [41].
The interdependent nature of the PDS and WDS operation has
been scarcely considered in metrics for resilience assessment.
A study based on data retrieved from the 1994 Northridge
earthquake was conducted in [42], where pumps stations were
identiﬁed as vulnerable elements during the power supply inter-
ruption. A framework for enhancing resilience in interdependent
power-water infrastructures is developed in [43], where the
resilience is quantiﬁed based on the number of users with access
to electricity and water supply after a disaster. On the other hand,
a metric is proposed in [44] to aggregate several independent and
interdependent performance functions of a power transmission
grid and a WDS, weighted in a single indicator based on a WDS
sensitivity analysis. Despite the existing methodologies, there is
a lack of mechanisms to model and quantify the interdependence
between the resilience of power and water distribution systems
during the response and recovery after disasters, which are
key tools to identify vulnerabilities affecting the infrastructures
during the high-impact events.
B. Contribution and Paper Structure
This paper proposes an analytics model for quantifying the
interdependence between the resilience of power and water dis-
tribution systems. The proposed model quantiﬁes the cascading
impact of power outage in PDS that is propagated into the WDS
by disrupting the operation of pumping stations, which would
subsequently cause a water shortage in the WDS. Different to
methodologies based on using a generic metric to assess the
resilience of power systems with water and other infrastructures,
the proposed approach quantiﬁes the interdependence between
the resilience of power and water infrastructures by tracking
how the PDS response during a disruption is cascaded into the
WDS operation, considering different stages of the operation
and performance of the systems after outages. The proposed
approach ﬁrst determines the average power and water serving
capabilities resulting from the spatio-temporal outage scenarios
emulating the impacts of an external event. The importance of
generating spatio-temporal outage scenarios is to include the
impact of the outage location on the WDS resilience. Also, the
assessment is not limited to a single scenario and instead aims at
considering multiple scenarios representing different outcomes
from the external event.
Furthermore, instead of deﬁning a single uniﬁed resilience
indicator, a set of six indicators are proposed, which are carefully
formulated to quantify effects on the WDS serving capability
caused by interruptions in the power supply during the outage
affecting the PDS serving capability. More speciﬁcally, the six
indicators aim at quantifying relative effects of the PDS outages
on the WDS operation and resilience, evidenced in different
stages of the serving capability curves associated with the effect
of the PDS degradation on the WDS degradation, the effect of
the PDS restoration on the WDS recovery, the ratio of supplied
water with respect to amount of power in the PDS, the simi-
larities between the power and water serving capabilities, and
the time differences between the power and water disruption
and restoration. Hence, the metrics aim at providing sensitivity
to WDS vulnerabilities intensiﬁed by its interdependence with
PDS and expect to motivate utilities from different infrastruc-
tures to switch paradigms from an isolated and independent
planning and operation to a comprehensive framework where
efforts are coordinated to make their systems more resilient.

II. OVERVIEW OF THE PROPOSED MODEL
A reliable power supply is critical for the operation of energy-
intensive processes in WDSs, including water withdrawal, treat-
ment, and pumping, which are supplied by PDSs [2], [45].
According to [46], almost all of the water treatment facilities
in the United States are dependent on an external power supply
to maintain their core operation. The occurrence of faults in the
PDSmayresult inapower outageinthewater facilityanddisrupt
the operation of WDS. The mitigation of power outage risk on
water treatment and water distribution facilities relies mostly on
the use of fuel-based backup power generators, including diesel,
naturalgas,propane,andgasolinegenerators.Stationaryengines
and portable generators are used depending on the amount
of power to be supplied. In case of large demand, stationary
engines provide uninterrupted electricity to supply the demand
and power pumps, while less expensive portable generators
are preferred to supply facilities with low power consumption.
In the United States, these portable generators can be owned
by the water facility or borrowed from other utilities before a
naturaldisasterthroughtheWater/WastewaterAgencyResponse
Network (WARN) to ensure the backup power requirements are
met during the event [47], [48]. Although 88% of the treatment
facilities are equipped with some form of backup generation,

systems.

resilience metrics, which quantify the impacts of PDS outage
scenarios in WDS operation according to the temporal evolution
of water shortage during power outages. The interdependence of
power systems to water systems at power transmission level is
well-known, since water is required for hydropower generation

III. OUTAGE ANALYSIS IN INTERDEPENDENT POWER AND
WATER DISTRIBUTION SYSTEMS
This section presents the proposed models for outage analysis
in interdependent power and water distribution systems. In the
following, we ﬁrst introduce the proposed spatio-temporal out-
age scenario generation and evaluation setup, followed by the
mathematical formulation of models for power outage analysis
in power distribution and the cascading water shortage analysis
in water distribution system under the scenarios.

topology transitions induced by the generated spatio-temporal
scenario. A topology transition at time t ∈T \{1} refers to a
change in the health status of power distribution lines between
time t −1 and t. Based on the outage and restoration times of
the damaged lines, a set of Rs + 1 sub-horizons are generated
for each scenario s ∈S, where Rs is the number of topology
transitions resulting from the outage/restoration events in the
scenario. Each sub-horizon, which starts at the time of the topol-
ogy transition and is initialized with the previous sub-horizon
results, may represent a normal condition where all power
distribution lines are operating (pre-contingency and restored
states) or faulted states with single or multiple line outages. The
solution of each sub-horizon corresponds to the reallocation of
power and water stored resources, given the new topology status
after the outage/restoration actions.

V. NUMERICAL STUDY
The proposed resilience analytics model for interdependent
powerandwaterdistributionsystemsisutilizedinthissectionfor
resilience assessment of a 15-node WDS interconnected with the
IEEE 33-bus test power distribution system [2], which is subject
to high speed wind that causes power outage in the test PDS, and
results in cascading outages in the test WDS. The diagram of
interdependent test PDS and WDS is shown in Fig. 4-(a), where
the pumps J1, J4, and J7 are supplied by buses 12, 24, and 30
of the test PDS, respectively. Two water tanks with maximum
capacity 1,200 m3 are operated at nodes 10 and 13 of the test
WDS, with initial stored water of respectively 1,000 m3 and 500
m3. Two controllable distributed generators are also available in
buses 16 and 22 of the test PDS. The power and water demand
proﬁles are shown in Fig. 4-(b), with peak values of 3.72 MW
and 1,188 m3/h for the PDS and WDS, respectively. The power
proﬁles are obtained from CAISO for March 29–30, 2017, and
the rest of the data for the 15-node WDS is available at [55].
For the spatio-temporal outage scenario generation, the set of
line outages at each time step t ∈T is determined by initially
calculating the failure probability πv
l,t of each line l ∈L for
a wind speed v. The line failure probability is modeled as a
function of the wind speed at the line location using piecewise
fragility curves [18], [20]. The wind speed is represented by a
Gaussian function scaled to a maximum wind speed of 78 mph,
with mean value equal to the expected disruption time, set for
hour 12, and a standard deviation of 2 hours. Once the failure
probability πv
l,t is determined, a uniformly distributed random
number rl,t is generated for each line l at time t and is compared
toπv

starting from t until the speciﬁed restoration time; otherwise, the
line operation remains in its normal condition. The restoration
time is sampled from a normal distribution with a mean value
equal to the mean-time-to-repair, which is assumed as 6 hours
with a standard deviation of 1 h for all lines.
Seven cases are studied for outage assessment and resilience
metrics evaluation of the test interdependent PDS and WDS in
Fig. 4. These cases consider different combinations of realistic
upgrades in the power and water distribution systems, repre-
sented by the inclusion of distributed power sources at different
locations and the increase of water storage capacity in tanks,
respectively. In Case 0, the operation of test PDS and WDS
is analyzed under 20 spatio-temporal single-equipment outage
scenarios, without DGs in PDS and restricting the water storage
in WDS tanks. Case 1 studies the operation of the systems under
the same scenarios in Case 0, with increased tank capacity of
1,200 m3 and initial volume in tanks 10 and 13 of 1,000 m3 and
500 m3, respectively. Case 2 extends the analysis of the effect of
the water storage to enhance the WDS resilience considering a
tank capacity of 2,500 m3 and initial volume 2,200 m3 and 1,900
m3, respectively. In Case 3, the response of the systems to more
severe disturbances is analyzed, where scenarios may contain
multiple outages to occur during the study window. Case 4 stud-
ies the effect of DGs on the resilience of the interdependent PDS
and WDS, while other conditions are the same as Case 3. Cases 5
and 6 evaluate the impact of DG location on the interdependence
between the resilience of power and water distribution systems.

As mentioned in Section II, urban WDSs are usually equipped

infrastructures.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed an analytics model to evaluate the cas-
cading effects of power outages on the operation of WDSs
and to quantify the interdependence between the resilience of
power and water distribution systems. The proposed analytics
model includes an outage analysis model to determine the power
serving capability of the PDS, and the resulting water serving
capability of the WDS, in response to external events disrupting
the grid operation. A set of six resilience metrics were deﬁned
to identify dependencies during the degradation and restoration
stages, which are calculated using the power and water serving
capabilities obtained from the outage analysis model. Numer-
ical studies were carried out on the IEEE 33-bus test power
distribution system, interconnected with a 15-node test water
distribution system. The results indicate that the increased water
tank capacity reduces the rate of decay of the water supply by
compensating the amount of water shortage during the PDS
outage. In addition, the allocation of DGs in PDS supplies
power during outages, which reduces the power interruptions of
pumps and the resulting water shortage in WDS. Although the
availability of additional water tank capacity in WDS reduces
the water shortage during power outages, the results indicate
that the impact of options to enhance the resilience of PDS,
such as utilizing DGs, is larger on enhancing the resilience of
WDS to power outages. In addition, the use of backup generation
weakens the coupling between the two infrastructures, mitigat-
ing the negative consequences of the power outages in the WDS
operation. Based on these results, the proposed metrics provide
a mechanism to evaluate potential upgrades in PDS and WDS
infrastructures and quantify their beneﬁts for the systems.
Future works include considering the impacts of other re-
medial actions by PDS, including network reconﬁguration, in
the proposed resilience analytics framework. Also, modeling
the impacts of emerging technologies that intensify the inter-
dependence of PDS on WDS, such as small pumped-storage
hydropower, are of interest. Although it is expected that these
technologies enhance the mutual resilience of both power and
water infrastructures, as WDS can support the PDS and reduce
power curtailments, the performance of the proposed metrics
to capture this effect in a bidirectional interdependent opera-
tion needs to be assessed. Another potential research area is
to develop the customer interruption cost function for water
distribution facilities, which would provide the outage cost for
a different water facility (such as pumping, desalination, and
treatment) as a function of outage duration.
