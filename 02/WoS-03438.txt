A B S T R A C T
The substantial energy consumption and operational costs in wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) necessitate 
effective energy management. While WWTPs are physically connected to the power distribution network (PDN) 
and the water distribution network (WDN) through the water-energy nexus, existing studies often overlook the 
interactions and coordination potential among these three independent stakeholders. In this regard, this paper 
proposes a coordinated scheduling strategy based on a tripartite game among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN. 
Specifically, a bi-level Stackelberg game framework is developed to capture their hierarchical decision-making 
process and competing interests. In this framework, the WDN plays a unique dual role, acting as a follower to 
the PDN in the upper-level game, and as a leader to the WWTP in the lower-level game. To solve the formulated 
model, a nested genetic algorithm (GA) is introduced. This approach is validated on an integrated system 
comprising an IEEE 33-bus PDN, a 12-node WDN, and a WWTP. Simulation results show that the nested GA 
reduces the solution time by 38% compared to conventional best-response iteration (BRI) methods. Furthermore, 
the tripartite game framework effectively balances the benefits of all entities, reducing the total operational cost 
by 28.5%, while decreasing the process energy consumption and relative carbon emissions of the WWTP by 2.6% 
and 1.3%, respectively.
1. Introduction
1.1. Motivation
Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are crucial municipal in­
frastructures in modern society, playing a significant role in improving 
urban environmental quality, supporting sustainable development, and 
facilitating water resource recycling [1,2]. With the rapid advancement 
of urbanization and increasing water demand in China, reclaimed water 
from wastewater treatment has become an indispensable supplementary 
water resource.
Wastewater treatment is inherently an energy-intensive process that 
involves substantial electricity consumption [3]. Specifically, electricity 
consumption for wastewater treatment typically ranges from 0.3 to 
0.6 kWh/m3 in most countries [4], accounting for approximately 3% to 
5% of the global electricity consumption [5]. Meanwhile, the waste­
water sector contributes approximately 1% to 2% of global carbon 
emissions [5], with total carbon emissions increasing by over 140% from 
2009 to 2023 [6]. In the context of China’s carbon neutrality and 
decarbonization targets, reducing energy consumption and mitigating 
carbon emissions in WWTPs have become urgent and critical challenges.
Addressing these multifaceted challenges necessitates a shift from 
plant-level optimization to a systemic perspective within the broader 
water-energy nexus. Equipped with on-site distributed energy resources 
(DERs) such as photovoltaics (PV), wind turbines, and battery energy 
storage systems (BESS), modern WWTPs act as prosumers within the 
power distribution network (PDN) and as the primary suppliers of 
reclaimed water to the water distribution network (WDN). This inter­
action creates a tripartite WWTP-PDN-WDN nexus. Therefore, a tripar­
tite coordinated scheduling strategy that integrates the operations of 
WWTP, PDN, and WDN is essential to fully exploit systemic synergies 
and achieve enhanced economic and environmental performance.

1.2.2. Interactions between WWTPs and external systems
Given the energy regulation potential of WWTPs, numerous studies 
have shifted focus toward their interaction with external infrastructures. 
Early research [14–17] primarily explored the economic feasibility of 
integrating PV systems and microbial fuel cell technology into WWTPs 
to enhance energy self-sufficiency and grid sustainability. With the 
utilization of biogas-to-energy pathways [18,19] and reclaimed water 
[20], the role of the WWTP has expanded beyond electricity to include 
interactions with natural gas networks and the WDN. However, in these 
studies, the WWTP is often treated as a passive resource or load inte­
grated into the external power, gas, or water networks, rather than as an 
independent decision-maker.
To date, only a limited number of studies have investigated the co­
ordinated operation between WWTPs and external systems. References 
[21] and [22] studied the coordinated economic dispatch of the WWTP 
and power systems while incorporating the internal process constraints 
of the plant. References [23] and [24] addressed the coordinated 
operation of power-gas and power-water systems involving WWTP en­
ergy consumption; however, the wastewater treatment processes were 
not explicitly formulated in their scheduling models. To the best of our 
knowledge, no existing literature has yet achieved simultaneous 
tripartite coordinated operation involving the WWTP, PDN, and WDN. 
Therefore, the development of a multi-stakeholder coordinated sched­
uling framework for the WWTP-PDN-WDN nexus remains a significant 
research gap.
1.2.3. Game theory applications in the water-energy nexus
To address the conflicts of interest and coordination challenges 
among multiple independent stakeholders, game theory provides a 
rigorous analytical framework for modeling strategic interactions. 
Among various models, the Stackelberg game is particularly well-suited 
for systems with hierarchical structures and sequential decision-making 
[25,26].
In the operation of energy systems, Stackelberg games are widely 
employed to coordinate interactions between system operators and 
participants. For instance, models have been developed to optimize 
service pricing and power management between IES operators (leaders) 
and end-users, as well as community-level IESs (followers) [27–30]. 
Similar paradigms have been proposed to coordinate an advanced vir­
tual power plant (leader) with distributed generation aggregators (fol­
lowers) [31], and a distribution company (leader) with citizen energy 
communities (followers) [32]. These studies demonstrate that 
game-theoretic scheduling can effectively maximize both the economic 
benefits of all stakeholders and the overall system efficiency.
Within the water-energy nexus, game theory has been increasingly 
applied to optimize the joint operation of interconnected infrastructure 
systems. Recent studies have employed bi-level and tri-level Stackelberg 
games to manage resource allocation among basin authorities, munic­
ipal water users, and energy providers [33–35]. In addition, Reference 
[36] proposed a non-cooperative hybrid game model, in which a 
Stackelberg game is adopted at the upper level to balance reservoir 
hydropower generation and urban water resource allocation. Specif­
ically, Reference [37] modeled the allocation of treated wastewater 
between sewage departments and regional districts to balance the 
transfer costs and water demand.
However, existing game-theoretic models often overlook the internal 
physical processes of the WWTP and the hierarchical interactions among 
the PDN, WDN, and WWTP. These unresolved challenges necessitate a 
more sophisticated game-theoretic framework to achieve systemic 
optimization in the water-energy nexus.
1.3. Research gaps and contributions
Based on the comprehensive literature review in Section 1.2, several 
critical research gaps are identified: 
• Most existing multi-energy coordination studies treat WWTPs as 
passive resources or loads integrated into external infrastructures, 
such as the PDN, WDN, or natural gas network. Consequently, the 
internal physical processes of wastewater treatment and the strategic 
decision-making behaviors of WWTPs are rarely modeled explicitly.
• While bilateral interactions (e.g., PDN-WWTP or PDN-WDN) have 
been investigated, a comprehensive framework that simultaneously 
captures the strategic interactions and conflicting interests among 
the PDN, WDN, and WWTP has yet to be established.
To bridge these gaps, this study proposes a bi-level Stackelberg game 
framework to enable coordinated operation among the WWTP, PDN, 
and WDN. A detailed comparison between the proposed approach and 
existing literature is summarized in Table 1.
The primary contributions of this work are as follows: 
• The WWTP is modeled as an active power prosumer and a reclaimed 
water supplier by explicitly incorporating detailed internal process 
constraints, such as aeration intensity, pumping energy, and sludge- 
to-biogas conversion, into the coordinated scheduling framework.
• A tripartite coordination architecture for the PDN-WDN-WWTP 
nexus is proposed to capture the cross-sector interactions between 
electricity supply, water distribution, and wastewater treatment.
• A hierarchical bi-level Stackelberg game-based strategy is developed 
to resolve stakeholder conflicts. It features a nested structure where 
the PDN acts as the primary leader and the WDN as a secondary 
leader in the lower-level game. This sequential framework effectively 
coordinates the strategic interactions among the PDN, WDN, and 
WWTP.

2. Water-energy nexus among WWTP, PDN, and WDN
Fig. 1 illustrates the tripartite coupling architecture, where the PDN, 
WDN, and WWTP are physically interconnected through multi- 
dimensional energy-water flows. The WWTP serves as the central hub 
of this integrated system. Internally, it is equipped with on-site PV sys­
tems and BESS to enhance operational self-sufficiency. A key feature of 
the facility is the sludge-to-biogas conversion process, where the pro­
duced biogas is utilized for on-site power generation. To maintain the 
optimal temperature for anaerobic digestion, an electric boiler and a 
thermal energy storage (TES) system are integrated into the thermal 
management module. The WWTP also interacts with the external nat­
ural gas network to balance its biogas supply through bidirectional 
exchange.
Beyond its internal operations, the WWTP is physically coupled with 
the PDN and WDN. The PDN provides supplemental electricity to power 
energy-intensive processes, such as aeration and pumping, while 
potentially receiving feedback power from the WWTP. Regarding the 
water-side coupling, the WWTP functions as a reliable supplier of 
reclaimed water for the WDN, thereby reducing freshwater extraction 
and linking the operational strategies of the two infrastructures. 
Furthermore, the WDN is intrinsically connected to the PDN through its 
electrically-driven water pumps. These intricate interdependencies form 
the physical foundation for the subsequent hierarchical game-theoretic 
scheduling framework.
3. Mathematical modeling of the tripartite stakeholders
This section formulates the mathematical scheduling models for the 
PDN, WDN, and WWTP. To maintain conciseness, the detailed technical 
constraints of the three stakeholders are provided in the online supple­
mentary materials [38], while the following subsections focus on their 
respective optimization objectives and key operational requirements.
3.1. WWTP

supplier, with the objective of minimizing its total net operational cost.

4. Hierarchical bi-level Stackelberg game framework and its 
solution
4.1. Game structure and interaction mechanism
Based on the individual optimization models developed in Section 3, 
this section defines the hierarchical interaction mechanism. As illus­
trated in Fig. 2, the coordination among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN is 
structured as a hierarchical bi-level Stackelberg game.
The PDN acts as the primary upper-level leader with a first-mover 
advantage, determining the internal electricity transaction prices 
(ζAE,t, ζEA,t, ζEW,t) to guide the energy consumption and generation 
patterns of the lower stakeholders. Within the lower level, a nested 
leader-follower relationship is established to manage the coupling be­
tween the WWTP and WDN. While both the WDN and WWTP simulta­
neously respond to the PDN’s electricity price signals, the WDN further 
serves as a secondary leader for the reclaimed water market. Specif­
ically, the WDN determines the water purchase price (ζAW,t) and the 
required electricity (PEW,j,t) from the PDN. The WWTP, acting as the 
ultimate follower, optimizes its internal scheduling to satisfy both the 
PDN’s energy requirements and the WDN’s water demand.
This “Price-Response” sequence forms a feedback loop where energy 
and water flows are transmitted back to the upper tiers to ensure 
strategy consistency. The game reaches a Stackelberg equilibrium when 
no participant can further reduce its operational cost by unilaterally 
changing its strategy.
4.2. Solution methodology
The hierarchical Stackelberg game formulated in Section 4.1 pre­
sents significant computational challenges. Conventionally, such bi- 
level problems are solved by transforming them into single-level pro­
grams via Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. However, since the 
proposed game model involves nonlinear hydraulic constraints in the 
WDN and discrete on/off decisions for pumps in the WWTP, KKT-based 
approaches are difficult to apply directly.
Alternatively, the best-response iteration (BRI, also known as fixed- 
point iteration) algorithm is commonly adopted to solve bi-level prob­
lems by iteratively updating the upper- and lower-level decisions. Spe­
cifically, at each iteration, the lower-level optimization is solved given 
the fixed upper-level decisions, after which the leader’s strategy is 
updated based on the followers’ responses. This process is repeated until 
convergence is achieved. Although the BRI method is straightforward to 
implement, it relies heavily on the initial solution and may suffer from 
poor convergence or numerical oscillations when the coupling among 
stakeholders is strong and the problem is non-convex, as is the case in 
this study.
To address these limitations, a nested genetic algorithm (GA) 
framework is developed, as summarized in Algorithm 1. The outer GA 
corresponds to the upper-level PDN decision-making problem shown in 
Fig. 2, while the inner GA solves the lower-level Stackelberg game be­
tween the WDN and the WWTP. In the outer GA loop, the PDN optimizes 
its 24-hour pricing strategies (ζAE,t, ζEA,t, ζEW,t) to minimize its opera­
tional cost. The resulting electricity price profiles are then passed to the 
inner GA, where the WDN optimizes the reclaimed water price (ζAW,t). 
Given the electricity and reclaimed water prices, the WWTP and WDN 
determine their optimal operational responses, including reclaimed 
water supply (qAW,t) and power exchanges with the PDN (PEW,j,t, PAE,t, 
PEA,t). The reciprocal of the WDN operational cost is adopted as the 
fitness of the inner-layer individual. Once the inner GA converges, the 
equilibrium power exchange responses (PEW,j,t, PAE,t, PEA,t) are returned 
to the outer GA to evaluate the outer-layer individuals’ fitness, defined 
as the reciprocal of the PDN operational cost. Through this nested 
structure, the PDN pricing strategy fully accounts for the coordinated 
responses of the WDN and WWTP.
Note that although multiple degenerate optimal responses (ties) and

5.2. Algorithm performance analysis
To verify the efficacy of the proposed nested GA, the Stackelberg 
game model is solved using both the GA and the benchmark BRI method. 
For the latter, two distinct initialization strategies are evaluated: Strat­
egy A1, which utilizes the boundaries of the electricity price interval as 
starting points, and Strategy A2, which employs the interval midpoint 
values. In addition, the nested GA is tested through multiple indepen­
dent runs with different random seeds to examine the consistency of the 
obtained equilibrium. The comparative performance metrics are sum­
marized in Table 2, and the operating cost convergence curves of the 
stakeholders are presented in Fig. 4.
Table 2 highlights the BRI’s oscillatory behavior and acute sensitivity 
to initial values, which lead to inconsistent iteration counts. Despite 
these fluctuations, all three methods yield highly consistent results, with 
a maximum relative error of only 0.09%. While the BRI suffers from 
initialization sensitivity, the nested GA utilizes fitness-driven optimiza­
tion to accelerate convergence and reduce computational burden. 
Consequently, the nested GA effectively meets the stringent accuracy 
and efficiency demands of practical day-ahead scheduling.
Fig. 4 illustrates the distinct convergence patterns of the game’s 
stakeholders. As iterations progress, PDN operating costs decrease while 
WWTP costs gradually increase. This trend reflects the leader-follower 
dynamics inherent in Stackelberg games, underscoring the dominant 
role of the PDN as the leader.
Consequently, the consistent convergence of the nested GA and the 
BRI-based algorithms starting from distinct initializations also 
demonstrates that the proposed bi-level game successfully converges to 
a unique and stable equilibrium.
5.3. System-level performance analysis of operational modes
The system performance is evaluated under four operational modes, 
which are described as follows:
Mode 1: The WWTP, PDN, and WDN operate independently. The 
WWTP follows fixed electricity and water prices as specified in [38].
Mode 2: The electricity pricing scheme in Mode 1 is modified ac­
cording to Table 3 in [38], while other conditions remain unchanged.
Mode 3: The water price in Mode 1 is adjusted by reducing the price 
at node W10 to 4.7 CNY/m3.
Mode 4: The proposed hierarchical bi-level Stackelberg game 
framework is implemented.
5.3.1. Economic performance analysis
Table 3 summarizes the scheduling results across the four opera­
tional modes. In Modes 1–3, subjective pricing schemes cause uneven 
cost fluctuations among entities, failing to ensure economic fairness. 
Conversely, the proposed Mode 4 significantly outperforms the baseline 
(Mode 1), reducing the total operational cost by 28.5%. Specifically, for 
the WWTP, Mode 4 decreases process energy consumption, carbon 
emissions, and PV curtailment by 2.6%, 1.3%, and 22.2%, respectively. 
These metrics validate the effectiveness of the proposed Stackelberg 
game framework in minimizing operational costs while securing 
tangible benefits in energy conservation and emission reduction.
5.3.2. Electricity and water pricing analysis
This section evaluates the impact of water and electricity pricing on 
individual stakeholders by comparing the outcomes of Modes 1–3. Fig. 5
illustrates the transaction volumes among the participants. For clarity, 
sold and purchased volumes are represented as positive and negative, 
respectively.
Fig. 5(a) illustrates the operational dynamics under Mode 1. The 
WWTP strategically adjusts its treatment processes and BESS to capi­
talize on price fluctuations. Specifically, it maximizes electricity sales to 
the PDN during peak pricing periods and increases power purchases 
during valley periods. Notably, during the peak pricing window (e.g., 
hours 19 to 22), the WWTP achieves temporary energy self-sufficiency 
by reducing secondary and tertiary treatment flows while simulta­
neously dispatching the BESS and biogas generators.
Under the earlier and extended peak pricing of Mode 2, the plant 
increases its electricity procurement during valley hours 3 to 6 and 
advances its electricity sales between hours 7 and 9. This operational 
adjustment enables the plant to maintain sustained energy self- 
sufficiency from hours 19 to 22, whereas Mode 1 requires a swift re­
turn to power purchases to meet internal energy demands. Ultimately, 
these behavioral shifts highlight the critical interdependence between 
the operational decisions of the WWTP and the electricity pricing signals 
from the PDN.
Compared to Mode 1, Mode 3 solely reduces the nodal water price 
while maintaining baseline electricity interactions. Driven by this cost 
reduction, the WDN significantly increases its reclaimed water pur­
chases to meet demand, a growth explicitly shown in Fig. 5(b). Conse­
quently, the increased wastewater treatment workload requires 
additional power, marginally raising the overall electricity procurement 
of the WWTP relative to Mode 1.

5.4. Impact of game structure
In practice, large- and medium-sized WWTPs often serve as stable 
water sources for the WDN, enabling them to influence the scheduling 
behavior of the WDN. Consequently, WWTPs may act as secondary 
leaders within the Stackelberg framework. To investigate this hierar­
chical structure, this section introduces Mode 5 and compares it with 
Mode 4. Under Mode 5, the PDN, WWTP, and WDN act as the primary 
leader, secondary leader, and follower, respectively.
The main scheduling results under Mode 5 are shown in Table 4. By 
assuming the secondary leader role, the WWTP reduces its operating 
cost compared to Mode 4, whereas the WDN cost increases. These out­
comes demonstrate that the game hierarchy directly dictates individual 
financial returns. Given the strategic advantage of leaders, the game 
framework must be carefully selected based on the actual roles and 
operational status of each stakeholder in the market.

water-energy nexus involving the WWTP, PDN, and WDN. By explicitly 
modelling the internal physical processes of the WWTP and the cross- 
sector couplings, a hierarchical bi-level Stackelberg game framework 
is developed to address the conflicting interests of independent stake­
holders. The main conclusions are summarized as follows: 
• The proposed bi-level Stackelberg game framework effectively co­
ordinates the operation of the WWTP, PDN, and WDN through 
electricity and water price interactions. Compared with independent 
operation, the proposed coordination strategy significantly reduces 
total operating costs, energy consumption, and carbon emissions. It 
also balances the economic benefits of individual participants while 
enhancing the overall sustainability of the integrated water-energy 
system.
• The hierarchical game structure has a significant impact on the 
benefits of each participant. As leaders in the game, the PDN and 
WDN (as a sub-leader) can guide the behavior of followers through 
pricing mechanisms. This framework mitigates the adverse effects of 
arbitrary pricing decisions and enables more flexible operation of the 
followers, thereby improving overall system performance.
• The nested GA proves to be an effective solution method for the 
proposed bi-level game. Compared with the BRI method, the nested 
GA achieves comparable solution accuracy while significantly 
reducing computation time. Moreover, it avoids dependence on 
initial values, demonstrating better robustness and suitability for 
day-ahead scheduling applications.
From a practical perspective, the proposed framework provides a 
feasible approach for coordinating independently operated water and 
energy infrastructures through pricing-based interactions, thereby sup­
porting the low-carbon and economically efficient operation of future 
smart urban systems with increasing water-energy interactions.
However, this study does not consider uncertainties in renewable 
energy generation and wastewater inflow. Future work will incorporate 
uncertainty modeling and investigate cooperative strategies among the 
three stakeholders to further enhance system performance.
