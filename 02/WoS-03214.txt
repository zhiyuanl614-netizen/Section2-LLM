A B S T R A C T
As the global community accelerates zero-carbon transitions toward 2030, decarbonizing energy-intensive 
wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) operating within the water-energy nexus has become an urgent 
imperative. Integrating renewable energy and flexible resource recovery into WWTPs has therefore emerged 
as a promising pathway to reduce operating costs and carbon emissions. While existing literature optimizes 
WWTPs integrated with photovoltaics (PV), scheduling studies on utilizing CO2 hydrogenation for methanol 
synthesis to enhance circular resource recovery and support denitrification remain scarce. Furthermore, 
coordinated scheduling models for WWTPs, power distribution networks (PDNs), and water distribution 
networks (WDNs) often overlook autonomous decision-making, rely on oversimplified WWTP constraints, and 
lack cross-sector pricing mechanisms to mitigate the WDN’s cost burden for reclaimed water. To address 
these gaps, this paper proposes a day-ahead scheduling model for a PV-integrated WWTP, incorporating 
CO2 hydrogenation for methanol synthesis to support biological nitrogen removal and drive sustainable low-
carbon operations. Based on water-energy coupling, an asymmetric Nash bargaining model is established to 
facilitate tripartite cooperation among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN, introducing an electricity-water ratio-
based bargaining power calculation to ensure equitable benefit allocation. Simulations on a 60,000 m3/d 
WWTP, an IEEE 33-node PDN, and a 12-node WDN demonstrate that on-site methanol synthesis reduces 
accounted operational carbon emissions and enhances PV self-consumption. Concurrently, tripartite scheduling 
substantially lowers total operational costs and optimizes reclaimed water pricing.
Introduction
As the global community accelerates toward a zero-carbon future 
by 2030, decarbonizing energy-intensive urban infrastructures has be-
come an urgent imperative. Within the urban water-energy nexus, 
wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) serve as critical infrastructure 
for safeguarding water circulation and ecological balance, with treated 
reclaimed water emerging as a vital resource (Beijing Water Author-
ity, 2024). However, wastewater treatment is an inherently energy-
intensive process, and its high operational costs coupled with car-
bon emissions have become bottlenecks constraining the industry’s 
green development (Ma et al., 2022). From an energy consumption 
perspective, electricity used in wastewater treatment accounts for ap-
proximately 3% to 4% of total electricity consumption in developed 
countries (National Energy Conservation Center, 2015). This not only 
constitutes the primary operational cost for WWTPs but also generates

Currently, numerous studies have focused on the scheduling and 
operation of novel WWTPs. Shao et al. (2024) investigated the coor-
dinated scheduling of treatment equipment, such as blowers, with PV 
within WWTPs. Zhao et al. (2025) proposed a day-ahead scheduling 
method for PV-integrated WWTPs. Considering the stepwise wastew-
ater treatment process and associated direct carbon emissions, Zhao 
et al. (2024) proposed a water-energy-carbon coordinated scheduling 
model for PV-integrated WWTPs. Liu et al. (2022) focused on WWTPs 
equipped with biogas-fueled combined heat and power units, heat 
pumps, and tailwater generation units, analyzing the impact of optimal 
scheduling on energy conservation and carbon reduction. Beyond oper-
ational scheduling, Abedini et al. (2022) proposed an electrochemical 
device for converting CO2 into methanol, aiming to effectively manage 
CO2 emissions from WWTPs. The aforementioned literature demon-
strates that novel WWTPs, characterized by multi-energy coupling and 
integrated generation, grid, load, and storage operations, significantly 
outperform traditional plants in terms of economic and environmental 
benefits. However, the optimization potential of individual WWTPs re-
mains limited; therefore, exploring cross-sector coordinated scheduling 
is essential for advancing urban sustainable development.
Ali et al. (2020) evaluated the impact of load shifting characteristics 
and biogas power regulation in WWTPs on reducing power distribution 
network (PDN) operating costs and installed capacity. Zhao et al. 
(2025) and Kirchem et al. (2018) discussed the regulation flexibility 
of aeration processes, water pumps, and water storage facilities, as 
well as the feasibility and strategies for their participation in electricity 
demand response. Assuming that the WWTP and the PDN operate under 
a unified dispatch center, Moazeni and Khazaei (2021) proposed an 
integrated scheduling model that accounts for treatment processes and 
water quality targets. Khatavkar and Mays (2019) utilized reclaimed 
water and water distribution networks (WDNs) as cooling sources 
for power plants, proposing a coordinated WWTP-WDN scheduling 
method. By integrating reclaimed water into WDNs, Oikonomou and 
Parvania (2020) and Joshi and Mohagheghi (2020) investigated the 
integrated scheduling of the WWTP-PDN-WDN system (Oikonomou & 
Parvania, 2020) and the coordinated scheduling of wastewater treat-
ment equipment, WDNs, and microgrids (Joshi & Mohagheghi, 2020), 
respectively.
Although previous studies have enhanced energy and resource uti-
lization through the coordinated scheduling of wastewater treatment 
and energy equipment, as well as sludge-to-biogas conversion (Liu 
et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024, 2025) and reclaimed water recycling 
(Joshi & Mohagheghi, 2020; Oikonomou & Parvania, 2020; Zhao et al., 
2024), there remains limited research regarding the coupling mecha-
nisms of novel processes within scheduling frameworks. For instance, 
external methanol addition is typically required in the biochemical 
treatment process to adjust the Carbon-to-Nitrogen (C/N) ratio, thereby 
facilitating denitrification and improving nutrient removal efficiency 
(Liu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). Abedini et al. (2022) introduced a 
novel process for CO2 hydrogenation for methanol synthesis. Integrat-
ing this process with carbon capture from biogas generation and oxygen 
supply from electrolyzers (to reduce aeration energy) into a day-ahead 
scheduling model holds the promise of further elevating economic and 
low-carbon benefits. However, to the best of our knowledge, no such 
study has been reported to date. Furthermore, literature addressing the 
tripartite coordinated scheduling of WWTPs, PDNs, and WDNs is scarce 
[16, 17]. Specifically, Oikonomou and Parvania (2020) only considered 
water pressure and flow constraints in the reverse osmosis stage, while 
Joshi and Mohagheghi (2020) merely accounted for power demand and 
water storage constraints. Consequently, the modeling of the complex 
wastewater treatment process in these studies remains oversimplified. 
Moreover, both Oikonomou and Parvania (2020) and Joshi and Mo-
hagheghi (2020) adopt centralized scheduling models under a single 
decision-maker, rendering them inapplicable to scenarios where the 
WWTP, PDN, and WDN operate as distinct entities with autonomous 
decision-making capabilities. Crucially, WWTPs and WDNs typically 
operate under an unbundled operation mode, managed by distinct cor-
porate entities. Although reclaimed water offers environmental and 
freshwater-substitution benefits, its procurement cost may exceed that 
of tap water due to the additional treatment and monitoring required 
to meet reuse standards. Government subsidies for reclaimed water 
procurement are customarily settled on an annual basis. Consequently, 
the temporal mismatch between immediate operational costs and de-
layed subsidy receipts imposes significant financial pressure on WDNs 
during the day-ahead scheduling horizon, thereby dampening their 
willingness to pay for reclaimed water. In this context, designing a ra-
tional transaction-pricing mechanism for reclaimed water transactions 
between the WDN and the WWTP is pivotal to ensuring the smooth 
operation of the reclaimed water supply chain. However, the price 
formation and coordination mechanisms specific to this transaction 
have received scant attention in existing literature.
In response to these challenges, this paper proposes a coordi-
nated scheduling framework for a PV-integrated WWTP, PDN, and 
WDN alliance based on asymmetric Nash bargaining. Specifically, a 
novel WWTP configuration incorporating the CO2 hydrogenation for 
methanol synthesis process is introduced to enhance internal energy 
and resource flexibility. Building upon the water-energy nexus, a multi-
entity cooperative game model is established to achieve system-wide 
optimization while ensuring individual interests. It should be empha-
sized that this study aims to develop a day-ahead water-energy-carbon
coordinated scheduling model for the WWTP-PDN-WDN alliance, with 
emphasis on energy-consumption characteristics and dispatchable op-
erational decisions. Therefore, the wastewater treatment process is 
represented at the hourly scheduling level, and detailed biological 
mechanisms, including dynamic biological kinetics, sludge-age evo-
lution, nutrient concentration propagation, and microbial community

Theory and calculation: PV-integrated WWTP with methanol syn-
thesis system
Process flow and structure of the PV-integrated WWTP
The WWTP investigated in this paper is not a conventional
discharge-oriented plant, but a reclaimed-water-oriented plant that 
supplies municipal-grade reclaimed water to the WDN. Therefore, as 
illustrated in Fig. 1, tertiary advanced treatment is included after 
primary physical treatment and secondary biological treatment. The 
variables depicted in Fig. 1 (e.g., 𝑞𝑖𝑛) represent the flow rates at 
various stages, with detailed descriptions provided in Nomenclature. 
Primary treatment primarily involves units such as the screen, the 
regulating tank, the lift pump, and the primary clarifier. Specifically, 
the screen is employed to intercept suspended solids, the regulating 
tank serves to stabilize and homogenize water quality, and the lift 
pump elevates the wastewater to the primary clarifier for further 
sedimentation and filtration. Secondary treatment widely adopts the 
Anaerobic-Anoxic-Oxic process, utilizing microorganisms to remove 
organic matter, nitrogen, and phosphorus. Notably, this stage generates 
carbon emissions due to the release of CH4 and N2O. The core units 
of this stage are the biochemical tank; in particular, the aerobic tank, 
also referred to as the aeration tank, requires a blower system to 
supply oxygen, thereby maintaining the dissolved oxygen concentration 
necessary to facilitate microbial degradation of organic matter. The 
outflow from the biochemical tank enters the secondary clarifier for 
sludge-water separation. Although the supernatant following secondary 
treatment still contains trace amounts of microorganisms, nitrogen, and 
phosphorus, it generally meets standard discharge requirements. Since 
the settled sludge possesses significant biochemical activity, a portion 
is recirculated to the biochemical tank via return pumps, while the 
excess activated sludge is collected together with the primary sludge 
and sent to the anaerobic biogas digester. The biogas produced from 
anaerobic digestion is used for power generation, whereas the residual 
digestate is assumed to be dewatered and transported off-site as sludge 
cake. Since sludge dewatering, transportation, and final disposal are 
not hourly dispatchable processes, they are outside the day-ahead 
scheduling boundary of this study. Finally, tertiary treatment processes 
the wastewater according to municipal water standards. Techniques 
such as adsorption, electrodialysis, and reverse osmosis are employed 
to deeply remove residual organic matter, nitrogen, and phosphorus 
from the secondary effluent. Ultimately, the tertiary effluent serves as 
a reclaimed water source injected into the WDN.
External methanol addition is required in the secondary biochemical 
treatment stage to facilitate denitrification and enhance the efficiency 
of nitrogen and phosphorus removal. The methanol synthesis system 
comprises an electrolyzer, a carbon capture unit, and a methanol 
synthesis unit. Specifically, the electrolyzer generates hydrogen and

The schematic diagram of the PV-integrated WWTP is presented in

sludge to produce biogas for electricity generation. To meet the tem-
perature requirements for biogas fermentation, an electric boiler and 
a thermal storage tank are configured to supply heat to the biogas 
digester. Evidently, the PV-integrated WWTP functions as a micro-
integrated energy system characterized by the mutual coupling and 
interaction of electricity, gas, heat, water, and carbon flows.

Day-ahead scheduling model for the PV-integrated WWTP with methanol 
synthesis
Given the day-ahead forecasts of influent flow rate, influent water 
quality, PV generation, ambient conditions, and market prices, the 
proposed day-ahead scheduling model for the PV-integrated WWTP 
with the methanol synthesis system solves for the hourly decision 
variables of the plant. The controlled decision variables in this schedul-
ing model include wastewater flow allocation, tank storage variations, 
operating states and outputs of treatment equipment, outputs of energy-
conversion and resource-recovery devices, charging/discharging sched-
ules of the battery and thermal storage tank, and electricity/reclaimed-
water transaction quantities, subject to water-quality requirements, 
multi-energy balance equations, equipment operating limits, and op-
erational carbon-accounting constraints.
The wastewater treatment process is represented at the hourly 
scheduling level, consistent with the focus of this study on WWTP 
energy management and dispatchable operational decisions rather 
than detailed biological and water-quality state dynamics. Specifi-
cally, hydraulic flexibility is captured by tank-storage dynamics and 
flow-rate constraints, while water-quality requirements are represented 
by BOD/TKN-related oxygen demand and effluent-quality constraints. 
The model retains the process mechanisms that directly affect hourly 
energy-management and scheduling decisions, including pollutant-
load-related oxygen demand, blower power consumption, methanol ad-
dition, electrolysis oxygen supply, sludge-to-biogas conversion, carbon 
capture, and tertiary reclaimed-water production. Detailed biological 
and water-quality dynamics, including dynamic biological kinetics, 
sludge-age evolution, nutrient concentration fluctuations, microbial 
community dynamics, and concentration propagation inside treatment 
units, are not explicitly resolved; instead, they are represented through

Coordinated scheduling model for the WWTP-PDN-WDN alliance
Water-energy nexus in coordinated scheduling
The water-energy nexus among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN is 
illustrated in Fig. 3. The boundaries of the three autonomous entities 
are defined according to ownership and operational decision-making. 
The WWTP boundary covers the wastewater treatment units and the 
coupled energy-conversion devices inside the plant, including PV gen-
eration, battery storage, anaerobic digestion, biogas generation, carbon 
capture, electrolysis, and methanol synthesis. The PDN boundary covers 
the distribution network, distributed wind generation, electrical loads, 
and the upstream-grid interface. The WDN boundary covers the water 
distribution pipelines, pumps, water source, and water demand nodes. 
The cross-boundary interactions considered in this paper include bidi-
rectional electricity trading between the WWTP and PDN, electricity 
supply from the PDN to WDN pumps, and reclaimed-water supply 
from the WWTP to the WDN. Specifically, the WWTP engages in

bidirectional electricity exchange with the PDN and supplies reclaimed 
water meeting municipal water quality standards to the WDN, while 
a unidirectional power flow drives the WDN’s pumps from the PDN. 
Although the WWTP also connects to an external gas network, given 
its weak coupling with the WDN, the gas network is excluded from this 
analysis. Consequently, this paper focuses specifically on the interest 
correlations among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN, which constitute a 
tripartite game for coordinated scheduling.
In the coordinated framework, scheduling and pricing have differ-
ent meanings. Scheduling determines the physical operating decisions 
and transaction quantities, such as equipment power, pump operation, 
electricity exchange, and reclaimed-water supply, over the 24-hour 
horizon. Pricing refers to the settlement prices used for electricity and 
reclaimed-water transactions among autonomous entities. In this study, 
pricing has two forms. Exogenous price parameters, such as time-of-use 
(TOU) electricity prices, benchmark reclaimed-water prices, methanol 
prices, and biogas prices, are used as input data for operating cost 
calculation. In contrast, the internal electricity and reclaimed-water 
settlement prices among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN are endogenous 
results of the Nash bargaining-based cooperative benefit distribution 
subproblem.
Nash bargaining is a prevalent cooperative game theory that has 
been extensively applied to address multi-entity cooperation and ben-
efit allocation issues. Typical application scenarios include microgrids 
(Z. Zhang et al., 2024), integrated energy systems (Wu et al., 2024), 
virtual power plant coalitions (Gougheri et al., 2022), load aggregators 
(Wang et al., 2024) and building-integrated photovoltaic systems (Liu 
et al., 2026). This paper specifically focuses on the cooperation among 
the WWTP, PDN, and WDN. It is assumed that the three entities form 
a cooperative alliance to achieve a mutually beneficial outcome via 
price-quantity negotiation and coordinated scheduling. Different from 
an additive social-welfare objective, the Nash bargaining objective 
maximizes the weighted Nash product of the cooperative surplus of 
all participants. Let 𝑆𝑖= 𝐶0
𝑖−𝐶𝑖 denote the cost reduction obtained 
by entity 𝑖 relative to its independent operation, i.e., its disagreement 
point. The multiplicative form is adopted because it jointly reflects 
system-wide cooperation and individual rationality: if any participant 
obtains zero or very small surplus, the Nash product becomes zero 
or is significantly reduced. Therefore, the solution cannot be highly 
evaluated only because a single entity achieves a large cost reduction 
while the other entities gain little benefit. In contrast, an additive 
objective mainly maximizes the total surplus and may overlook the 
balance of benefit allocation among autonomous entities.
As illustrated in Fig. 3, the objective of each entity is to minimize 
its respective operating cost. The WWTP scheduling model in Eq. (40) 
describes the hourly operation of treatment equipment, coupled energy-
conversion devices, storage units, and electricity/reclaimed-water
transactions under water-quality and multi-energy balance constraints. 
The PDN is described by the DistFlow model (Wang et al., 2023) to rep-
resent radial distribution-network power flow, distributed generation, 
wind curtailment, upstream-grid purchase, and electricity exchange 
with the WWTP and WDN. The WDN model describes hydraulic con-
straints, pump operation, water-source supply, water demand, and 
reclaimed-water intake from the WWTP (Li, 2021). Therefore, the three 
subsystem models provide the physical basis for the WWTP-PDN-WDN
coordinated scheduling problem. Detailed PDN and WDN models are 
provided in Appendix A.

Solution procedure
The solution procedure for the coordinated scheduling problem of 
the WWTP, PDN, and WDN is summarized in Table 1. The calculation 
process consists of four sequential steps. First, each entity solves its 
independent operation model to determine the disagreement point. 
Second, the alliance cost minimization subproblem is solved to ob-
tain equipment schedules and electricity/reclaimed-water transaction 
quantities. Third, the bargaining weights are calculated according to 
the converged transaction quantities. Finally, the cooperative benefit 
distribution subproblem is solved to determine the internal electricity 
and reclaimed-water settlement prices. The detailed A-ADMM update 
equations are provided in Appendix B.
In the A-ADMM iterations, convergence is evaluated by both primal 
and dual residuals. The primal residual measures the mismatch between 
the electricity/reclaimed-water transaction decisions of two interacting 
entities, while the dual residual reflects the variation of the exchanged 
variables between two consecutive iterations. The iteration is termi-
nated only when both residuals satisfy the prescribed convergence 
thresholds. In addition, the penalty parameter is adaptively adjusted 
according to the relative magnitudes of the primal and dual residu-
als, which helps balance feasibility restoration and solution stability.

are analyzed in this section. The A-ADMM iterations of Scheme 5 
are summarized in Fig. 16, where P1 denotes the scheduling and 
transaction-quantity optimization subproblem, and P2 denotes the in-

As shown in Fig. 16, the two A-ADMM subproblems both reach 
convergence under their prescribed primal and dual residual tolerances.

For P1, the primal and dual residual tolerances are both set to 10−2. Fig. 
16(a) shows that the cost trajectories of the WWTP, PDN, WDN, and the 
total alliance gradually stabilize during the scheduling and transaction-
quantity optimization process. P1 converges after 54 iterations, with a 
total computational time of 590.22 s. Although P1 requires fewer iter-

well as their expected transaction quantities; therefore, each iteration 
is computationally more demanding.
For P2, the primal and dual residual tolerances are both set to 10−3.

are settlement prices, whose numerical magnitudes are smaller than

convergence after 87 iterations, with a total computational time of 
136.79 s. Although P2 requires more iterations, it only optimizes the 
transaction prices for cooperative benefit distribution, and thus each 
iteration is relatively simple.

solution can stably coordinate both physical scheduling decisions and 
cooperative benefit allocation. During the solution process, the WWTP, 
PDN, and WDN only need to exchange their expected transaction quan-
tities in P1 and expected transaction prices in P2. Detailed equipment 
parameters, user data, network topology, and other private operational

Scalability and solver robustness
The scalability of the proposed WWTP-PDN-WDN coordinated
scheduling model is mainly supported by the mathematical tractability 
of the subsystem models. For the PDN, the DistFlow-based power-
flow model is transformed into a second-order cone programming 
problem through second-order cone relaxation (Wang et al., 2023). 
When the number of distribution-network buses increases, the number 
of power-flow variables and network constraints increases accordingly, 
but the conic optimization structure of the PDN model remains un-
changed. For the WDN, after introducing the flow-direction variable, 
the bilinear hydraulic terms are handled using McCormick envelope 
techniques (Li, 2021), thereby reformulating the WDN model as a 
mixed-integer convex optimization problem. Similarly, when the num-
ber of water-network nodes or pipes increases, the number of hydraulic 
variables and constraints increases, while the mixed-integer convex 
structure of the WDN model is preserved. For the WWTP, nonlinear 
and bilinear relationships in modules such as temperature-dependent 
anaerobic digestion, blower aeration, and BOD mass-load calculation 
are represented by SOS2-based piecewise linear interpolation when 
necessary. Therefore, the WWTP model is also transformed into a 
tractable mixed-integer optimization formulation.
With the above tractability treatments, the local models of the PDN, 
WDN, and WWTP can be solved by mature commercial solvers such 
as Gurobi and Mosek. Therefore, the proposed coordinated scheduling 
model remains applicable to larger urban water-energy systems with 
more distribution-network buses or water-network nodes, although the 
computational time may increase with the number of variables, integer 
variables, and network constraints. In the reported simulations, all local 
subproblems were solved successfully, and the A-ADMM residuals con-
verged within the prescribed tolerances, indicating acceptable solver 
robustness for the tested WWTP-PDN-WDN system.
Conclusion
This study investigates a PV-integrated WWTP utilizing CO2 hydro-
genation for methanol synthesis and proposes a day-ahead scheduling 
model for the WWTP, together with an asymmetric Nash bargaining 
model for coordinated scheduling among the WWTP, PDN, and WDN. A 
joint coupled system, consisting of a medium-sized WWTP, the IEEE 33-
node PDN, and a 12-node WDN, is employed as a test case to verify the 
effectiveness of the scheduling models. The simulation results indicate 
that:
• The CO2 hydrogenation for methanol synthesis process reduces 
the WWTP operating cost, facilitates PV consumption, and in-
creases carbon trading revenue, demonstrating economic and 
low-carbon advantages.
• Compared with the independent operation of the WWTP, PDN, 
and WDN, Nash bargaining reduces the total social cost by 
32.98% while improving the benefits of all three entities, achiev-
ing coordination between individual and collective interests.
• The proposed electricity-to-water ratio-based bargaining-weight 
method converts tertiary-treated reclaimed-water transactions 
into electricity-equivalent quantities. Compared with equal-
weight Nash bargaining and the separated multi-resource weight-
ing method in Q. Zhang et al. (2024), it avoids fixed water-
side weights under unidirectional water trading and better re-
flects seasonal changes in entity contributions, thereby reducing 
the reclaimed-water settlement price and mitigating the WDN’s 
short-term cash flow pressure.
It should be noted that the proposed WWTP model is developed for 
hourly energy management and day-ahead water-energy-carbon coordi-
nated scheduling, rather than for detailed biochemical process control. 
Therefore, dynamic biological kinetics, sludge-age evolution, nutrient 
concentration fluctuations, microbial community dynamics, and con-
centration propagation inside treatment units are simplified in the 
present model. Future work will further investigate how these dynamic 
wastewater-treatment processes can be represented in a form compat-
ible with hourly day-ahead scheduling, thereby enhancing the process 
fidelity of scheduling models for energy management of WWTPs and 
coordinated operation.
