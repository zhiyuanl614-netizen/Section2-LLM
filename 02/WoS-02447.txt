A B S T R A C T
Although water and energy systems are interdependent, they are still designed individually due to their
complexity. This paper proposes a multiple input multiple output (MIMO) model predictive control (MPC) for
demand management of an integrated water distribution system (WDS) supplied by an islanded microgrid (MG)
system, offering an additional opportunity for energy-saving at the WDS. This strategy benefits the island and
remote communities, which have limited access to the centralized electric grid. The existing literature either
used an open-loop approach for energy management of water-energy microgrids or ignored the interconnected
nature and MIMO nature of these systems. Compared to that, the proposed closed-loop energy management
system is developed to address the detailed dynamics of assets, the inherent interconnections between water
and energy networks, and to provide resilience as well as energy saving and demand management capabilities.
The integrated MPC algorithm is designed in discrete time, utilizing a state-space model and sequential
quadratic programming (SQP) as the solution method. Considering the intensive use of electricity by the
pumps in WDS, their energy consumption is accommodated by an islanded microgrid system that incorporates
a diesel generator, renewable energy sources (wind and solar), and a battery bank that serves as an energy
source and a storage device. The proposed approach is investigated using base water demand from a real-world
community, Telford, in Pennsylvania. Several case studies, such as variations in renewable energy contributions
and increments of the control actions, are carried out to demonstrate the feasibility and reliability of the
integrated MPC to provide efficient water and energy demand management concurrently.
1. Introduction
In view of its significant impact on the day-to-day functioning of
society and the economy, the water system is described to be one
of the critical infrastructure systems that need to be well understood
and controlled to enhance its resiliency. The water system is also
closely linked to the energy sector, as it contributes to high energy
intensity. A significant portion of the operating costs for water systems
is accounted for energy consumption, estimated at 40% [1], with water
distribution system (WDS) contributing the most [2] due to pumps’
electrical usage [3]. Therefore, water and energy systems will need to
be coordinated effectively and efficiently. By leveraging the interdepen-
dencies of the water-energy systems, an optimal control strategy can be
designed that will result in better water-energy demand management
and a reduction of energy consumption and operating costs. Microgrid
(MG) is a local energy solution that can be utilized to manage the
water demand and energy distribution in local and island communities,
which can result in lower carbon emission and operational costs, and
enhanced energy management [4,5]. The components of an MG system

A number of co-optimization studies have been implemented to
illustrate the benefits of optimizing both water and energy systems
simultaneously. In [10], multiple management strategies regarding the
energy usage and cost of operations in two water utilities were exam-
ined, using energy and water quality management systems (EWQMSs).
Another study by [3] developed a mixed integer nonlinear and linear
programming (MINLP & MILP) to minimize the operation cost of an
integrated water-energy microgrid by incorporating renewable energy
sources with piecewise linear functions to approximate nonlinear be-
havior. The findings demonstrated daily energy savings in both models,
while the MILP formulation provided fast convergence and CPU time
close to the MINLP formulation with only a small error percentage.
In [8], optimal demand management of a micro water-energy network
was investigated, where control the energy demand of the water system
improved the grid’s flexibility significantly. Another study by [11]
proposed a water-energy co-optimization model for a community-scale
microgrid, featuring both a microgrid energy management (MEM) and
a microgrid water management (MWM) specifically for a wastewater
treatment unit. The results indicated that MWM inclusion decreased
the cost of meeting water demand and the microgrid operating cost.
A neural-network-based energy management system for a renewable-
based microgrid was developed by [12] to minimize operational costs
while guaranteeing water and electricity load supplies. The study uti-
lized piecewise linear models and binary variables to represent both
units and used a rolling horizon strategy to alleviate the effect of
uncertainties in forecasting renewable sources. In [9], three optimiza-
tion models using MINLP were developed to minimize the electricity
costs in a micro water-energy system, illustrating that a co-optimization
model that includes renewable and energy storage system was able to
minimize daily electricity costs and water pump’s power consumption
and reduce fossil fuel-based power generation. The results of these
studies offered insightful approaches to the model formulation of the
water-energy microgrid. In addition, these studies showed that co-
optimization provides cost-efficient and energy-saving benefits when
compared to independent optimization of the two systems. Nonetheless,
these studies are applied in an ‘‘open-loop’’ control setting. The absence
of feedback control prevents the system from being adjusted and cor-
rected, leading to high error margins when dealing with disturbances
to which water-energy microgrid is highly susceptible [13].
1.2. Literature review on model predictive control of standalone water and
microgrid systems
A high rate of success has been demonstrated for model predictive
control (MPC) of standalone (i.e., off-grid) water systems and smart
grids [13,14]. Compared to conventional control that relies on current

state measurement to provide a feedback loop to adjust the control
action, MPC incorporates the dynamic and static models of the plant
that is being controlled to predict the system’s future behavior and
then uses this prediction to generate an optimal control signal [15,16].
MPC determines a control sequence by solving a finite-time horizon
of an online model-based optimization at each sampling instant, using
the plant’s current state as the initial state [13]. MPC follows the
receding horizon framework by applying only the first value of the
optimized control and output sequences and disregarding the rest of
the trajectory [17]. The optimal solution derived from the MPC is a set
of future controls subject to the objective functions and constraints at
future time instants [15]. This is also one of the main features of MPC,
which provides an effective solution to follow several performance
goals while considering hard constraints to the multiple inputs and
outputs of the system [13]. Overall, MPC is a powerful control tech-
nique that offers many advantages over the traditional control approach
that utilizes pre-computed control laws, particularly in a time-varying
system, potentially subject to disturbance and where hard constraints
must be handled.
Developments of MPC strategy for the management of WDSs have
been continuously investigated with different approaches such as flow-
based control [18], a linear approximation of flow and pressure head
model [19], combined control scheme of linear MPC and constraint-
satisfaction formulation that avoids nonlinear MPC formulation [20],
and nonlinear flow-pressure based model in economic MPC [14,21,22].
For example, A nonlinear economic MPC for WDSs was developed
in [14] with a two-layer approach to obtain optimal hourly solutions
of actuators to meet water demand and pressure requirements, while
the lower layer was used to generate a shorter sampling period for
pump scheduling. In [21], geometric programming MPC was utilized
for WDSs management to deal with the non-convexity of MPC when
considering WDSs models. Another study done by [22], also explored a
flow-pressure-based model in economic MPC that includes controllable
pressure and varying risks in the MPC formulation. Nevertheless, these
studies have not taken into account the interdependencies of the water-
energy system. For instance, the focus was mainly on minimizing
the electricity cost from the actuators, either by regulating the pump
schedule in accordance with the safety heads, minimizing the pump’s
flow rate and switches to attain the smoothness of the controllers, or
adding economic consideration to the objective functions. Although
these objective functions would indirectly result in reduced energy con-
sumption in WDSs, there are still significant limitations to be addressed:
In order to meet electricity demand, a variety of available energy
resources should be considered; MPC is currently conducted utilizing
conventional fossil fuels or the main grid as the sole energy resource,
whereas, in reality, a sustainable and clean water system cannot be
achieved without its electricity derived from renewable and clean
sources. Thus, energy management should also consider the application
of renewable energy sources (RES), such as wind and solar, as well
as energy storage units that allow a high degree of penetration of
renewable energy sources. Moreover, in the control strategy of above-
mentioned studies, the dynamic of the available energy resources is not
taken into account.
From a microgrid perspective, developments of MPC algorithm
have been conducted to provide optimal control of grid-connected and
islanded (i.e., standalone) microgrid with different energy management
strategies. In [23], a real-time bi-level optimization model was utilized
in the MPC framework to minimize the cost and the usage of a diesel
generator for an islanded microgrid. In another study by [5], demand
management of an off-grid (i.e., islanded) MGs was studied while
considering the power flow variation and the stochastic behavior of
demand load and renewable sources. A two-layer economic model
predictive control with MILP formulation was experimentally studied
by [4] in an isolated microgrid. However, these studies focused pri-
marily on household electricity demand. Furthermore, existing research
for MPC in standalone microgrid systems only applies loads from the
water supply without taking into account essential parameters and
hydraulic laws involved in the water system, which can impact both
systems’ control and operation. In [6], a two-stage MPC optimization
was proposed to optimize the operation of an integrated islanded
microgrid-seawater desalination plant. However, the water system con-
trol was oversimplified, limited to a water tank and desalination plant
scheduling at certain times without addressing pumps as one of the
most energy-intensive units in WDSs. In [24], stochastic behaviors of
RESs and weather conditions were investigated in a networked smart
greenhouses integrated microgrid using MPC. While in this study, a
water pump was considered one of the sources of electricity demand,
its dynamic model was oversimplified, and the control strategy did
not take into account the polynomial relationship between flow and
pressure head. This can have a significant impact on the actual energy
consumption and the control outcomes of the system.
1.3. Contributions of this work
To address the limitation of the existing studies, this paper pro-
poses an integrated water-energy microgrid control by utilizing the
MPC framework as an advanced control approach. The control-oriented
model incorporates the dynamics of water and energy system assets
to enhance real-time demand management in smart-islanded commu-
nities. MPC is selected as the control method as it combines the first
three elements of control actions by utilizing the feedback control to
minimize the error signal of the controlled output with the desired
setpoints [17], feedforward control to measure and predict the dis-
turbance [25], and optimal control [26] to obtain control law that
minimizes a quadratic objective function developed from system’s ex-
pression in state space model, making MPC an advanced and preferred
control strategy. To summarize our work, the main contributions of the
paper are listed as follows:
1. Compared to the state-of-the-art, which mainly ignored the in-
herent interlinks between water and energy assets, these inter-
links as well as the full dynamics of energy-consuming assets of
the water system in discrete time are considered in the proposed
MIMO interconnected water-energy MPC algorithm.
2. The developed closed-loop systems featured two objective func-
tions: ensuring the security level of the storage tanks and energy
storage unit, and minimizing abrupt changes in the actuators and
controllable power units, thereby increasing their lifespans.
3. Energy management of the microgrid assets (i.e., economic dis-
patch problem) is integrated in the MPC framework to optimize
the closed-loop resource allocation problem.
4. To eliminate the discrepancies between the actual plant model
and the prediction model implemented in MPC, this work de-
velops an offset-free control-oriented model formulation in con-
junction with feedback mechanisms and receding horizons (MPC
strategy).
5. Telford, Pennsylvania, was selected as the test site to implement
the proposed optimal water-energy management while satisfy-
ing operational constraints, ensuring compliance with customer
demands, and achieving sustainable resource utilization.
The remainder of the paper is organized in the following order.
Section 2 describes the dynamic model and layout of the water-energy
microgrid. The integrated MPC is formulated based on the nodes’
mass conservation law, the tanks’ energy conservation law, the power
balance, and the energy storage unit’s dynamic. Section 3 introduces
the MPC strategy of the water-energy microgrid based on state-space
representation. The results of the proposed control strategy in different
case studies are discussed in Section 4. Section 5 concludes the work
and briefly describes future work.

2. Model layout and formulation
2.1. Schematic system layout
Fig. 1 illustrates the system’s conceptual layout and elements. The
existing system is based on WDS in Telford, Pennsylvania, which in-
volves five wells scattered across the region, providing water to more
than 4500 people. In this paper, actual water demand from one of
the wells is utilized for the small-scale WDS, shown in Fig. 1. The
studied WDS consists of a clear well connected to two storage tanks,
two pumps, and a valve. The outflow of the water tanks mimics the
water demand of Telford’s WDS. The pumps are among the major
contributors to energy-intensive components of a water system [2].
Thereby, their consumed power is considered as the electrical load
of the islanded microgrid system. The studied microgrid includes an
aggregated wind turbine with 700 kW capacity, a diesel generator, a
battery energy storage system, and a solar photovoltaic (PV) generation
system with 600 kW capacity (exhibited in the lower part of Fig. 1).
Based on the average power consumption of the WDSs, the wind
turbine is determined to account for 54% of the average load, while
the photovoltaic unit is to contribute 46%.

3. Model predictive control of interconnected water-energy micro-
grid system
MPC algorithm provides a closed-loop formulation for minimizing
the tracking error trajectory through feedback control in the system.
The proposed control law applies a receding horizon approach in which
the optimal control action is derived from the first sample of the input
sequence, allowing a new measurement of the system’s state. Subse-
quently, a finite horizon is shifted, and a new optimization problem is
solved.
Integrated MPC for the water-energy microgrid operates to predict
the hour ahead control inputs to minimize the tracking error between
the predicted output and the known set-points and to obtain stability
of the control actions. MPC also serves as the energy management
system (EMS) to ensure power balance in the microgrid is obtained
effectively [5,35].

4.4.1. Scenario 1: Penalty to the water distribution actuators
In the first scenario, only penalty weights to the controllers of
the water distribution is implemented. The results of the microgrid
operation in Scenario 1 will be further discussed in the second scenario.
The goal of penalizing the controller of the WDS is to ensure that MPC
utilizes the storage tanks interchangeably with the water pumps to meet
the water demand. Fig. 12 illustrates the results for case study 4. Five
variables are displayed in the graphs to provide clarity on the impact of
penalizing the WDS’s controllers, such as the water levels, flow rates of
the water pumps as the input flows to the storage tanks, output flows
of the tanks denoted as 𝑞𝑜𝑢𝑡
𝑇1 & 𝑞𝑜𝑢𝑡
𝑇2 and water demand denoted as 𝑞𝑑1 &
𝑞𝑑2, respectively.
As illustrated in Fig. 12(a)–(b), demand compliance is consistently
obtained throughout the iterations, indicated by the converging lines
of the storage tanks’ output flows denoted by the green lines, with
the water demand demonstrated by the dashed red lines. Due to the
added penalty weights, MPC prioritizes minimizing the controller over
the tracking setpoints objectives for most iterations while satisfying the
water demand.
Both graphs illustrate three operating conditions of the pumps; (1)
when the lines associated with the controllers 𝑞𝑝,1 & 𝑞𝑝,2 overlap with
the water demand, it means that the pumps were the sole source to
satisfy the water demand, (2) when the 𝑞𝑝,1 & 𝑞𝑝,2 lines are higher than
the water demand, pumps were utilized to store water in the water
tanks to meet the setpoints, and (3) when the lines are lower than the
water demand, stored water in the tanks is solely utilized to meet the
demand.
In the first hour, it is observed that the storage tanks and the water
pumps collectively meet the water demand, as indicated by the reduced
water level from 5 m to approximately 4.5 m, and the lines of the
pumps’ flow rate are below the respective water demand. A similar
condition can also be observed during hours 14 to 19 for Tank 1, and
also 12 to 22 for Tank 2. Due to higher water consumption in Tank
1’s outflow, Tank 2 is expected to supply water for a longer period.
During hours 2–13, when water demand is stable, the pump is operated
steadily to store water in the tanks (exhibited by the small deviation
between two consecutive time steps). This allows stored water to be
distributed during peak hours and fluctuations in demand. In addition,
as required in the objective function, the rate of change of the actuators
should also be minimized; therefore, it is also seen that when there is
no fluctuation in the water demand, the flow rates of the pumps remain
constant within two subsequent time steps. During hours 22 to 24,
when water demand decreases, pumps were operated to reserve water
in the tanks to comply with the tracking set points formulated in the
MPC objective function. Notably, during hours when controllers were
minimized, the maximum percentage of change of the pumps’ energy
consumption compared to the baseline condition described in 4.1 was
up to 30%.
4.4.2. Scenario 2: Penalty to the power dispatch of the diesel generator unit

5. Conclusion
In this paper, a novel MIMO MPC algorithm is formulated for
islanded and interdependent water-energy microgrid systems. The in-
tegrated MPC allows simultaneous control of both water and power
systems to obtain optimal demand management subject to operational
standards and interdependencies. Real-world water demand data from
Telford, Pennsylvania, is used as the disturbance to verify the reliability
of the model against diurnal water demand fluctuations, which also
affect the hourly electricity demand.
Different case studies have been performed to verify the effec-
tiveness of the proposed MIMO MPC, namely (1) baseline conditions
of the water-energy microgrid, (2) the effect of limit on the rate of
change of the actuators in the water system, (3) the effect of RESs
deterministic uncertainty on the energy management system, and (4)
the effect of penalty weights to the controllers. Cost analysis of the
islanded microgrid system is performed by changing the operating
conditions of the water system (presented in the second case study).
The findings indicated that higher energy savings led to greater cost
savings of up to $360k/year at the expense of lower security levels for
storage tanks. The findings of this study also demonstrate that due to
the interconnections between water and energy systems, it is essential
to integrate their control models in order to achieve cost-effectiveness
while simultaneously satisfying both water and electricity demands.
Multiple optimal operation configurations (demonstrated in case
study 3) led to significant outcomes which could potentially benefit
both water and power systems: (1) WDS’s carbon footprint could be
reduced by utilizing less fossil-fuel-based electricity (from the diesel
generators) when an equal amount of solar and wind power is available.
(2) Smoothness of operation and longer lifespan of BESS are obtained
during high penetration of wind power. (3) A greater cost-saving of up
to 14.5k/year is attained when the utilization of BESS remains limited.
When the minimization of the controllers’ usage is added to the
objective function in case study 4, the pumps’ energy consumption
and the diesel generator’s power dispatch decreased up to 30% and
60%, respectively, despite still maintaining demand compliance. The
minimization was attained at the cost of disregarding the setpoints
tracking for the stored water levels and the BESS’s state of the charge.
This is anticipated as in a multi-objective function, the fulfillment of the
objective function becomes Pareto optimum, in which no improvement
can be made without deteriorating at least one other solution [35].
Additionally, in the fourth case study, the expenses associated with
starting up and shutting down the diesel generator were incorporated
into the problem formulation to sufficiently model real-world opera-
tions. The study found that by minimizing the diesel generator’s energy
dispatch, a daily cost reduction of $103 was obtained compared to the
baseline condition, which excludes the start-up and shutdown costs. In
all the case studies and operating conditions performed in this paper,
demand compliance to both water and electricity is always obtained.
In future work, we will focus on (1) developing the MPC algorithm
to deal with the nonlinearity of water-energy microgrid, (2) the dy-
namic feature of the water pump’s efficiency, and (3) exploring the
stochastic nature of RESs and demand.
