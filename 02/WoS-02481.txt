Peak demands for electricity and water can be managed in a sustainable size and shape via residential
demand response. Moreover, the mutual interaction of electric and water distribution management
systems can enable to present new services and benefits. The collaborative loads that have both
manageable electrical demand and water capacity can have great roles in the interaction. Peak
electricity demand can be shaped by deferring their working schedule, interrupting of their run
cycles, and assigning new set values that can lead to less consumption when the electricity demand
and its price are high. In this paper, novel methods were introduced to uncover the impact of
the residential electric demand response on household water demand. The methods were proposed
for manageable capacities of electricity and water, daily normalized profile, feature extraction, and
problem formulation. A feature extraction method based on Empirical Mode Decomposition was
used to find convenient case profiles for incentives. Realistic electricity and water demand profiles
were generated with twelve activities in 100 houses via MATLAB. Time intervals of the electricity
tariff and the intervals belong to the user’s daily habits were placed in the activity-based power
profiles. A high correlation was obtained in the daily normalized demand for electricity and water.
Collaborative appliances had a significant share (approximately 34%) in total electricity demand.
Deferring collaborative loads from on-peak time caused the shaping of the water demand. The amount
of the water managed in on-peak time was between 87.35 L and 120.96 L case by case in a home.

1. Introduction
Electricity and water are vital resources for sustainable eco-
nomic development. Depletion of fossil resources drives us to find
new reserves to meet increasing electricity power demand [1].
Especially reliable, cheap, and environmentally friendly sources
are gaining more importance. Global water use has also increased
six times over the past 100 years due to the increasing popula-
tions and economic growth [2]. Trend in increase is expected to
grow gradually at a rate of 1% per year [3]. Statistics are showing
that agriculture (dominated by irrigation), industry (dominated
by energy and manufacturing), and domestic use account for 70%,
20 %, and 10% of global water consumption respectively [4]. In
addition, global demands for energy and agricultural productions,
which are both heavily dependent of water, are expected to in-
crease by 60% up to 2025 [5]. Domestic water use is also foreseen
to increase significantly, particularly in African and Asian sub-
regions due to the expected rapid increase of populations [6].
Again, the increase in negative effects of global warming is a ma-
jor threat to meet water demand [7]. The lack of water resources

Energy consumption in houses is between 30% and 40% of total
consumption in the world [9]. Daily and seasonal changes in do-
mestic energy consumption are very effective in the formation of
the peak demand and it can be met by adding new production ca-
pacity, using new storage units, and applying Demand Response

the end users’ electrical consumption or production behavior to
help grid events [11]. It is carried out automatically or manually
in a response to price signal or the bid generated in the electricity

market directly or through an aggregator. Price is tailored for
retail or wholesale participants. Residential consumers can partic-
ipate DR program through the smart home energy management
systems (HEMS) [12]. Electricity users in houses are motivated via
retail tariffs or incentives. Home energy management systems are
used to create a working schedule for the electrical appliances to
obtain economic benefits for participants and aggregators [13].
A price function is formulated to obtain the targeted shape of
demand, which can be followed by users [14]. In residential
DR studies, it is accepted that manageable electrical appliances
have shiftability properties [15]. Shiftability means that it can
be possible to defer their working time to another time interval
when a request is sent by an energy management system. Air
conditioner (AC), electric water heater (WH), dishwasher (DW),
washing machine (WM), and electric vehicle are considered as a
deferrable (manageable) load [16].
Electric water heaters are the most decisive appliances in a
DR study. There is a high correlation between their electricity
consumption and daily total demand [17,18] because WHs have
a high share in residential electricity consumption [19]. Control
implementing for WHs is easy because of a resistor is used
to heat water and they can be preferred as an energy storage
device [19]. Electric water heaters were handled in Tammam
et al. [20] and Gelažanskas and Gamage [21] as an energy storage
device to shape electricity demand. Energy consumption of a
WH alters with the parameters such as tank volume, the heat
conductivity of the insulation, rated power of the heating compo-
nent, set temperature and the control algorithm [22]. Mean hot
water use profiles are not similar in all countries, but morning
and evening are found as on-peak time intervals in a day [23].
A high tank volume with a high set temperature is offered to
increase users’ satisfaction in Lübkert et al. [22]. Combi boilers
(CBs) are the most preferred technology for residential space
and water heating. There is a 23% share in energy consumption
in the UK for both heating purpose [24]. Energy consumption
pattern of heating is related to the daily activities in a home [25].
Heating related electricity demand is high in the morning and
evening [26]. Washing machine and DW are the loads that pro-
vide flexibility to aggregators due to their use can be deferred by
several hours [27]. Consumer survey studies are needed to obtain
their flexible electricity capacity [27]. Power profiles are available
in Pipattanasomporn et al. [28] for WM and DW that are gathered
in one-second intervals. In addition, WMs are also the appliances
that contribute 24% to residential water demand [29].
Residential water and electricity use related studies in the
literature are commonly focused on reducing methods of peak
electricity demand for water utilities [30–32]. A large part of the
current DR program is generally interested in wholesale mar-
kets [33] and electricity grid level issues are neglected with
the other important local challenges such as water distribution
problems faced in daily operation. But the integration of water
management systems and electric distribution management sys-
tems via an energy management interface can enable to present
additional services and benefits. Not only change in electricity
power profile, but also change in the water use profile can be
important in places if a high electrical energy is required to pump
water and transport it to consumers [34]. These benefits are the
following. Water consumption behavior can be changed via the
deferrable loads that have both manageable electric power and
manageable water capacity. Runtime schedules of the deferrable
residential appliances in a daily DR plan can help estimation
and control of the amount of water consumption. Electricity and
water demand shifting from the on-peak intervals to the off-peak
intervals increase the reliability of the water and the electricity
distribution system. There will be an increase in efficiency both
distribution systems. Balance stabilization between the supply
and demand will reduce interruption risk, costs, especially the
energy requirements in water systems [34]. Residential users can
make changes in their habits or behaviors because they want
to reduce their costs. It can also contribute to the awareness of
residential users about efficiency and savings.
It is found that most of the DR approaches in the mentioned
studies above are inadequate to consider both the mutual in-
teraction of electric and water in the distribution management
systems. This study aimed to contribute to this less addressed
topic in the literature related to the management of end users’
both electricity and water demand via DR. The main contributions
of this work are given below:
• Activity-based load models that can provide both electricity
and water consumption are proposed. In this study, the
method introduced with [35], which is activity-based and
only for obtaining electrical power profiles, has been further
developed. Proposed methodology can produce extremely
realistic output by as little data input as possible. In order to
produce very realistic results in the enhanced load models,
various measurement data of the devices with water con-
sumption have been taken as a basis. The new load models
that developed for the devices with both electricity and
water consumption have been transformed into activity-
based appliance models that will include not only water
consumption but also wastewater quantities.
• Normalization and feature extraction via Empirical Mode
Decomposition methods are developed to obtain the effect
of the interaction of electricity and water consumption of
the household appliances on a demand response request.
• The residential demand response via the collaborative ap-
pliances is performed. In the proposed demand response
method, there is a new perspective that aims to find man-
ageable capacity in a way that considers both the tariff and
the time intervals of the consumers’ habits.
As a result, novel methods were introduced that aimed to uncover
the impact of the residential DR with manageable electricity
capacity on the water demand of the residents. Behavioral load
models allowed both aggregator and consumer to see the effect
of the users’ behavior changes. Price-based tariff time intervals
based on the user’s daily habits were considered in the power and
water profiles. Realistic demand and water consumption profiles
that were gathered in two typical houses via energy analyzers
were also used in the study. Section 2 of the paper introduces the
methodologies for the mutual interaction of electricity and water
consumption. Section 3 presents results of the case studies. The
findings are discussed in Section 4. The challenges of this study
are highlighted in Section 5 as a conclusion.

the heater power switch [36]. The power of a WH that is approx-

appliances that preferred widely in houses for both space heating

2.3. Normalization and feature extraction for power and water con-
sumption
Power and water demand data provided by the scripts written
in MATLAB were used for a year in such a way that, one week in
each month with a one-minute progress step. The power matrix
P and water matrix W in each month consists of data one row
by 10,080 columns. Matrices with the size of one row by 10,080
columns were rearranged so that there were 7 rows by 1440
columns for each month. In other words, they were rearranged
to build a day profile consists of a seven-day consumption. The
total power of a day profile in a month was obtained by adding
the values of the same column to each other. It resulted with a
matrix that has one row with 1440 columns was created for each
month. The calculation process mentioned above was repeated
in 12 months. The total power of the day profile in a month was
added each other to reach for a day profile in a year. The results
were kept in the array Y. The total length of array Y was 1440.
Hourly data were obtained by grouping 60 consecutive minutes
of data in Y. As a last step, normalized values were calculated by

2.4. Problem formulation for mutual interaction
Electricity and water consumption are shaped depending on
when and how the appliances are used. The modification in a
runtime of loads causes the change in both the electricity and
water consumption pattern. Making a day-ahead schedule gives
opportunities both user and aggregators to manage peak demand
via DR incentives and tailored tariffs. For example, electricity
retail flat price for the residential user in 2022 is charged with
1.37 TL/kWh up to the 210 kWh monthly capacity in Turkey. 2.06
TL/kWh is applied with a 50% increase in retail price for each
exceeding consumption. The cost benefit can only be possible
by less consuming with the flat price in DR. But, time of use
(TOU) tariff consists of the mid-peak in 6.00–17.00, on-peak in
17.00–22.00, and off-peak in 22.00–06.00 intervals for a day in
Turkey. Time of use tariff can be useful to reduce bill in DR.
Because, off-peak time is charged with the least price 0.739241
TL/kWh that is 54% cheaper than the flat price in mid-day. On-
peak time is charged with the highest price 2.159889 TL/kWh
(63% more expensive than the price in mid-day). There is not
available such a TOU tariff for water use in Turkey. Water retail
price for the residential users is 5.56 TL/m3 for up to 15 m3 that
is the threshold for water usage capacity in Turkey. If the total
water amount during a bill period (typically one month) is above
the 15 m3, it is charged with 8.35 TL/m3 for each exceeded water
volume. Overuse tariff is 67% more expensive than the regular
price. The cost benefit can only be possible by less water demand
than the threshold.
An aggregator needs to know consumers’ activity data for
a successful DR study. If aggregators ignore users’ activities,
changes in consumption behavior can be resulted with a cus-
tomer dissatisfaction. Participants can withdraw from the DR
study. That is why activity-based power and water demand
profiles are crucial for DR. Mutual interaction can be done in a
DR study according to the profits can be taken by the TOU tariff.
A user can make a profit by shifting or reducing the electricity

5. Conclusion
This study, conducted with activity-based load models, can
make an important contribution to reducing withdrawals from
the DR event. Customer dissatisfaction can be eliminated because
demand profiles can be reachable during each activity and each
time periods of the interested tariff. It is highly possible to new
collaboration for the same customer between water and elec-
tricity distribution operators. In this study, on-peak time power
demand deferring caused the deferring the water demand in the
same way. Deferring working schedules of collaborative loads
resulted with the high correlated changes in electricity and water
demand. The high value of the correlation coefficient was clear
to show the mutual interaction between daily-normalized power
and daily-normalized water. The mutual correlation coefficient in
the daily normalized power curve and water consumption was
found in the values from 0.9594 to 0.9749. Distribution operators
can use to find the suitable activity-based power profiles with the
help of statistics obtained by feature extraction. It was found that
empirical mode decomposition method can be used successfully
to determine the most distinctive characteristics in power and
water demand profiles. Each consumer that met the activity pro-
file can be identified by the aggregator to offer tailored incentives.
The amount of the water managed by incentives in on-peak time
was 104.9 L in Case 1, 120.96 L in Case 2, and 87.35 L in Case 3.
Total incentive amount (discount) was obtained maximum 4.78
TL when it was compared to the on-peak time cost in Case 1 that
was 7.27 TL. Discount was calculated minimum 2.18 TL when it
was compared to the on-peak time cost in Case 3 that was 5.95
TL. As a result, it was seen that a DR application with residential
electricity consumers can also make an important contribution to
the management of the water distribution system. Electricity and
water demand shifting from the on-peak intervals to the off-peak
intervals increases the reliability of the water and the electric-
ity distribution system. The ownership ratio of appliance, usage
habits, solar radiation value, outdoor temperature are effective
on change in the total demand of power, water and wastewater
in the cases. Duration of forward and backward deferring in
the activity pattern should be determined by considering both

electricity and water demands of electrical appliances. Because
the results showed that the demand for electricity and water
varies depending on when and how these appliances are used,
the research data and surveys can help to generate activity data.
In this study, the cases were performed with twelve activities
in 100 houses. The activity data were taken from the results of
the research conducted by KONDA (a research and consultancy
company, and it conducts surveys to make understandable the
society in Turkey) and placed in Case 1, which is the base case.
The results of activity data research reflect the daily practices
and lifestyles of the Turkish adult population over the age of 15.
The margin of the findings is ±1.7% and ±2.3% for the 95% and
99% confidence intervals, respectively. The appliance penetration
rates of the European region were used. This study assumes
that aggregators may collect data from geographical information
systems, building energy identity information, registry and cit-
izenship databases and weather forecast servers. The enhanced
behavioral appliance modeling is started by assigning activities to
the number of houses. Activities that require persons in the home
are described. The devices are distributed to houses based on
their penetration rates. The devices that need people to operate
are identified. Activities and condition of use of the device are
matched. The beginning times of the devices are defined in the
activities. The realistic energy consumption values gathered by
the energy analyzer are used in the enhanced behavioral power
profiles. Although our study was conducted for 100 houses, this
number might be increased by the fact that the better technical
resources. It can be focused on developing consumer classification
methods in our future studies.
