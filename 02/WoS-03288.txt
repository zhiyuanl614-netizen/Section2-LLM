A B S T R A C T
Assessing the reliability of critical infrastructure networks, such as urban systems essential for city functions 
like electricity and water, is key for robust operation and risk management. Traditional methods for reliability 
estimation, such as minimal cut-sets and path enumeration, often become computationally infeasible for 
large-scale, complex networks due to the need to evaluate all possible node-to-node paths. This paper 
introduces a novel approach based on landmark nodes – critical nodes essential for maintaining network 
connectivity – to estimate reliability more efficiently. Instead of analysing all paths between nodes, the method 
focuses on those connecting regular nodes to landmark nodes, significantly reducing the number of paths 
considered and improving computational efficiency. The network is first decomposed using a graph clustering 
algorithm, producing internally dense subgraphs. Reliability is then evaluated through intra-subgraph and inter-
subgraph paths. A bipartite network model is also employed to represent inter-cluster structure, accounting 
for failures in both nodes and links. This supports a multi-scale reliability analysis across local areas and 
the full network. The methodology is validated using benchmark power distribution networks to ensure 
reproducibility. To demonstrate practical relevance, it is also applied to a real-world case study involving the 
water distribution system of Pavia, Italy. This application highlights how key urban areas and components can 
be efficiently identified to prioritise maintenance and guide resource allocation, contributing to more resilient 
and sustainable infrastructure management.
1. Introduction
Sustainable urban management requires assessing the reliability 
and resilience of critical infrastructure systems, including electricity, 
water distribution, transportation, and communication networks [1]. 
These systems ensure the continuous delivery of essential services 
that underpin societal well-being and environmental stewardship. Reli-
able infrastructure reduces service disruptions, enhances public safety, 
and contributes to efficient resource utilisation and adaptive capac-
ity. In transport networks, reliability is typically evaluated in terms 
of the probability of meeting a given demand within specific time 
and cost constraints, accounting for stochastic travel times [2]. In

An extensive body of research has been dedicated to investigating 
the reliability of water distribution systems (WDSs). Yang et al. [10] 
and Shinstine et al. [11] demonstrated the application of minimal cut-
sets to estimate WDS reliability. This was done in conjunction with 
supply path availability, and the results’ dependency on network design 
was emphasised. Giustolosi et al. [12] shifted the focus to mechanical 
reliability. They developed indices that link WDS reliability with the 
network’s structural and topological features. This concept was also 
highlighted in the works of Walski [13] and Ostfeld et al. [14], who 
integrated graph theory into WDS reliability estimation. Building on 
these methodologies, Scheidegger et al. [15] introduced a statisti-
cal framework for analysing WDS reliability. Furthermore, Hajibabaei 
et al. [16] employed complex network theory to address missing data 
reconstruction in WDSs, enhancing the accuracy and robustness of 
reliability assessments by mitigating data gaps. Guidotti et al. [17] 
expanded the scope of this research by incorporating spatial analysis 
and examining the interaction with urban social systems. Other re-
cent frameworks have focused on dynamic vulnerability assessment, 
using advanced structural models like multilayer networks to identify 
critical nodes [18]. Jeong et al. [19] provided a comparative analysis 
of WDS reliability, considering various indices related to hydraulic, 
mechanical, and network topology aspects. These diverse approaches 
collectively enhance the understanding of WDS reliability, offering 
valuable feedback for its improvement and underscoring the impor-
tance of considering multiple factors, from mechanical components to 
socio-environmental interactions.
Assessing the reliability of complex systems involves a variety of 
strategies that address the challenges posed by diverse network struc-
tures and operational conditions. Key approaches in the literature 
include: (1) Sample average approximation methods [20], (2) exact 
methods for multi-state series–parallel systems, typically for redun-
dancy allocation issues [21], and (3) meta-heuristic or evolutionary 
algorithms [22]. These techniques are effective for simpler network 
topologies. However, estimating reliability in complex networks, es-
pecially those with multi-state nodes, becomes more challenging due 
to the increased computational demands and mathematical complex-
ity [23,24]. For such networks, methods like stochastic models [25], 
system dynamics [26], Markov models [27], and Petri-nets [28] are 
commonly used. Despite these approaches, a significant challenge in 
real-world applications is the network size and associated scalability 
issues. As a result, practical reliability estimation in complex networks 
often involves min-cut decomposition [29], simulations for determin-
ing network states and failure probabilities [30], and the use of ap-
proximate methods [31]. Hitherto, the estimation of complex system 
reliability remains an open challenge, owing to the computational dif-
ficulty inherent in system reliability evaluation, known as NP-hardness. 
Additionally, the problem is compounded by the non-linear character-
istics of reliability functions (non-convexity) and the unpredictability 
of the system’s lifespan distribution, which gives rise to inaccuracy in 
predicting system reliability. These factors make the task challenging 
and necessitate advanced analytical approaches.
This paper introduces a novel approach to estimate the reliabil-
ity of infrastructure networks through the use of landmark nodes - 
selected nodes that are critical for maintaining network connectivity. 
The proposed methodology evaluates network reliability by analysing 
potential paths from any node to these landmark nodes, incorporating 
the probabilities of path failures. The proposed method offers two 
significant advantages:
• Reduces the number of paths that need to be considered, thereby 
enhancing computational efficiency in reliability assessment.
• Naturally uses network communities, allowing for the estimation 
of reliability in distinct network areas.
This paper makes several original contributions to the field of 
network reliability analysis. It introduces a modular framework that 
uses community detection to decompose large networks into man-
ageable subgraphs. It also formalises a landmark-node strategy that 
enables scalable reliability estimation by identifying landmark nodes, 
as those that connect different subgraphs (boundary nodes). The pro-
posed approach supports reliability analysis across multiple parts of the 
network, via subgraphs, and accounts for both node and link failures. 
In the particular case of urban water networks, the method further 
incorporates a logistic surrogate model for pipe failure probability, 
enabling the use of hydraulic data even when detailed failure records 
are sparse.
Initially, the method assumes that failures occur at the link level, 
reflecting the higher vulnerability of physical assets (e.g., pipes or 
cables). Reliability is first estimated independently within each sub-
graph, based on the network’s modular decomposition. In the literature, 
node failures are commonly approximated by assuming that a node’s 
failure results in the simultaneous failure of all its incident links, 
thereby reducing the problem to a link-only reliability calculation [32]. 
This assumption is conceptually simple and avoids the need to model 
node failure explicitly, while still capturing its functional impact. In 
the current proposal, however, node failures are explicitly represented 
by adopting a bipartite network model. This preserves the original 
topology while allowing simultaneous or independent failures of nodes 
and links, as well as configurations with multiple parallel links. The 
resulting formulation enables a more comprehensive and structurally 
accurate representation of failure scenarios in networked infrastructure 
systems [33].
The reliability estimation process is validated using IEEE power dis-
tribution benchmark networks to ensure repeatability, available at the 
urls http://ieee...reliability-based-studies-1 and http://ieee...reliability-
studies-2 [34,35]. Additionally, the case study on the WDS in Pavia, 
Italy, [36] demonstrates the practical application of this method in 
real-world complex networks, informing stakeholders and engineers to 
support informed decision-making in network reliability, maintenance, 
and resource allocation [37].
Section 2 presents an overview of the main methods for reliability 
assessment in complex networks, emphasising minimal cut-sets. Sec-
tion 3 addresses reliability estimation on complex systems allowing 
failures to happen on both network links and nodes. Section 4 illustrates 
how to proceed when the probability of failure is not directly provided. 
This approach is tailored for urban water distribution systems. Section 5 
closes the paper presenting the work conclusions and future work.
2. Reliability assessment in complex networks: current approaches
The minimal cut-set method is a prominent technique for assessing 
the reliability of complex networks [38]. It identifies critical groups 
of components whose simultaneous failure results in network collapse. 
The method includes: (1) identifying network components (nodes and 
links); (2) assigning reliability parameters; (3) determining minimal 
cut-sets; (4) calculating failure probabilities of these cut-sets; and (5) 
deriving the overall network reliability from these probabilities.
Despite its strengths, the minimal cut-set method faces limitations in 
large-scale applications due to combinatorial complexity [39]. As net-
works grow, identifying all potential minimal paths or cut-sets becomes 
computationally impractical. Nevertheless, the method remains funda-
mental, particularly when integrated with complementary approaches 
to manage computational demands.
To determine cut-sets effectively, various approaches exist: topo-
logical and path-enumeration methods, simulation and sampling pro-
cedures, and approximate heuristic techniques. Related approaches, 
such as percolation methods and dismantling frameworks, systemati-
cally identify critical components whose removal can trigger cascading 
network failures [40].

2.1. Topological and path-enumeration methods
Topological analysis for estimating network reliability scrutinises 
the network’s structure by identifying minimal cut-sets using its graph 
representation. This involves examining all possible paths between a 
source and a sink, as each minimal cut-set is a minimal group of 
components whose simultaneous failure disconnects the sink from the 
source. Minimal cut-sets are determined by identifying components that 
intersect all available paths, such that the removal of these components 
leads inevitably to network failure. Soh et al. [41] developed the 
subset-cut technique, enhancing the efficiency of path enumeration 
in communication networks, though their tests were on networks of 
limited size. This type of approach exemplifies how approximating 
reliability often involves identifying and focusing on a strategic subset 
of critical paths or components rather than exhaustive enumeration. 
To apply reliability assessments to larger and more complex networks, 
concepts like centrality metrics from network science become essential. 
Amani et al. [28] highlighted these challenges in power grids, advocat-
ing for measures based on complex network analysis to assess reliability 
in larger, information-rich, and interconnected systems [42].
Path enumeration algorithms, such as depth-first search and
breadth-first search, complement the minimal cut-set method by sys-
tematically identifying all paths from a network’s source to its sink, 
aiding in the extraction of critical paths and minimal cut-sets [43,44]. 
Xu et al. [45] and Mishra et al. [46] have furthered this approach 
by linking reliability to the network’s ability to maintain connectivity 
between nodes and by using network spanning trees and k-trees to 
select path sets. This highlights the strategy of considering multiple 
minimal cut-sets as an effective approximation for reliability, a concept 
aligned with Zhao et al. [47], who emphasised the NP-hard challenge in 
network reliability estimation. Such methods often involve identifying 
specific nodes or links for removal or analysis to simplify the problem 
and achieve computational efficiency in large-scale networks. To ad-
dress these computational difficulties, other methods, such as Monte 
Carlo simulation and sampling, are also proposed, offering alternative 
solutions to the challenge of reliability estimation in complex networks.
2.2. Approximate and heuristic techniques
The main methods employed in approximate and heuristic tech-
niques to estimate reliability are topological and path-enumeration 
methods, simulation and sampling procedures, and heuristic tech-
niques. These approaches aim to simplify the calculation of reliability 
by reducing the dimensions and complexity of the problem on the 
interrelationships of network elements.
2.2.1. Simulation and sampling procedures
Monte Carlo simulation, a probabilistic technique, is employed for 
estimating network reliability. This method involves generating ran-
dom samples of component failures based on their known reliability 
probabilities. The aggregation of numerous such simulations makes it 
possible to statistically estimate the probabilities of various cut-sets, 
thus arriving at an overall assessment of network reliability [48,49]. 
Although this method might not yield exact results, it offers statistically 
significant reliability estimates.
In addition to this, other heuristic approaches include using graph 
simplification methods to manage the network’s complexity while re-
taining its key features. For example, Chen et al. [50] suggested em-
ploying graph theory algorithms like breadth-first search for efficient 
network sampling, thereby facilitating reliability estimation. Similarly, 
Dey et al. [51] introduced a method to simplify reliability estimation 
by extracting network motifs to form building blocks, aiding in both 
component-level and overall network reliability analysis. A key aspect 
of any simulation-based method is the preliminary identification of 
the most critical elements in the network’s topology. This involves 
techniques like dismantling frameworks or targeted removal heuristics, 
where influential nodes or links are systematically identified to approx-
imate network reliability by focusing on essential connectivity. This 
approach ensures that the analysis is both focused and efficient, thereby 
enhancing the effectiveness of the reliability assessment [52].
2.2.2. Birnbaum’s importance measure for heuristic techniques
Heuristic strategies, such as the iterative removal of multiple se-
quences of network nodes and links, play a pivotal role in ascertaining 
which are critical within a network. These components are essential 
because their failure can significantly impact the system’s overall re-
liability [53,54]. An integral tool within these heuristic methods is 
Birnbaum’s importance measure [55], which quantitatively assesses the 
significance of each component in maintaining system reliability.
Birnbaum’s importance measure is mathematically defined as the 
differential probability of the system’s failure with the presence and 
absence of a specific component. In essence, Birnbaum’s measure calcu-
lates the change in system failure probability attributable to a particular 
component, providing a precise quantification of its impact on system 
reliability as shown by Eq. (1):

where 𝐼𝐵(𝐶) is the Birnbaum’s importance measure for component

3. Network decomposition using landmark nodes for reliability 
estimation
This section introduces the core of the proposal: a novel approach 
for estimating network reliability through dimension reduction, lever-
aging network decomposition and landmark nodes. The overall work-
ing perspective involves decomposing the network into subgraphs, 
identifying critical landmark nodes, and modelling reliability using a 
bipartite network representation. The following subsections detail the 
methodology and analyse its computational efficiency.
3.1. Methodology
The proposed approach starts by considering a graph 𝐺= {𝑉, 𝐸,
𝑊}, where 𝑉 is the set of vertices (or nodes), 𝐸 is the set of edges 
(or links), and 𝑊 is the set of weights on those edges, representing the 
probability of failure.
The methodology begins with a network decomposition into clus-
ters, referred to as subgraphs throughout the remainder of this paper, to 
manage the computational complexity inherent in large-scale network 
reliability assessment. These subgraphs, obtained through a clustering 
process, consist of node groups with denser internal connections than 
external ones.
The decomposition process formally partitions the entire set of 
nodes 𝑉 into 𝑞 disjoint subsets 𝑉1, … , 𝑉𝑞, where each 𝑉𝑖 constitutes a 
unique cluster (Algorithm 1, Step 3). Concurrently, the set of edges 𝐸
is categorised into two types: those connecting nodes within the same 
cluster, denoted as 𝐸𝑖, and those connecting nodes between different 
clusters, denoted as 𝐸𝑖𝑗, for 𝑖≠𝑗. This explicit partitioning establishes 
the structural boundaries within the network.
The clustering can be typically computed using graph-based algo-
rithms [56], such as graph spectral clustering [32], node embedding 
processes [57,58], and techniques based on computing network com-
munities [59], among others. The choice of this paper is to employ 
community detection methods, particularly the Louvain algorithm [60],

specification of the number of communities. The Louvain algorithm 
detects communities in networks by iteratively maximising modularity; 
measuring how densely nodes are connected within communities versus 
between them. This maximisation of modularity serves as the optimal-
ity criterion for the network decomposition, ensuring that the resulting 
subgraphs exhibit high internal edge density and minimal external con-
nectivity. The resulting modular structure supports both computational 
scalability and structural interpretability in the subsequent reliability 
analysis. Nodes are iteratively reassigned to neighbouring communi-
ties to maximise local modularity gains, after which communities are 
aggregated into larger nodes, continuing until no further improvement 
occurs. Its efficiency makes it particularly suited for large networks. It 
is important to note that the Louvain method was selected for its perfor-
mance. However, this stage of the methodology is adaptable, and other 
clustering algorithms could be interchangeably applied based on spe-
cific network characteristics or analytical objectives. The identification 
of these community structures is fundamental to understanding system-
wide resilience, as the vulnerability of these distinct communities often 
dictates the failure propagation pathways of the entire network [62].
Following the network decomposition, the most critical nodes in 
terms of maintaining connectivity between subgraphs are identified 
as landmark nodes. These correspond to boundary nodes in standard 
graph terminology and serve as key connectors for inter-subgraph 
communication.
The set of landmark nodes of cluster 𝑉𝑖, denoted as 𝐿𝑖, is defined as 
the set of all nodes 𝑣∈𝑉𝑖 such that there exists an edge (𝑣, 𝑥) ∈𝐸, 
where 𝑥∈𝑉𝑗, for some 𝑗≠𝑖 (Algorithm 1, Step 3). These nodes 
form the interface between subgraphs and are essential for preserving 
inter-cluster connectivity. Fig. 1 shows an example of the network 
decomposition process based on community detection.

landmark nodes in neighbouring subgraphs. The presence of multiple 
landmark nodes increases the robustness of the reliability estimation, 
as it allows for multiple potential paths between subgraphs. These 
paths, connecting different landmark nodes across subgraphs, are as-

The process of estimating overall network reliability is simplified by 
modelling the decomposed network as a bipartite graph, as illustrated 
in Fig. 2. A bipartite network is a type of graph comprising hypernodes 
that are divided into two distinct and non-overlapping subsets [64]. A 
characteristic of bipartite networks is that hypernodes in one subset 
are connected only to hypernodes in the other subset, that is, the 
hypernodes of the same subset are not directly connected (only con-
nected through hypernodes of the other subset as intermediary actors). 
This model provides a clear, high-level representation of the network’s 
modular topology.
In this representation, the graph consists of two distinct sets of hy-
pernodes: The left-side nodes represent the subgraphs, with each node 
assigned the internal reliability, 𝑅𝑖, calculated previously. The right-
side nodes represent the individual inter-subgraph links (e.g., pipes or 
cables). An edge in the bipartite graph connects a subgraph node to 
a link node if that link is physically connected to the corresponding 
subgraph in the original network. This structure encodes both the 
internal robustness of each subgraph and the failure probability of the 
specific links that connect them.
The overall network reliability is calculated from this bipartite 
representation. The model considers two types of components: the 
subgraph hypernodes and the ‘connection blocks’ that join them. A 
connection block 𝐶𝑖𝑗 is defined as the set of all parallel links connecting 
any two subgraphs, 𝑖 and 𝑗. This block is considered operational if at 
least one of its constituent links remains operational.

3.2. Computational efficiency

highly efficient, with a time complexity of approximately (|𝐸|) for

4. Experimental studies
This section is divided into two parts. The first part uses an IEEE 
power distribution benchmark networks to test the reliability esti-
mation of general networks of varied size and topology. This initial 
analysis allows the proposal to be reproducible. The second part of 
the experimental studies focuses on estimating the reliability of a 
water network in Pavia, Italy, and delves into the particular hydraulic 
implications associated with its network reliability.
4.1. Benchmark database and network metrics
The IEEE benchmark database is founded on the test cases generated 
by [68]. The networks employed in this context have sizes of 24, 
54, and 118 nodes, available at the url http://ieee...reliability-based-
studies-1. Additional networks with sizes of 37, 85, 137, 145, and 230 
nodes are available at the different url, http://ieee...reliability-based-
studies-2. In all cases, the network information includes variables such 
as link ID, node 1, node 2, link length, failure rate, and repair time. 
Additional node-specific information, such as demand and number of 
customers, is provided because the benchmark databases are related to 
power distribution networks. This node information allows for reliabil-
ity assessment not only in terms of network connectivity but also in 
terms of the impact of failures, supporting risk-related analyses.
Table 1 summarises key network topology measures and reliability 
estimations for the benchmark networks. The diameter, 𝐷(𝐺), repre-
sents the longest of all the shortest paths between any pair of nodes and 
indicates the maximum distance over which information or resources 
must travel across the network. In addition to the diameter, the average 
path length, 𝑙(𝐺), provides a more typical measure of the effort required 
for information or resources to flow through the network. Another 
important metric is the average clustering coefficient, 𝑐𝑐(𝐺), which 
measures the tendency of nodes to form interconnected groups. A 
higher clustering coefficient suggests greater local redundancy and, 
consequently, a network that is better able to withstand individual node 
failures [69].
Table 1 presents the results of the landmark-node-based approach 
for estimating reliability across multiple network topologies. The dif-
ference in reliability estimation between using the bipartite network 
of subgraphs and the direct all-paths approximation is negligible, with 
an error margin of less than 0.0001. However, the computational 
efficiency of the landmark-node approach is evident, as execution times 
increase only marginally with network size: 0.0560 s for a 24-node 
network, 0.0619 s for a 54-node network, and 0.0721 s for a 118-
node network. In contrast, the computational time for the all-paths 
approximation grows significantly with network size: 8.2209 s for 
24 nodes, 116.6295 s for 54 nodes, and 753.6948 s for 118 nodes. 
This stark difference highlights the scalability of the landmark-node 
approach, making it a far more efficient option for larger networks. 
All computations were performed in Python 3.11 on a Google Colab 
instance equipped with an Intel Xeon CPU @ 2.20 GHz and 13 GB of 
RAM.

4.2. Water distribution system
The proposed topology-based approach for complex network re-
liability assessment was also tested on a real hydraulic engineering 
infrastructure. Indeed, water distribution systems (WDSs) have al-
ready been successfully studied using graph theory and topological 
approaches [70–72], especially in site-specific data scarcity scenar-
ios [73], which hamper the application of physically-based methods. 
In this regard, the real WDS serving the city of Pavia, Northern 
Italy [74], was considered for the case study. Fig. 3 shows the network 
representation of Pavia WDS partitioned into 9 subgraphs, or supply 
clusters (SCs) following the notation of Herrera et al. [75]. Although 
these areas are not strictly defined as district metered areas (DMAs) in 
the conventional hydraulic sense [76], this paper refers to them as SCs, 
or simply as subgraphs.
This WDS includes 12 source nodes (representing groundwater wells 
with elevation ranging between 55.0 m and 88.1 m), 4277 demand 
nodes (with ground elevation ranging from 57.19 m to 86.11 m above 
sea level), 4733 pipes (with diameter ranging between 25 mm and 
500 mm, Hazen–Williams (H-W) roughness coefficient 𝐶 between 130 
and 150, and a total length of about 213 km), and 12 pumps drawing 
water from the wells, grouped in six pumping stations.
Numerous factors contribute to pipe failures, broadly categorised 
into three main areas [77]: pipe-related factors, which include age, 
material, diameter, length, manufacturing flaws, installation quality, 
and burial depth; environment-related factors, encompassing corrosion, 
soil type, soil pH, temperature, traffic, chemical and microbiological 
agents, and external loads; and operational-related factors, involving 
internal pressure, water type, water hammer, and water quality. Each 
of these categories encompasses a variety of specific elements that can 
impact the integrity and performance of water pipes.

structural stresses, especially under high-pressure conditions [82].

The bipartite network representation is not only useful for sum-
marising the key elements of a WDN at a larger scale, it is also key for 
estimating the overall reliability of the network. This bipartite network 
effectively captures the modular structure of the network, highlighting 
both the intra-SC robustness and the inter-SC connectivity. These inter-
SC pipes are the most critical pipes for water to flow between WDN 
areas. If the connecting pipes have high failure probabilities, they can 
become bottlenecks, isolating SCs and preventing the network from 
compensating for failures within a district by rerouting water through 
neighbouring SCs.
The combined effect of intra-SC and inter-SC link reliabilities deter-
mines the global reliability of the WDN, 0.9999 in this case. Although 
high intra-SC reliability ensures that each district can function effec-
tively in isolation, the overall network reliability is dependent on the 
robustness of the interconnections. In the case of the Pavia WDN, if the 
links between subgraphs with lower reliability (such as SC 7 and 8) 
and their neighbouring districts are also weak, the network’s ability to 
maintain service during failures is compromised. In addition, SC 7 and 
8 are particularly vulnerable since they are the subgraphs with lowest 
inter-SC connectivity, apart from SC 9 which is only connected by 1 
pipe to the rest of the WDN.

pressure ranges, as they are more prone to failures, and to analyse 
demand levels to assess the potential impact of disruptions on each 
SC. In addition to computing a ranking directly based on pressure 
range and demand levels, the proposal also considers a combined 
ranking which assigns weighted scores to each criterion to balance their 
respective impacts. For demonstration purposes, the following weights 
are assumed: 0.5 for reliability, 0.3 for pressure, and 0.2 for demand, 
after scaling all three to the same range.
According to Fig. 6, when considering the combined ranking, main-
tenance efforts should be strategically aligned to focus on SC 8 and SC

such as SC 7. Water authorities can use such results to inform econom-
ically justifiable maintenance prioritisation. One particular application 
is on the identification of at-risk subgraphs, aiming to provide a direct 
input for advanced predictive group maintenance models that optimise 
strategies for multi-component systems [84].
5. Conclusions
Complex networks serve as representative models for engineering 
systems such as critical infrastructure, making an accurate estimation 
of their reliability paramount for infrastructure operation and man-
agement, particularly in maintenance strategies. This paper presents a 
novel approach to reliability estimation that enhances the traditional 
cut-set method through the efficient selection of landmark nodes, an 
innovation that reduces computational complexity while preserving 
accuracy. Another original contribution is the use of a bipartite network 
representation, which provides a unified framework to model failures 
in both nodes and links across subgraphs. Furthermore, the method 
introduces a decentralised reliability estimation process based on com-
munity structure, which allows estimations to be conducted at different 
network scales and nested across all levels to enhance precision.
The proposed methodology was tested on a synthetic benchmark 
of power distribution networks, where the results demonstrated sig-
nificant benefits in network reliability computation. These benefits, 
especially the computational efficiency of the method, are evident in 
dynamic network environments, which is a common scenario for many 
technological networks exposed to environmental factors and/or vary-
ing internal conditions, leading to continuous changes. In addition to 
the benchmark networks, the approach was also tested in a real-world 
case study of the water distribution system of the city of Pavia in Italy. 
The results highlight the advantages of the reliability computation for 
asset management, particularly by providing efficient and detailed re-
sults, at network element, subnetwork, and whole system, for network 
analysis and maintenance prioritisation. Hence, the method enables 
more informed and effective decision-making processes, supporting the 
resilience and sustainability of critical infrastructure systems.
The water case study introduces the additional novelty of a surro-
gate expression for pipe failure, which has shown great utility for es-
timating reliability, conditional upon the availability of failure records

Overall, the approach presented in this paper offers a significant 
advancement in network reliability estimation. The landmark-node 
strategy provides computational efficiency, while the bipartite model 
offers a clear framework to account for both subgraph and link failures. 
The method’s capacity for area-based and nested reliability assessments 
directly supports targeted maintenance and management strategies, 
which are crucial for enhancing the robustness of critical infrastructure 
networks. This contributes to the creation of more resilient and sustain-
able urban environments, ensuring the continuous provision of essen-
tial services and minimising the environmental impact of infrastructure 
failures.
Future research directions include the extension of the proposed 
methodology to other types of critical infrastructure networks, such 
as transportation and communication systems, to assess its versatility 
and adaptability. The incorporation of more complex failure models, 
such as dependent and cascading failures, could enhance the robustness 
of the reliability estimation. An exploration of the impact of differ-
ent network topologies and configurations on reliability assessments 
may provide deeper insights into the structural factors that influence 
network resilience.
