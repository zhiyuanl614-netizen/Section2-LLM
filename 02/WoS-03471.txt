A B S T R A C T
Urban power-water systems (PWSs) exhibit complex dependence that exacerbates the degradation of their 
infrastructure service capacity under earthquake hazards. Existing earthquake risk analysis frameworks for PWSs 
usually characterize the system dependencies as binary states through joint failure probabilities, which fail to 
capture the dynamics of cascading failure among dependent PWSs. From the perspective of energy transfer 
among the dependent infrastructure systems, this study proposes a novel seismic performance analysis frame­
work for urban PWS to reveal the energy transfer amid the power system (PS), water distribution system (WDS), 
and terminal users, which integrates power flow and water hydraulic models. A dual-network model is first 
constructed to characterize PWS with directional dependency between power substations and water treatment 
plants. Seismic damage samples of PWS are then generated by seismic fragility models of components and quasi- 
Monte Carlo simulations. Finally, two real-world PWS cases are employed to validate the effectiveness of this 
framework. Results show that the proposed framework is capable of depicting the WDS performance that lies 
between the fully and non-power supply, which is usually higher than the estimations by the previous binary- 
dependent failure model of the PWS, providing a new perspective for disaster risk reduction of PWS.
1. Introduction
The normal operation of modern urban infrastructure systems relies 
on the dependence between distinct systems, which not only expands 
the functional scope of individual infrastructure systems, but also 
simultaneously increases the vulnerability [1,2]. For example, the 
power system (PS) provides energy to the pumping stations within the 
water distribution systems (WDS). Because of the strong dependencies 
between the two infrastructure systems, earthquake-induced failure of 
components within the PS would lead to impaired functionality of the 
associated water pumps, disrupting the reliability of power-water sys­
tems (PWSs) under earthquake hazards.
Over the past several decades, recurrent earthquake hazards world­
wide have caused extensive damage to urban infrastructures, which 
significantly disrupted the normal functioning of urban areas [3]. For 
instance, earthquake-induced water pipeline leakages and breakages 
have led to post-earthquake unmet water demand for urban residents, 
hospitals, and rescue operations [4]. Similarly, the PS exhibits high 
vulnerability under earthquake hazards, and there is not only the po­
tential for widespread power outages, but also severe impacts on the 
WDS performance due to their strong dependencies. Therefore, it is 
essential to conduct earthquake hazard analysis for PWSs. Such in­
terdependencies pose challenges to the disaster risk reduction of the 
critical urban infrastructure systems, which are more complex than in­
dividual systems [5].
The complexity in performance evaluation of interdependent critical 
infrastructure systems derives from two types of uncertainty factors: 
modeling uncertainty (parameter and model output uncertainty) and 
inherent system uncertainty (physical and operational uncertainty) [6].

formance of PWS is selecting an appropriate method to characterize

network-based approaches (including topology-based and flow-based 
methods) offer advantages such as more intuitive characterization of

explored a flow-based analytical model to assess the seismic perfor­
mance of PWS, with a specific focus on examining the interdependencies 
between water pumps and electrical substations. Li and Zhang [16] 
developed a flow-based model for the PWS and analyzed the seismic 
performance evolution for different system performance metrics. The 
findings indicate that in comparison with topology-based models, 
flow-based performance metrics yield more accurate assessments of the 
seismic performance of PWS.
Alternatively, the analysis of the dependent failure process is the 
second challenge in the assessment of the seismic performance of PWS. 
The transmission medium of the PS is electromagnetic waves, while the 
transmission medium of the WDS is water flow. These two systems 
exhibit substantial heterogeneity across dimensions, including topo­
logical characteristics, transmission properties, operational mecha­
nisms, and response behaviors. How to integrate heterogeneous 
infrastructure systems with substantial heterogeneity for seismic per­
formance evaluation is an urgent issue requiring more attention. In the 
research on the seismic performance assessment of PWS, the dependent 
failure models fall into two categories: binary-dependent failure model 
(i.e., substation failure results in water pump malfunction) and joint 
probabilistic dependent failure model (i.e., incorporating the 
earthquake-induced failure probabilities of substations and pump sta­
tions). For example, Ouyang [17] employed the binary-dependent fail­
ure model to consider the interdependent influence between the WDS 
and the PS, and conducted response analysis of PWS under local dis­
turbances. Yang et al [18] utilized the binary-dependent failure model to 
consider the impact of dependency relationships between WDS and PS 
on the service functionality of building facilities in the recovery process 
optimization based on reinforcement learning. Xiao et al [19] employed 
a joint probabilistic failure model to analyze the seismic resilience of 
interdependent engineering systems, an approach similar to the one 
used by Li and Zhang [16]. However, these dependent failure models are 
essentially binary-dependent failure models that treat the systems as 
operating independently.
In the propagation of cascading failures within the PWS, substation 
damage does not always lead to complete water pump failure, which 
depends on the extent of substation functional loss and the proportion of 
power that the pumps consume. For example, based on reconnaissance 
reports from the 1994 Northridge earthquake, Ça˘gnan et al [20] pointed 
out that damaged substations consist of multiple subsystems, and the 
failure of some components only leads to a partial functional derating of 
the substation, rather than a complete shutdown. Furthermore, damage 
records from the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake demonstrate that the total 
water supply of the water treatment plants in Deyang City decreased by 
17.8%, while Guangyuan City experienced a 37.5% reduction [21]. 
Consequently, these historical reconnaissance reports clearly demon­
strate that the functional state of the PWS components under seismic 
hazards is a continuous degradation process, which discrete binary 
models fail to capture [22]. Therefore, the dependent failure models 
used in the aforementioned studies are all binary-state models, failing to 
characterize the multi-state dynamics of the nonlinear interaction 
between dependent infrastructure systems [23]. Table 1 summarizes the 
characteristics of the PWS performance analysis framework in some 
existing studies.
In summary, several research gaps exist in the seismic performance 
assessment of PWS: (i) The dependence of PWS essentially involves the 
conversion of electrical energy into hydraulic potential energy. How­
ever, existing studies fail to quantify the energy transfer relationship 
within such systems; (ii) Evaluating seismic performance of PWS by 
binary-dependent failure model or joint probabilistic dependency fail­
ure model cannot capture the multi-state of functionality and cascading 
failure process; (iii) Seismic performance models for PWS have typically 
been validated through a single case study, thereby failing to explore 
how the system topological and functional attributes affect the seismic 
performance of PWS.
Given the gaps in the aforementioned studies, this study presents a 
novel framework to investigate the seismic performance of PWS from 
the perspective of energy transfer. The objectives of this study include: 
(i) Develop a dependent failure model of PWS to quantify the energy 
transfer processes in the PWS based on the power flow model and water 
hydraulic model; (ii) Quantify the power-water energy disturbance 
under earthquake hazards by the electrical energy supply rate from 
adjacent substations to water treatment plant and pump station; (iii) 
Reveal the influence of system topological and functional characteristics 
on the seismic performance of dependent PWS.
2. Methodology
The comprehensive procedures for the proposed research framework 
of this study are shown in Fig. 1. Firstly, the functional and topological 
parameters of the Power-Water Systems (PWS) are modeled, coupled 
with a regional seismic hazard analysis within the geographic location of 
the PWS. Subsequently, earthquake-damaged samples for the PWS are 
generated from seismic fragility models of the components and quasi- 
Monte Carlo (QMC) simulations. The second part is the functional 
analysis for the independent WDS and PS based on the power flow model 
and hydraulic model, and modifying functional parameters of the PWS 
based on the earthquake-induced damage states and the dependencies of 
the components. The third part is to analyze dependency parameters, 
seismic performance, and case-specific topological parameters of the 
PWS through two real-world cases across different seismic intensity 
levels.
2.1. Earthquake hazard analysis
Because of the large spatial extent of such infrastructure systems, 
uniform intensity measures (IMs) without accounting for the location of 
seismic fault zones will result in an underestimation of the performance 
of these infrastructure systems [24–26]. This study employs ground 
motion attenuation models that are from Ref [27]. to acquire peak IMs at 
individual sites for a specified earthquake scenario. For a given earth­
quake scenario, the IMs (i.e., PGV, PGA) at site i can be expressed as:

2.2.1. Seismic fragility for pipelines of the WDS
The failure probability of WDS components can be evaluated by 
seismic fragility models. According to Ref [28]., the earthquake-induced 
pipeline damage usually distributes over the whole urban area and takes 
the majority of damage in WDS. Earthquake-induced damage to the 
WDS pipelines relies on the IMs, pipeline structural types, and soil 
characteristics around pipelines, which result in uncertainty in 
earthquake-induced pipeline damage. American Lifeline Association 
(ALA) [29] assumes that the number of earthquake-induced damages to 
pipelines follows a Poisson distribution. The probabilistic parameters of 
the seismic damage event of the pipeline are estimated using a statistical 
method based on the pipeline damage data that occurred in historical 
earthquakes. Subsequently, the damage probability of the pipeline 
under earthquake hazards can be expressed as follows:

2.3. Seismic performance analysis of individual system
2.3.1. Seismic performance analysis for the WDS based hydraulic model
Earthquake-induced pipeline damages are classified as leakage or 
breakage [32]. Based on the HAZUS [33], the proportion of these 
damage types varies significantly between liquefied and non-liquefied 
regions: non-liquefied areas see 80% of pipeline damage in the form of 
leakage and 20% as breakage, while the inverse holds for liquefied 
zones, with breakage accounting for 80% and leakage for 20%. The 
leakage modes are categorized into five types by Shi and O'Rourke [34], 
which also include occurrence probabilities for each leakage type. Ac­
cording to Refs [3,35,36], the models of leakage and breakage are shown 
in Fig. 2.

functional state of the PWS under seismic hazards is better represented

3. Case study
This section first introduces the power-water system (PWS) case in 
Shelby County, Tennessee. Subsequently, the analysis of different 
dependent failure models and the evolution of seismic performance 
under varying earthquake intensities are performed to verify the 
effectiveness of the energy-dependent failure model. Furthermore, the 
evolution comparison between the Shelby case and the NY case is car­
ried out to reveal how the topological and functional characteristics of 
PWS influence the seismic performance. Finally, a dependent parameter 
sensitivity analysis on the seismic performance of PWS is performed to 
identify critical links and parameters that affect cascading failures in 
PWS.
The case study of the PWS in Shelby is employed to analyze the 
seismic performance of PWS using different dependent failure models 
[42]. As shown in Fig. 6, the water distribution system (WDS) of this 
PWS comprises 9 water sources, 71 pipelines, and 49 user nodes, 
whereas its power system (PS) consists of 8 power plants, 21 12-kV 
substations, 30 23-kV substations, and 71 distribution lines.
This study selects the New Madrid Seismic Zone (NMSZ), situated 
northwest of Shelby County, with an earthquake magnitude of MW=7.5

3.1. Comparative analysis of dependent failure models

3.2. Seismic performance analysis for the PWS
To analyze the differences in PWS performance between various 
dependent failure models and the completely independent PWS model, 
this section conducts a comparative analysis of seismic performance for 
the Shelby case under different seismic intensities within the same 
seismic fault zone (binary-dependent failure model with a threshold of 
100%). This section selects five seismic scenarios (MW = 6.0, MW = 6.5, 
MW = 7.0, MW = 7.5, and MW = 8.0), and the analysis results have been 
presented in Fig. 11. As presented in Fig. 11(a), the average SSIP of the 
PS gradually decreases from 0.945 to 0.405 across earthquake magni­
tudes ranging from MW =6.0 to MW =8.0. As shown in Fig. 11(b), 
compared to the independent WDS, the average SSIQ of the WDS is 
lower when considering the PS dependencies, regardless of the type of 
dependent failure model employed. This highlights the necessity of ac­
counting for the impact of the PS dependencies when assessing the SSIQ 
of WDS.
The SSIQ of the WDS calculated by the energy-dependent failure 
model presented in this study falls between that of the independent 
operation model and the binary-dependent failure model across all 
earthquake magnitudes. This indicates that the binary-dependent failure 
model typically overestimates the seismic performance loss of the WDS. 
However, as earthquake intensity increases, the variation between the 
energy-dependent failure model and the independent operation model 
in assessing the WDS gradually increases from 5.17% to 60.39%, while 
the variation between the energy-dependent failure model and the 
binary-dependent failure model diminishes from 94.8% to 39.61%. This 
occurrence is attributable to the extensive pipeline damage that leaves 
the WDS almost paralyzed during extremely rare earthquakes, meaning 
the choice of dependent failure models for the PWS exerts minimal in­
fluence on the seismic performance outcomes of the WDS.
To further analyze the energy supply relationship between sub­
stations and water treatment plants, the MW =7.5 seismic scenario is 
used as an example, and the probability density distribution of damaged 
samples results is presented in Fig. 12. A comparison of Figs. 12(a) and 
12(b) shows that the energy demand of water treatment plants ranges 
from 50 kW to 600 kW, while the active power supplied by substations 
ranges from 18 MW (18 000kW) to 120 MW (120 000kW). This indicates 
that the power demand of water treatment plants in the WDS accounts 
for a small proportion of the total load of substations. However, a 
comparison of Figs. 12(a) and 12(c) reveals that due to the islanding 
effect between substations (i.e., the failure of adjacent substations can 
lead to regional blackouts), the water supply of water treatment plants 
in the WDS is highly prone to complete failure after an earthquake. 
Consequently, the vulnerability of the PS under the earthquake disaster 
can significantly affect the functionality of water treatment plants, 
thereby impacting the entire water supply within the city.
Fig. 13 also spatially displays the energy values (equivalent power) 
of each user node. From Fig. 13(b), it can be observed that the three 
nodes of the WDS with the highest pre-earthquake energy values (Nodes 
19, 20, and 27) exhibit relatively low post-earthquake energy satisfac­
tion rates despite being directly connected to water sources. Conversely, 
nodes with lower pre-earthquake energy demand (e.g., Nodes 24, 26, 
and 28) show relatively higher post-earthquake energy satisfaction 
rates. This phenomenon may be attributed to the vulnerability of PS 
after the earthquake (as shown in Fig. 13(a)): the PS fails to supply 
sufficient power to the connected water treatment plants to support the 
normal operation of their pumps. However, the available power remains 
adequate for users with low energy demand, resulting in higher post- 
earthquake energy satisfaction rates for these users.

3.4. Influence of backup power facility in water pump station
The above analysis indicates that the PS plays a critical role in the 
normal operation of WDS. Insufficient power supply can severely affect 
water supply across an entire city. However, not all water treatment 
plants typically have backup power supplies, and even for those that do, 
their backup capacity may be insufficient to sustain the normal opera­
tion of pumps. While previous analyses focused on scenarios where 
WTPs completely lacked backup power, this section conducts a param­
eter sensitivity analysis using the Shelby PWS case. This section con­
ducts the sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of backup power 
supply probability (P(W|E)) and the power supply priority for the WTP 
(α) on the seismic performance of the PWS. Here, α characterizes the 
priority level of the dependent substation supplying power to the WTP. 
For instance, α=0.7 indicates that as long as the dependent substation’s 
post-earthquake active power remains at or above 70% of its pre- 
earthquake capacity, it will prioritize supplying power to the WTP. 
Consequently, a smaller α value represents a higher emergency power 
supply priority for the WTP.
Fig. 16(b) shows that the seismic performance of the WDS gradually 
increases as the P(W|E) increases (green line>blue line>yellow line). 
For the scenario with P(W|E) =0.5, the seismic performance difference 
betweenα=0.3 and α=1.0 is within 6%, corresponding to the maximum 
and minimum values of the blue line. This suggests that a higher α value 
(i.e., a lower power supply priority for the WTP during post-disaster 
power allocation) limits the seismic performance of the WDS. When α 
is fixed at 0.7 (shown in the subfigure of Fig. 16(b)), the seismic per­
formance of the WDS gradually increases with the rise of the P(W|E), 
and the seismic performance gradually approaches that of the inde­
pendent operation model (i.e., the P(W|E) =100%). When the P(W|E) 
increases from 10% to 90%, the seismic performance of the WDS im­
proves significantly by 29.9%.

4. Conclusions
To investigate the seismic performance of dependent power-water 
systems (PWS) during earthquake hazards, this study proposes an 
energy-dependent failure model for the power system (PS) and water 
distribution system (WDS) based on the power flow models and hy­
draulic models. This dependent failure model characterizes the 
cascading failure propagation process between these two heterogeneous 
infrastructure systems through energy conservation based on the 
dependent substations and pumps of the water treatment plant. The 
characteristic analyses on PWS cases are conducted with varying 
earthquake magnitudes, topologies, and functional characteristics. The 
main conclusions are summarized as follows: 
(i) The fundamental assumptions of dependency failure models 
critically dictate the seismic performance outcomes of the PWS, 
with variations reaching up to 90.9%. The binary-dependent 
model may overestimate seismic performance losses, and 
different thresholds led to WDS seismic performance variation 
ranging from 19.3% to 90.9%. In contrast, the energy-dependent 
failure model proposed by this study can continuously charac­
terize the impact of partial functional failures in substations on 
WTP functionality, without relying on threshold parameters.
(ii) The difference in the seismic performance of PWSs evaluated by 
the binary-dependent model and the energy-dependent model 
decreases as the magnitude increases. Accordingly, under rare 
earthquakes, extensive pipeline damage to the WDS leads to an 
insignificant difference in the PWS seismic performance evalua­
tion across different dependent failure models.
(iii) The seismic performance of PWS is highly sensitive to the spatial 
topology of WTPs. Comparative analysis reveals that systems 
with highly concentrated WTP layouts (e.g., the NY case) degrade 
much faster under seismic hazards than decentralized ones. 
Therefore, the spatial locations of WTPs constitute a critical fac­
tor influencing the earthquake risk of the WDS. To reduce the 
malfunction of WDS caused by the dependent power system 
failure, the WTP layout planning should be spatially decentral­
ized to prevent concurrent failures due to regional power outages. 
On the other hand, improving the power supply reliability to the 
region where these WTPs located is paramount to preventing 
severe cascading failures.
(iv) Parameter sensitivity analysis demonstrates that enhancing the 
availability of backup power supplies at WTPs significantly 
strengthens the seismic performance of the WDS. Consequently, 
mandating routine inspections and rigorous maintenance of 
backup power generators by water management authorities must 
be considered a vital pre-disaster mitigation measure. Specially, 
in disaster scenarios where WTP backup power supplies are 
unavailable or fail to operate, proactively prioritizing grid power 
assignment to WTPs (i.e., assign power supply to WTPs by re­
sidual power capacity in substations) serves as a highly effective 
emergency response strategy to maximize the seismic perfor­
mance of WDS.
Overall, this study concentrates on the seismic performance assess­
ment within the PWS, aiming to reveal the energy transfer amid power 
system-water system-users with a unidirectional dependency between 
substations and water treatment plants. However, the PS and the WDS 
exhibit complex interdependencies, and there are still several limita­
tions in this study: 
(i) The proposed energy-dependent model characterizes the degra­
dation of power transmission from substations to WTPs under 
seismic hazards through energy conservation among active 
power, water head, and flow rate. However, the reverse de­
pendency of the PS on the WDS is not addressed. Quantifying the 
energy dependence of power plants or substation cooling systems 
on water supply capacity will be a critical focus for future 
research.
(ii) This study conducts a steady-state power flow analysis tailored to 
city-scale distribution networks in evaluating the seismic per­
formance of the PS. However, expanding the spatial scope to a 
regional scale to incorporate the seismic vulnerability of trans­
mission networks and adopting a detailed power flow model to 
capture more failure mechanisms present a significant challenge 
and a crucial direction for future work.
(iii) Regarding the facility damage to the WDS, this study primarily 
accounts for pipeline damage and the loss of power supply to 
WTPs. Incorporating the direct structural damage to WTPs into 
the proposed framework, and evaluating how such physical 
damage degrades the overall functionality of the broader PWS, 
will be another key priority in subsequent studies.
