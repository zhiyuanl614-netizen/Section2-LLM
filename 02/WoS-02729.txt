A B S T R A C T
This paper addresses the critical need for enhancing security in Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition 
(SCADA) networks within Industrial Control Systems (ICSs) to protect the industrial processes from cyber- 
attacks. The purpose of our work is to propose and evaluate lightweight security measures to safeguard crit­
ical infrastructure resources. The scope of our effort involves simulating a secure SCADA/IoT-based hardware 
test bench for ICSs, utilizing Modbus and MQTT communication protocols. Through case studies in remote servo 
motor control, water distribution systems, and power system voltage level indicators, vulnerabilities such as 
Denial of Service (DoS) and Man-in-The-Middle (MiTM) attacks are identified, and security recommendations are 
provided. To execute our work, we deploy lightweight ciphers such as Prime Counter & Hash Chaining (PCHC) 
and Ascon algorithm with Compression Rate (ACR) for secure information exchange between the plant floor and 
the control center. Evaluation of these ciphers on Raspberry Pi focuses on execution speed and memory utili­
zation. Additionally, a comparison with the AGA-12 protocol standard for SCADA networks is conducted to 
underscore the efficacy of the proposed security measures. Our findings include the identification of SCADA 
network vulnerabilities and the proposal of lightweight security measures to mitigate risks. Performance eval­
uation of the proposed ciphers on Raspberry Pi demonstrates their effectiveness, emphasizing the importance of 
deploying such measures to ensure resilience against cyber threats in SCADA environments.
1. Introduction
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems play a 
crucial role in monitoring and controlling industrial processes such as oil 
mining, smart grids operations, nuclear power plants, water & sewage 
systems. Originally, SCADA systems were primarily designed to enhance 
accurate and efficient process execution within localized settings such as 
manufacturing plants. The emphasis was on local operations rather than 
securing network information [1]. However, with the increasing inte­
gration of SCADA systems for remote monitoring to enhance efficiency, 
they have become vulnerable to cyber-attacks. Fig. 1 illustrates a generic 
SCADA communication architecture consisting mainly of three sections, 
namely, field site, intermediate SCADA, and control center. Field site 
devices are all known as plant floor components and consist of sensors, 
Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), 
and Intelligent Electronic Devices (IEDs). These devices facilitate the 
transfer of data from the field instrumentation level to the supervisory 
level, where the expected parameter values are validated by components 
such as the Master Terminal Unit (MTU). Once validated, the data is 
then transmitted back to the field instrumentation level. Additionally, 
the SCADA control center serves as a central hub for storing and dis­
playing the exchanged measured data in a database, enabling central­
ized monitoring and system control. In network infrastructures for 
power plants or water plants, extensive SCADA networks are employed, 
which consist of numerous remote dedicated control units to distribute 
the load on the centralized server [2]. The increased interconnectivity of 
networks and the remote accessibility of systems within a SCADA 
network pose risks stemming from diverse vulnerabilities and potential 
cyber-attacks. The impact of quantum computing on SCADA systems is 
an area of active research, with efforts to classify attacks based on 
SCADA security requirements and assess the risks associated with 
quantum computing in the SCADA network [3]. In February 2021, an 
incident occurred at a U.S. Water Treatment Facility, wherein unau­
thorized access to SCADA systems resulted in a compromise. The

It is crucial to implement sufficient safety measures to enhance the 
security of SCADA networks at this point. Key precautions involve 
limited perimeters, patch management, robust cryptography, and, 
notably, segregating the control network and corporate network 
through the defense-in-depth approach. However, applying these secu­
rity measures is challenging due to inherited weaknesses from legacy 
systems and the potential risk of exploitation during real-time commu­
nication [6].
The consequences of failing to secure SCADA systems can be cata­
strophic, as demonstrated by the successful attack on the core command 
control system in Rye Brook, which remained undetected for several 
years [7]. Traditional SCADA standards such as IEC 60,870, DNP3, IEC 
61,850, and Modbus are known to have various security limitations. 
However, the AGA-12 standard has been developed to provide improved 
security features compared to these traditional standards [8]. AGA-12 
incorporates three primary algorithms, namely, AES with a minimum 
key length of 124 bits, RSA with a minimum key length of 1024 bits, and 
ECDSA with a key length of at least 160 bits, along with SHA-1 for 
cryptographic operations. These algorithms are utilized in AGA-12 to 
ensure confidentiality, integrity, and authentication [9]. However, 
Gidney et al. conducted theoretical calculations and provided evidence 
that Shor’s algorithm can be utilized to exploit the vulnerabilities of RSA 
[10]. They also estimated the quantum resources required for such at­
tacks. Additionally, the quantum search algorithm known as Grover’s 
algorithm has weakened the security of AES and ECDSA. Moreover, it 
has been demonstrated that SHA-1 is susceptible to collision attacks 
[11]. These findings emphasize the need for enhancing AGA-12 with a 
more robust scheme that can withstand both traditional and quantum 
attacks [12].
Several security standards and mechanisms have been proposed by 
researchers and standards bodies to address the vulnerabilities and 
strengthen SCADA security [13]. Security protocols rely on strong key 
management schemes, providing confidentiality, integrity, authentica­
tion, and non-repudiation for SCADA communications. Implementing 
well-designed key exchange and management systems is essential for 
maintaining the security and resilience of SCADA-based Industrial 
Control Systems (ICS). Researchers have developed protocols such as 
hybrid cryptosystems to address the availability, performance, and se­
curity requirements of SCADA infrastructure [14]. The foregoing dis­
cussion underscores the necessity for an effective cryptography solution 
that can prevent these systems from potential breaches. The objective of 
this paper is to design and develop a small SCADA testbed to measure the 
efficiency of proposed robust and low-cost security algorithms for 
automated industries, aiming to mitigate various security flaws and 
cyber-attacks. This paper aims to offer a robust and secure 
system-in-chip component that can be easily integrated with field site 
components while securely communicating with control center 
components.

2. Background and related work
Failure to secure SCADA systems can have catastrophic conse­
quences. According to the U.S. Department of Justice, an attack in Rye 
Brook was successfully launched in 2013, where hackers gained control 
over the core command control system. This breach went unnoticed 
until 2016 [15]. Additionally, SCADA standards and security protocols 
face threats due to a lack of confidentiality, integrity, and availability. 
To address these vulnerabilities, researchers and organizations have 
proposed various security standards and schemes. The current crypto­
graphic standard, AGA-12, employs RSA as asymmetric key cryptog­
raphy for secure seed exchange, AES for encryption, and ECDSA based 
on SHA-1 for generating signatures. However, RSA and AES are 
vulnerable to Shor’s and Grover’s algorithms in a quantum setting, and 
SHA-1 has been compromised in practice [16].
Secure key exchange and management systems are critical in 
ensuring the security of SCADA-based Industrial Control Systems (ICS). 
These systems are responsible for establishing and managing crypto­
graphic keys that are used to secure communication channels, authen­
ticate users and devices, and protect sensitive information in SCADA 
environment. Secure key exchange is the process of securely establishing 
shared cryptographic keys between communicating entities in a SCADA 
system. It ensures that the keys are exchanged in a way that is resistant 
to eavesdropping, tampering, and unauthorized access. The common 
secure key exchange mechanisms used in SCADA include Diffie-Hellman 
(DH) Key Exchange, Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), and Quantum Key 
Distribution (QKD). A DH Key exchange allows two parties to agree 
upon a shared secret key over an insecure channel without directly 
transmitting the key. DH key exchange provides a secure way to 
establish symmetric keys for encryption and authentication in SCADA 
systems. While PKI is a public-key cryptography framework to facilitate 
secure key exchange and authentication, it employs digital certificates 
and a trusted certificate authority to verify the identities of users, de­
vices, and control centers in a SCADA system. Whereas QKD is a quan­
tum cryptography technique that leverages the principles of quantum 
physics to securely exchange cryptographic keys. QKD ensures the se­
curity of key exchange by detecting any attempt to eavesdrop or tamper 
with the keys.
Key management systems are responsible for the generation, distri­
bution, storage, revocation, and lifecycle management of cryptographic 
keys within a SCADA system. During key generation, keys used in 
SCADA systems are generated using secure random number generators 
or cryptographic algorithms. The keys may include symmetric encryp­
tion keys, asymmetric key pairs (public and private keys), session keys, 
and authentication keys. Secure distribution of keys is crucial to prevent 
unauthorized access. Key distribution mechanisms may involve secure 
channels, pre-shared keys, key agreement protocols, or the use of trusted 
third parties. Moreover, keys need to be securely stored to prevent un­
authorized access or compromise. Hardware security modules (HSMs) or 
secure key storage systems can be used to protect keys from physical or 
logical attacks. While key revocation involves when a key is compro­
mised or no longer needed, it should be revoked to ensure the security of 
the system. Key revocation mechanisms, such as certificate revocation 
lists (CRLs) or key escrow systems, are used to invalidate and replace 
compromised or outdated keys. Key management systems handle the 
entire lifecycle of cryptographic keys, including key rotation, expiration, 
renewal, and archival. Proper key lifecycle management ensures the 
security and integrity of key usage over time. Furthermore, key man­
agement systems should incorporate auditing and monitoring capabil­
ities to track key usage, detect anomalies, and identify any unauthorized 
activities related to key management. Secure key exchange and key 
management systems in SCADA-based ICSs are crucial in addressing the 
unique security requirements and challenges of industrial control envi­
ronments. They aim to provide confidentiality, integrity, authentication, 
and non-repudiation for SCADA communications and protect sensitive 
information from unauthorized access or manipulation. Implementing 
robust key exchange and key management systems is essential to 
maintaining the security and resilience of SCADA-based ICSs.
Researchers have developed various key management protocols, 
based on symmetric, asymmetric, and hybrid key cryptosystems. The 
symmetric key scheme is efficient and provides authentication and 
confidentiality. However, it fails to provide integrity. Typically, asym­
metric key cryptography provides message integrity and authentication 
at the cost of high availability. Thus, hybrid cryptosystems are more 
feasible for SCADA infrastructure. Rezai et al. propose an advanced 
Hybrid key management architecture (HSKMA) that addresses the lack 
of availability and increases the performance and security of the 
communication channel [17]. It also provides a replacement scheme to 
address the availability requirement. Table 1 summarizes various key 
exchange and management schemes.
Various testbeds have been designed by researchers and organiza­
tions and can be categorized based on their features. The hardware- 
based large-scale testbeds have high costs and are difficult to imple­
ment. However, Idaho National Laboratory (INL) has developed a 
comprehensive smart grid infrastructure of 61 miles with a 138 kV dual- 
fed power loop using seven subterminal units for real-time testing [23]. 
It has layers of security features including encryption schemes based on 
hybrid algorithms, intrusion detection systems and firewalls. The Na­
tional Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) focuses on assessing 
Distributed Energy Resource (DER) system performance, supporting 
standards development, and addressing complex system integration 
challenges. Another smart grid test bed is Jeju Island which acts as a 
real-world smart grid testbed platform in collaboration with South 
Korea Electronic Power [24]. The microgrid at the Illinois Institute of 
Technology (IIT) was constructed to provide economically viable, 
environmentally friendly, resilient, and self-healing capabilities for the 
university campus during power outages. It consists of two 4-MW gas 
turbines, 140 KW solar PV cells, an 8-KW wind turbine with EV charging 
stations, and a 500-KWH storage system [25]. The Energy Systems 
Research Laboratory (ESRL) at Florida International University boasts a 
comprehensive testbed that encompasses both hardware and a complete 
cyber-physical infrastructure. This testbed facilitates research in multi­
ple areas and includes a total power capacity of up to 136 kW, incor­
porating conventional generation, renewable units, and storage 
capabilities. Actual generation stations are integrated with PMUs, IEDs, 
and PLCs, forming a complete platform for various smart grid domains 
[26].

of their research. Sandia National Laboratories developed The Virtual

analyze the threats and vulnerabilities of the critical infrastructure. The 
testbed involves encrypted and secure data communication between 
Internet Protocol routed units, simulated RTUs, and Human Machine 
Interfaces (HMI). The DNP3 and Modbus protocols are also implemented 
and are tested against various cyber-attacks including MiTM, precision 
insider, and rogue software attacks. Furthermore, Wireshark is used as a

limitations related to system identification and attack detection in select 
experiments highlight areas for potential enhancement in future itera­
tions of the testbed. Furthermore, in [34] authors have addressed the 
need for comprehensive validation and testing approaches, proposing a 
multi laboratory cooperation model within the ERIGrid project to 
establish a pan-European smart grid research infrastructure. Moreover, 
The RGCE was conceptualized as a versatile hybrid testbed, enabling the 
creation of tailored scenarios involving interdependencies across 
various infrastructure and organizational tiers, suitable for both training 
and research endeavors [35]. The HEDvA served as a pivotal factor, 
inspiring numerous architectural choices that shaped the design of the 
CANVAS laboratory. Following a three-tier structure, it incorporates 
layers for assets, infrastructure, and the laboratory environment [36]. 
However, most of the past research have utilized simulators to evaluate 
their protocols. Moreover, few of the implementation and development 
of SCADA-based ICS testbeds are costly and time-consuming to imple­
ment. Currently, there is a lack of significant research studies or small 
testbeds that offer a low-cost setup for evaluating lightweight security 
algorithms and conducting performance assessments for SCADA-based 
ICSs.
The foregoing discussions highlight that in the current threat land­
scape, the increasing interconnectivity of SCADA systems exposes them 
to a wider range of cyber threats. The advent of quantum computing 
poses a specific risk to traditional cryptographic standards, like AGA-12, 
necessitating innovative security solutions. Recent security breaches 
underscore the urgent need for robust measures in SCADA systems, 
especially in addressing vulnerabilities in confidentiality, integrity, and 
availability. The challenges in existing cryptographic standards, exem­
plified by AGA-12, call for a re-evaluation and enhancement of security 
frameworks, particularly in key management, to address evolving 
threats such as quantum attacks. To address these issues, our motivation 
is to develop a comprehensive SCADA/IoT test bench that combines 
hardware simulation and lightweight cryptographic algorithms. The 
proposed test bench aims to fill existing research gaps by providing a 
controlled environment for testing the security measures implemented 
in SCADA systems against a range of cyber-attacks. This paper lays the 
foundation for a practical, efficient, and resilient security framework for 
SCADA/IoT systems, ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and avail­

3. Design and development of secure hardware test bench for 
SCADA/IoT-based ICSs
The methodology to design and develop a secure SCADA/IoT-based 
hardware simulation for ICSs is discussed in this section. An overview of 
the testbed design, along with attack opportunities and attack locations, 
has been provided in Section 3.1. Furthermore, we explain the inte­
gration of the security module to protect the critical resources of in­
dustrial systems. Following that, we discuss the installation steps to 
create a communication environment for both communication parties, 
namely, MTU and RTU in Section 3.2. The testbed focuses on two 
communication protocols: MQTT protocol for IoT devices and Modbus 
for SCADA components. Additionally, the discussion focuses on under­
standing the functionality of the lightweight cipher proposed in one of 
our earlier research papers is broadly explained in Section 3.3. The 
simulation of security solutions on the hardware test bench is discussed 
in the next section. Finally, we assess the performance of the lightweight 
cipher by deploying it into a Raspberry Pi, which acts as a system-on-

3.1. Overview of proposed secure SCADA/IoT testbed
This section covers the three major modules of the SCADA networks, 
namely, the design and development of a small testbed of SCADA/IoT- 
based Industrial Control Systems, attack opportunities and locations of 
DoS and MiTM attacks, and integration of the security module to protect 
the system. Fig. 2 demonstrates the concept of testbed design for secure 
SCADA communication. We have considered the communication 
between PLCs and MTU where PLC is situated on the plant floor and 
MTU at the control center. At the control center, VTSCADA Light is used 
for various purposes such as controlling and monitoring the plant floor 
devices remotely via HMI, maintaining the log files at reporting server, 
and generating alerts for suspicious events. Here, we have configured 
the MTU Server in the Windows subsystem of Linux (WSL). The HMI is 
connected to MTU Server using an HDMI cable (wired connection). The 
proposed security module is deployed in the MTU Server for secure 
communication with plant floor devices. However, owing to legacy 
inherited security loopholes of Modbus protocol, the internal intruder 
has the ability to intercept the traffic between MTU and HMI. To protect 
the system from such internal attacks, deployment of IDS is essential. We 
have proposed lightweight IDS systems for the control center and plant 
floor in one of our previous research papers [27,29]. Moreover, the 
secure channel is established by deploying a lightweight cipher at the 
end of both communication parties.
3.2. Communication protocols and environment setup
3.2.1. MQTT protocol
The MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport) protocol is a 
lightweight, publish-subscribe messaging protocol designed for efficient 
communication in constrained environments, making it ideal for IoT and 
SCADA systems. Here’s a detailed breakdown of its implementation and 
setup:
Basics of MQTT Protocol:
• MQTT operates on a publish-subscribe model, where clients (pub­
lishers) send messages to a central broker, which then distributes 
them to subscribing clients based on predefined topics.
• It utilizes TCP/IP as the underlying transport protocol, ensuring 
reliable communication over networks.
• MQTT is renowned for its low bandwidth usage, efficient message 
distribution, and support for Quality of Service (QoS) levels to ensure 
message delivery.

3.2.2. Modbus protocol
The Modbus protocol is a widely used communication protocol in 
industrial automation systems which facilitate communication between 
various field devices and control systems. Below we have explained in- 
depth exploration of its configuration and setup for the proposed 
testbed:
Basics of Modbus Protocol:
• Modbus operates on a client-server architecture, where a master 
device (e.g., SCADA system) communicates with slave devices (e.g., 
PLCs, RTUs) using a request-response mechanism.
• It supports different data types and functions, allowing for versatile 
communication and control of industrial processes.
• Modbus typically uses serial communication (RS-232, RS-485) or 
Modbus TCP/IP for communication over Ethernet networks.
• Modbus TCP/IP is a variant of the Modbus protocol that operates 
over Ethernet networks. It allows devices to communicate using the 
Modbus protocol over TCP/IP, which is the standard communication 
protocol of the internet.
• In Modbus TCP/IP, data is encapsulated in TCP/IP packets, allowing 
for reliable and efficient communication between devices on a 
network. Unlike Modbus RTU or Modbus ASCII, which are serial- 
based variants of the protocol, Modbus TCP/IP utilizes Ethernet as 
the physical layer for communication, offering advantages such as 
higher data transfer rates, longer communication distances, and 
support for more concurrent connections.

3.3. Security modules used for secure information exchange
3.3.1. Prime Counter & Hash Chaining (PCHC) using multi-layered 
architecture
Many SCADA-based industrial applications rely on real-time 
communication and require highly available systems. Therefore, for a 
secure communication channel between the plant floor and control 
center components, we have deployed the lightweight cipher that we 
have proposed in our earlier work [38]. The proposed security solution 
follows a hybrid efficient key management scheme to distribute the 
session key. Further, the lightweight cipher is used for secure informa­
tion exchange by merging the concept of a random number generator 
and Hashed Message Authentication Code (HMAC). The proposed 
scheme used Random Number (RN), Fraction Square Root of Prime 
(FSRP), and Current Date and Time (CDT) to generate a fresh session key 
(KS) for every session at both ends, namely, MTU, and RTU. However, 
these elements are not transferred openly, rather RN is encrypted using a 
session key generated using CDT and FSRP. Moreover, XOR operation is 
applied on FSRP and CDT to generate key salt which will be shared over 
the network along with the cipher text of RN and HMAC. Here, the index 
of FSRP acts as a root of trust for the entire scheme. Once, elements of 
the session key have been distributed securely, both communication 
parties generate the session key for secure information exchange.
A lightweight symmetric key cryptography (Vernam Cipher) is used 
during the encryption and decryption process. Vernam cipher requires a 
fresh key during every message exchange, and hence symmetric key is 
generated using a session key, CDT, and FSRP depending on the pro­
posed approaches. Prime Counter needs both FSRP and CDT to generate 
the symmetric key, however, Hash Chaining only requires FSRP for 
secure information exchange. Moreover, FSRP can be generated by 
increasing the index of a prime number using the prime counter method.

3.3.2. Ascon algorithm using compression rate (ACR)
The proposed security solution provides secure communication be­
tween MTU and RTU in SCADA infrastructure. The MTU first generates 
key pairs based on the Huffman Coding Function along with time- 
variant parameters, pseudorandom number generator parameters, and 
the unique id of the MTU. Furthermore, the proposed algorithm in Fig. 4
involves using a radioactive decay equation to generate the compression 
rate of Huffman coded raw key, which is processed to generate the 
public key and cipher. The public key is hashed and split into two chunks 
(r,k). The cipher and k is hashed to generate the shared secret key. MTU 
sends the public key and cipher to RTU. The RTU decrypts the cipher and 
follows key decapsulation to obtain the essence of the public key, which 
includes the parameters such as time variants, chunks, and the hashed 
value of the raw key as well as the unique id of MTU. Based on the 
essence, the MTU generates its own compression rate and shared secret 
key. The shared secret key is validated by comparing the received 
compression rate and generated compression rate by RTU. Once the 
session key establishment is achieved, the MTU encrypts the sensitive 
message using the ACSON algorithm to generate a cipher and an 
authentication tag. The RTU received the cipher and the tag, decrypts it 
with a shared secret key, and extracts the message and the tag is used to 
validate the data integrity.

PCHC and ACR both address various security goals such as confi­
dentiality, authentication, message integrity, and non-repudiation. 
PCHC provides secure session key exchange using NTRU, secure infor­
mation exchange through Hash-chaining, and employ Hash Function 
Blake2 for generating hash values. ACR The first involves a shared secret 
key exchange using innovative techniques based on Huffman coding and 
the concept of Radioactive Decay rate. This method ensures a secure and 
unique exchange of keys, enhancing the confidentiality of information. 
The second mechanism involves secure information exchange using 
ASCON, a lightweight authenticated encryption scheme. ASCON pro­
vides a robust and efficient way to exchange information securely, 
ensuring the integrity and authenticity of the data being transmitted. 
Additionally, the use of HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication 
Code) with Sha3 enhances the security of the exchanged data by 
generating a hash of the MAC (Message Authentication Code) function. 
This ensures that the integrity and authenticity of the transmitted data 
are maintained, providing a secure communication channel. Unlike 
AGA-12, the proposed modules effectively prevent eavesdropping, man- 
in-the-middle attacks, and spoofing, ensuring a higher level of security.
PCHC and ACR address the vulnerabilities found in AGA-12, such as 
length-extension attacks. They enhance security by preventing these 
attacks through advanced algorithms and protocols providing HMAC as 
encapsulation layer. Additionally, the proposed modules mitigate 
quantum attacks and collision attacks based on Grover’s search algo­
rithm, which AGA-12 fails to address. They utilize NTRU-based post- 
quantum algorithms and ASCON with a digest based on SHA-3 for better 
resistance against quantum attacks and improved message integrity.

6. Summary and concluding remarks
In this paper, we present a comprehensive framework for a SCADA 
testbed aimed at ensuring the security and availability of industrial 
control systems during real-time communications. Leveraging compo­
nents from an industrial field site and control center devices, we outline 
a robust methodology for designing and developing SCADA/IoT-based 
ICS applications. Our testbed supports both MQTT for IoT devices and 
Modbus for SCADA components, establishing a versatile platform for 
secure communication.
A key aspect of our framework involves the implementation of 
lightweight ciphers at both endpoints to establish a secure channel be­
tween the control center and plant floor devices. We evaluate the effi­
ciency of our proposed security algorithms in terms of computational 
speed and memory utilization through deployment into a hardware 
testbed. Our proposed security modules address various vulnerabilities 
and prevent advanced attacks by employing advanced cryptographic 
techniques, post-quantum algorithms, and machine learning-based 
Intrusion Detection Systems. The evaluation of proposed security algo­
rithms, including Prime Counter & Hash Chaining (PCHC) and Ascon 
algorithm with Compression Rate (ACR), demonstrates superior per­
formance compared to the current SCADA security standard AGA-12. 
This comparative analysis highlights the efficacy of the proposed secu­
rity measures in terms of computational speed, memory utilization, and 
resilience against cyber threats.
Furthermore, through case studies involving remote servo motor 
control, water distribution systems, and power system voltage level in­
dicators, vulnerabilities such as Denial of Service and Man-in-The- 
Middle attacks have been identified and effectively addressed. For 
example, in remote servo motor control, the implementation of light­
weight ciphers ensures that commands transmitted from the control 
center to servo motors are encrypted, preventing unauthorized inter­
ception or manipulation. This encryption not only safeguards command 
integrity but also restricts communication to authenticated devices, 
thereby mitigating the risk of Man-in-The-Middle attacks. In water dis­
tribution systems, where reliability and data integrity are paramount, 
our proposed cryptographic techniques such as PCHC and ACR are 
crucial. Specifically, the Ascon algorithm with Compression Rate secures 
data transmission between sensors and the central control system while 
enhancing system resilience against DoS attacks. By efficiently man­
aging computational resources and maintaining consistent communi­
cation channels, ACR ensures uninterrupted operation of critical 
infrastructure. Similarly, for power system voltage level indicators, the 
deployment of lightweight ciphers and advanced cryptographic pro­
tocols ensures the secure and tamper-proof transmission of data to the 
central control system. These protocols guarantee that voltage readings 
cannot be manipulated or falsified during transmission, preventing 
malicious actors from disrupting the power grid through injection of 
false data or unauthorized access attempts to critical control systems.

Overall, the use of our proposed lightweight ciphers facilitates secure 
information exchange between plant floor and control center compo­
nents, safeguarding the integrity and availability of industrial control 
systems across diverse operational environments. By demonstrating the 
integration of our robust cryptographic measures tailored to specific 
industrial applications, our goal is to showcase the strength of our 
proposed framework against real-world security threats, ensuring reli­
able and secure operation of critical infrastructure components.
The successful hardware simulation of our testbed enables operators 
and technicians to remotely monitor and control plant floor machinery 
via Human-Machine Interface (HMI), thereby protecting the entire 
network from potential breaches. Through this research, we aim to 
demonstrate the feasibility of low-cost, secure, and highly available 
hardware testbeds for industrial control systems, with scalability to 
large-scale SCADA infrastructure.
In conclusion, our work addresses the critical need for enhancing 
security in SCADA networks within ICSs, offering practical solutions to 
safeguard critical infrastructure resources. By proposing a secure 
framework design, validating it through hardware simulation, and 
providing a thorough comparative analysis of security algorithms, we 
contribute valuable insights towards ensuring resilience against cyber 
threats in SCADA environments. Overall, this research contributes to the 
practical understanding and implementation of SCADA systems in in­
dustrial control environments.
