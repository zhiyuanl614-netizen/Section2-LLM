A B S T R A C T
Water, energy, and environmental sustainability are among the most important global challenges. Process
integration and multi-generation systems, due to their ability to increase efficiency and reduce environmental
impacts, are promising solutions that have attracted significant attention. In this study, an existing combined
cycle power plant was retrofitted and integrated with a multi-effect desalination with a thermo-vapor compressor
and a chlor-alkali plant, to fulfill simultaneously the demand for electricity, water, and valuable chemicals while
addressing environmental concerns by achieving zero brine discharge and minimizing greenhouse gas emissions.
In the proposed integrated system, the required energy was supplied by an existing power plant. To minimize
environmental impacts, cooling water of the power plant was used in the desalination process, and the brine of
desalination was fed into the chlor-alkali plant. After optimizing the system with objective functions for exergy
efficiency and payback period, comprehensive energy, exergy, economic, and environmental analyses were
conducted on the optimized system. The results indicated that consuming 43.48 kg/s of fuel, the system
generated 736.74 MW of electricity and produced 186 kg/s, 15.24 kg/s, 0.59 kg/s, and 31.98 kg/s of desalinated
water, chlorine, hydrogen, and caustic soda, respectively, with an energy efficiency of 58.88 %. Utilizing cooling
water in the desalination plant increased the energy efficiency of the plant by 17.60 %. Moreover, the utilization
of brine in the chlor-alkali plant not only upgraded the system to a zero brine discharge plant but also improved
the energy efficiency of the desalination plant by 8.44 %. The overall exergy efficiency of the system was 41.07 %
and based on environmental analysis the system emits CO, CO2, and NOx at the values of 0.542 kg/s, 379.18 kg/
MW, and 0.0042 kg/s, respectively. According to economic assessments, the payback period of the proposed
multi-generation system is less than four years.
1. Introduction
In recent years, the high population growth and the significant trend
of industrial and economic development in the world have led to an
increase sharply the water consumption. It has been forecasted that the
gross domestic product (GDP) of the E7 member countries (including
Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Russia, and Turkey) will in-
crease from 69.3 to 138.2 T$ within 35 years (from 2015 to 2050) [1].
Meanwhile, the world population is expected to rise from 7.2 to 9.74
billion people [2]. It is noteworthy that approximately 70 % of the
Earth’s surface is covered by water; however, only 2.5 % of Earth’s
water is freshwater and is suitable for human consumption [3]. More-
over, average precipitation has recently decreased in some regions of the
world, mainly in hot and dry geographies, such as the Iran plateau, due
to climate change which has caused a severe drought occurrence and
consequently economic and social impacts. One of the most important
consequences of the decreased rainfall in the Iran Plateau is the
approximately 80 % reduction in the size of Lake Urmia [4]. As the
largest saltwater lake in the Middle East and the second-largest in the
world [5], Lake Urmia has undergone this dramatic shrinkage primarily
due to a 9.2 % decrease in average rainfall between 1964 and 2005 [6].
Therefore, water scarcity has become a substantial issue in various parts
of the world. This problem, intensified by rising consumption rates, has
developed into a significant challenge affecting the lives of people and
the prospects for sustainable development, especially in hot and arid
regions like the Middle East. According to predictions, over 5 billion
people worldwide will face water shortages by 2050 [7]; consequently,

Desalination plants are usually divided into two main groups of
thermal desalination units (such as multi-stage flash (MSF) and multi-
effect distillation (MED)) and membrane separation systems (including
electrodialysis (ED) and reverse osmosis (RO)) [8]. Improving the per-
formance of SWDP has been investigated in various studies. Several
studies have analyzed different desalination processes using energy,
exergy, and economic analyses. Alasfour et al. [9] conducted a thermal
analysis of various multi-effect thermal vapor compression configura-
tions, revealing that the primary sources of exergy destruction are the
steam ejector and evaporators. Mabrouk et al. [10] performed a ther-

analyzed a large MSF distillation plant thermodynamically, indicating a
significant exergy destruction rate of 77.7 % in the MSF unit, which
suggests substantial opportunities for efficiency improvements. Finally,
Saffari et al. [12] presented a comprehensive exergy analysis of a
multiple-effect distiller with thermal vapor compression, highlighting its
operational effectiveness and cost efficiency in the desalination in-
dustry. However, despite solving the water crisis, SWDP can pose sig-
nificant environmental challenges. The required thermal or electrical
energies in SWDP are usually supplied by burning fossil fuels in the
boilers [13], resulting in the release of sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon di-
oxide (CO2), and other greenhouse gases and pollutants which have
harmful environmental effects [14]. Furthermore, the temperature and
concentration of brine effluent in industrial desalination plants are
generally high. In thermal SWDP, the temperature of the brine can vary,
depending on factors such as motivation steam and the number of
stages, typically falling between 40 and 50 ◦C. Moreover, according to
environmental regulations, the brine concentration from the thermal
and RO desalination plants should not exceed 70,000 ppm [15].
Nowadays, this hot and concentrated wastewater is discharged directly
into surface waters (such as rivers, seas, or oceans) and evaporation
ponds or injected into deep wells, which have numerous detrimental
effects on the environment [16]. The direct discharge of hot and
concentrated brine into the oceans and seas poses a threat to marine life
by upsetting the ecosystem’s balance due to its thermal and chemical
pollutions; for instance, coral bleaching, and the decrease in plankton
and fish populations are two of the most significant side effects of this
discharge [17]. In addition, the surface disposal of brine affects the
underground water and soil quality [18]. Considering these serious
environmental problems, and the cost of constructing and expanding
water supply systems to meet the needs of industry and the growing
population, it is necessary to develop and use efficient methods to
maximize high-quality water production and minimize energy con-
sumption and harmful environmental effects of water treatment pro-
cesses. One of the most useful and cost-effective methods is the
application of integrated energy and process systems (IEPS), in which,
by combining different systems, various types of energies (heating,
cooling, and power), high-quality water, and valuable chemicals can be
simultaneously produced. The implementation of IEPS is a high-reliable
approach to sustainable development in different industries and soci-
eties by reducing the emission of environmental pollution [19].
So far, many studies have been carried out on the application of IEPS
to improve efficiency and reduce environmental impacts. One of the
most efficient methods for supplying the required energy (especially
thermal energy) of SWDP is the simultaneous production of water and
power using a proper IEPS. The integration of power plants and SWDP

emissions and the cost of desalinated water production; energy expenses
account for 30 % of this cost [20]. Ahmadi et al. [21] investigated the
integration of MED-TVC units with gas turbine power plants from the
energy, exergy, and economic perspectives. The findings show that the
temperature of flue gases entering the gas turbine and the pressure ratio

exergy efficiency and the flow rate of freshwater production. Harandi

This research reveals that increasing top brine temperature (TBT) from

to 2.0 $/m3. They also reported the most efficient state for the studied
integrated system using multi-objective optimization by gain output

12.2 and 12058 K$ /yr, respectively. Kaabinejadian et al. [23] investi-

hybrid desalination plant with integrated latent heat thermal energy
storage. In their study, they used multi-objective optimization with a
genetic algorithm to enhance system performance. Their findings show
that integrating multi-effect distillation, thermal vapor compression,
and humidification-dehumidification units leads to a total cost rate of
0.548 $/s and a sustainability index of 1.54. Adding the humidification-
dehumidification unit increases freshwater production by 0.5 % and
raises costs by 0.1 %. Wu et al. [24] studied the integration of an LT-
MED desalination system with a nuclear power plant (NPP) to
enhance operational flexibility for peak shaving. By extracting main
steam, inlet reheat steam, and outlet reheat steam from the NPP, the
power output ratios were reduced to 12 %, 65 %, and 61 %, respectively.
The optimized system achieved a minimum water production cost of
0.64 $/m3 at 65 % output power, while exergy efficiency improved by
7.9 %, 18.3 %, and 25.6 % compared to AP1000, based on steam
extraction points. Ghadamyari et al. [25] proposed an integrated system
combining a gas turbine (GT), molten carbonate fuel cell (MCFC), single-
effect absorption refrigeration, and multi-effect desalination with ther-
mal vapor compression (MED-TVC). Their parametric study revealed
that the MCFC and GT produced 96.2 MW and 50.32 MW of power,
respectively, with a freshwater output of 126.9 kg/s. The system’s
electrical efficiency reached 61.83 %, with CO2 emissions at 389.3
kgCO2/MW and an emission penalty cost of 926.8 $/h.
Despite evaluating numerous integrated water and power production
units in the literature and proposing various methods to reduce both
water desalination costs and fossil fuel consumption, the management of
the brine effluent from desalination plants has received limited atten-
tion. This hot, saline wastewater poses significant environmental chal-
lenges, yet existing studies have rarely addressed these issues, some of
which are mentioned below.
Martel et al. [26] presented a feasible solution for using RO system
effluent to prevent its disposal into the sea while simultaneously adding
value via its conversion into valuable chemicals. In this research, the RO
unit effluent, which its salt content is about double that of seawater, has
been used in a Chlor-alkali process plant (CAPP) to produce various
chemicals, such as hydrogen, chlorine, and caustic soda. In another
study, conducted experimentally in 2012, RO desalination wastewater
was processed to be used as a feed for a CAPP [27]; an ED system was
applied for concentrating the wastewater of RO up to 280 mg/L of NaCl.
Mavukkandy et al. [28] have investigated the extraction of minerals
from SWDP effluent. This method was assessed as a feasible approach to
prevent and stop the discharge of hot concentrated brine into surface
waters such as oceans, which causes environmental concerns, and
consequently supplies a new source of minerals to meet the growing
demand of the global population. Chen et al. [29] have investigated the
integration of an evaporative crystallizer, with a MED desalination unit
to use and prevent discharging the brine effluent of a SWDP into the
ocean to achieve toward a zero liquid discharge (ZLD) system. This ZLD
system can be effectively utilized to convert the environmentally hazard
brine effluent into water and salt which are valuable products. Thummar
et al. [30] developed a dynamic model of a chlor-alkali membrane
electrolyzer cell to evaluate its operational flexibility when coupled with
a wind energy source. By improving previous models, they created a
more accurate representation of modern chlor-alkali plants and simu-
lated the system’s performance with varying wind power throughout the
day. The simulations showed that the plant could manage the ramp rates
needed to adjust to fluctuations in wind power within its normal oper-
ating range. Additionally, they demonstrated that electrolyte concen-
trations could be effectively controlled by adjusting inlet flows and
concentrations. In another study, Tu et al. [31] proposed a new strategy
called reclaimed seawater discharge (RSD) for treating desalination
brine to seawater levels for disposal. This research aims to reduce
environmental impact and energy wastage associated with traditional
brine discharge methods. They report that by coupling the RSD process
with resource recovery and CO2 capture techniques, brine salinity can be
reduced from (56.0 ± 2.1 g/L) to seawater level (32.0 ± 1.4 g/L).
Additionally, the process recovers valuable resources such as magne-
sium hydroxide and chlorine and achieves net climate change impact
abatement of 9.90 CO2 e/m3.
To date, numerous studies have been conducted on water desalina-
tion systems, energy supply methods, and efficiency increasing. More-
over, a few investigations have been conducted to manage properly the
concentrated saline wastewater, independent of energy supply and
increasing the efficiency of SWDP to minimize its environmental impact.
However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no comprehensive
released research has proposed an IEPS to produce power, water, and
valuable chemicals, simultaneous with overcoming the challenging
environmental problems of the discharging hot and concentrated brine
effluent of SWDP into the seas and oceans.
This study presents an innovative multi-generation system for pro-
ducing power, water, hydrogen, chlorine, and caustic soda, while
simultaneously minimizing environmental impacts related to green-
house gas emissions and the thermal and chemical pollution of the
discharged cooling water from the power plant and brine from the
desalination unit into the seas and oceans. This studied IEPS, focuses on
retrofitting and integrating a combined cycle power plant (CCPP),
located in the coastal regions of the Persian Gulf, with a MED-TVC
seawater desalination plant and a CAPP. In the proposed integrated
system, an existing CCPP supplies the required electrical and thermal
energies of the SWDP and CAPP, resulting in the reduction of fuel con-
sumption and greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, to mitigate
thermal pollution in the integrated system, a portion of the returned
cooling water from the steam surface condenser of the CCPP is fed into
the SWDP during the cold months of the year. The hot concentrated
brine is also utilized as suitable feed for the CAPP to avoid thermal and
chemical pollutions of the discharged brine from the SWDP into surface
water, thereby achieving an integrated zero brine discharge (ZBD) sys-
tem. After process integration, the proposed system was optimized using
a multi-objective optimization method (genetic algorithm), focusing on
enhancing exergy efficiency and minimizing the payback period (POR)
to achieve optimal capacity and operating conditions of the units. Sub-
sequently, the integrated system at the optimal point was comprehen-
sively evaluated from energy, exergy, economic, and environmental
perspectives. Finally, the impact of key operational parameters on the
performance of the proposed IEPS was studied.

2.1. System description
A scheme of the proposed integrated system composed of CCPP,
SWDP, and CAPP is depicted in Fig. 1 as a block flow diagram (BFD). In
the existing setup, these three plants are operated independently
without any mass or energy exchange. In the proposed system, a retrofit
design was performed to integrate both SWDP and CAPP with the
existing CCPP. The primary objective of this integrated system is to
efficiently produce power, desalinated water, and valuable chemicals
such as hydrogen, chlorine, and caustic soda. In addition, it aims to
minimize environmental impacts such as the hot concentrated brine
discharge into the sea and air pollution caused by fossil fuels burning;
the most important minimization is the application of hot concentrated
brine (discharged from SWDP) as the feed for CAPP to control and
mitigate its harmful thermal and chemical pollutions.
2.1.1. Combined cycle power plant
Electrical energy is generated in the studied integrated system using
a CCPP consisting of two identical modules; the process flow diagram
(PFD) of this CCPP is shown in Fig. 2. Each CCPP module consists of two
162 MW gas turbines, two dual pressure HRSGs, one 160 MW steam
turbine-electric generator (STG), and a once-through steam surface
condenser cooling system; the details of operation and related variables
(under real industrial conditions) can be found in the research con-
ducted by Pourali et al. [32]. In the integrated system, a portion of the
low-pressure (LP) steam generated by HRSGs is transferred to SWDP and
CAPP to supply their required thermal energy for proper operation.
Moreover, a part of the return cooling water from the steam surface
condenser is routed into SWDP to enhance the efficiency of the studied
system during cold months.
2.1.2. Seawater desalination plant
Fig. 3 illustrates the integrated SWDP with the retrofitted CCPP; the
main components of SWDP are a condenser, a thermo-vapor compressor
(TVC), evaporators, flash boxes, and pumps. In this unit, feedwater
(either seawater or the returned cooling water from the steam surface
condenser) is heated in the down condenser to increase its temperature
to the necessary requirements and a portion of this stream is discharged
into the sea, while the remainder is directed into the evaporators where
it is undergone conversion into vapor and hot brine. A fraction of the
vapor derived from the final evaporator and flash box is delivered to the
TVC, which is operated by a motive LP steam (supplied by CCPP). This
process decreases the operating pressure to facilitate the conversion of
feedwater into desalinated water (the primary product) and hot
concentrated brine (a byproduct). In general, the hot concentrated brine
is released into the sea in existing desalination plants, causing serious
environmental issues such as chemical and thermal pollution; however,
in the proposed system, the discharged hot concentrated brine of SWDP
is utilized as the feed for the studied CAPP to control and prevent
environmental hazards, and optimize desalinated water production.
2.1.3. Chlor-alkali process plant
This plant is the third section of the proposed integrated system,
consisting of six process units of brine purification, electrolysis, caustic
soda
concentration,
chlorine
recovery,
hydrogen
recovery,
and
hydrogen chloride production [33]. A simplified PFD of the investigated
CAPP is portrayed in Fig. 4; heat exchangers and pumps are not shown in
this simplified scheme, and all shown pieces of equipment of this process
plant (in Fig. 4) are listed in Table 1.
The first part of this process is the purification of brine. In this stage,
the heated incoming brine from the desalination unit is mixed with
dechlorinated anolyte returned from the electrolysis section in the
saturation tank. Then, rock salt is added to this mixture to increase its
salt concentration, making it suitable for entry into the electrolysis
section. The resulting brine is then mixed with calcium chloride (CaCl2)
solution to remove its sulfate content. After this stage, the brine stream
passes through the pieces of equipment shown in the figure, where
compounds including Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+, and SiO2, which are detri-
mental to the electrolysis process, are removed. Following pH adjust-
ment is performed in the pH adjustment tank to approximately 2
[33–35], and then brine is ready to enter the electrolysis process. In the
electrolysis process, the purified brine after being heated via heat ex-
changers, proceeds through the anolyte cycle to the anodic section,
where sodium chloride undergoes electrolysis, resulting in the produc-
tion of chlorine gas (Eq. (8)). Concurrently, deionized water, generated
by the SWDP, enters the catholyte cycle and then the cathodic section,
where it is electrolyzed to produce hydrogen and hydroxide ions (Eq.
(9)). These hydroxide ions are then combined with sodium ions at the
cathode to synthesize caustic soda (Eq. (10)).
Subsequently, chlorine gas undergoes treatment in the chlorine re-
covery section to remove organic impurities and reduce its water con-
tent to below 12 ppm [33]. Simultaneously, 99.5 wt% of the hydrogen
gas is directed to the hydrogen recovery section to enhance its purity to
over 95 vol% [33]. The 33 % caustic soda solution is transferred to the
caustic concentration section to achieve a 50 % concentration [33]. In
the final stage, the remaining 0.5 % of hydrogen gas reacts with a
portion of chlorine gas from the chlorine recovery unit in the hydrogen
chloride production section, resulting in the formation of hydrogen
chloride acid, which is utilized for pH adjustment of the brine. A
comprehensive description of the chlor-alkali process is provided in the
supplementary file.

2.2. Modeling
In this study, various components of the proposed IEPS, comprising a
combined cycle power plant, a MED-TVC desalination plant, and a chlor-
alkali process plant, were modeled utilizing thermodynamic principles
and MATLAB software. A multi-objective optimization was carried out
to maximize exergy efficiency and minimize the payback period. Finally,
to thoroughly and accurately assess the performance of the proposed
IEPS, energy, exergy, economic, and environmental analyses were per-
formed. The necessary equations for modeling and analyzing the IEPS
are discussed in the following sections.
2.2.1. Thermodynamic assumptions
To model the IEPS, the following assumptions have been employed
in the mathematical modeling process.
• All processes are in steady state.

2.2.7. Optimization
When an energy system has several competing objectives, multi-
objective optimization is applied. In this aspect, when one goal im-
proves, another falls. As a result, there is no one optimal solution that
simultaneously minimizes or maximizes all objective functions. Instead,
optimization produces a group of ideal solutions known as Pareto
optimal solutions, and the related objective function values are known
as the Pareto frontier [64]. To optimize the proposed IEPS, after
modeling the various units of the system using the equations presented
in the previous sections and integrating them, the genetic algorithm was
employed. One of the most notable features of the genetic algorithm
method is its ability to evaluate a range of potential solutions simulta-
neously, rather than focusing on a single solution at a time [65]. The
genetic algorithm method fundamentally relies on assessing a group of
potential solutions to generate better approximations toward finding the
optimal solution. Among its variants, NSGA-II, which is based on a ge-
netic algorithm, stands out as a highly effective multi-criteria evolu-
tionary algorithm for optimizing energy systems which offers several
benefits, including robustness and rapid estimation of population dis-
tances [66]. In the optimization of the IEPS, decision variables are
chosen from the key design parameters of each plant, as detailed in
Table 16. The range of variations for each parameter is also specified.
The objective functions of the system include exergy efficiency and
payback period. The genetic algorithm is employed to simultaneously
optimize the system by adjusting the decision variables within the
defined ranges, aiming to maximize exergy efficiency and minimize the
payback period. The design algorithm for the proposed IEPS is illus-
trated in Fig. 5.

3.2. Optimization results
This study explores multi-objective optimization of the IEPS using a
genetic algorithm. The optimization focuses on simultaneously maxi-
mizing exergy efficiency and minimizing the POR. The decision vari-
ables for this optimization and their ranges are detailed in Table 14. The
Pareto frontier distribution of the objective functions is illustrated in
Fig. 7. In this research, the optimal point was identified using the
TOPSIS method and its results are reported in Table 15. TOPSIS method
identifies the best solution from the Pareto front by comparing each
option to the ideal and the least desirable solutions. This method mea-
sures the distance of each option from these two reference points. The
best option is the one that is closest to the ideal solution and farthest
from the least desirable solution, effectively balancing multiple criteria.
Subsequently, the proposed IEPS at the optimal point (as introduced
in Table 15) undergoes a comprehensive and detailed evaluation. In this
analysis, the integrated system is assessed from the perspectives of en-
ergy, exergy, economic, and environmental aspects.

3.3.3. Effect of brine salinity on the exergy efficiency of SWDP, energy and

The concentration of discharged brine from SWDP is one of the most
significant factors affecting its performance. The impact of increasing
the concentration of discharged brine from SWDP on POR, GOR, the
exergy efficiency of the SWDP, as well as the exergy and energy effi-

Consequently, the exergy and energy efficiency of the IEPS, the exergy
efficiency of the SWDP, and the GOR show an upward trend with
increasing brine concentration. According to Fig. 16, the elevation of

and 5.47 % to 12.85, 3.52, 58.88 %, 41.07 %, and 5.95 %, respectively.

increases, while the LP steam flow rate remains constant, the brine and

3.3.4. Effects of current density, brine temperature, and concentration on

The cell voltage of the electrolyzer is a function of temperature, brine
concentration, and current density. The effect of these variables on the
electrochemical cell voltage in the CAPP is investigated in this study and
is illustrated in Fig. 17. As shown in the figure, increasing the current
density from 1.5 to 8 kA/m2 results in an increase in the cell voltage
from 2.7 to 4.8 V. Contrary to current density, an increase in the cell
temperature and brine concentration reduces the resistance to electric
current in the electrolyte. Consequently, the cell voltage exhibits a
decreasing trend. Fig. 17 demonstrates that with an increase in cell
temperature and brine concentration from 50 ◦C and 160 g NaCl/l to 90
◦C and 300 g NaCl/l respectively, the cell voltage changes from 3.92 and
3.8 V to 3.59 and 3.55 V. Based on the results of this study, the effect of
temperature and brine concentration on membrane cell voltage is
negligible compared to current density.
3.3.5. Effect of current density on the period of return, energy, and exergy
efficiency of the IEPS and CAPP
In Fig. 18, the influence of current density on POR, the exergy and
energy efficiency of IEPS, as well as the exergy and energy efficiency of
CAPP, are illustrated. According to the Eqs. (13,14, 25,26, 32–34 and
36–38), with the increase in current density, the production of products
(chlorine, hydrogen, and caustic soda), the voltage in the membrane
cell, and consequently the electricity consumption increase. Therefore,
if the increase in electricity consumption surpasses the growth in
product production, the exergy and energy efficiency of the IEPS, as well
as the exergy efficiency of the CAPP, will exhibit a declining trend within
the specified range. According to Fig. 18, elevating the current density
from 1.5 to 8.0 kA/m2 leads to a change in the exergy and energy effi-
ciency of IEPS from 43.05 % and 62.9 % to 34.76 % and 49.7 %,
respectively. Moreover, the figure indicates that as the current density
increases up to 3.5 kA/m2, the energy efficiency of CAPP improves by 3
% and reaches 25.17 %. However, with further increases in current
density up to 8 kA/m2, due to higher energy consumption compared to
hydrogen production, the energy efficiency experiences a declining
trend, decreasing from 25 % to 21.39 %. As evident, with an increase in
current density from 1.5 to 5 kA/m2, the POR decreases from 4.96 to
3.49 years. However, when the current density exceeds 5 kA/m2, the rise
in capital cost associated with CAPP surpasses the rise in revenue from
product sales (due to increased electricity consumption and investment
costs related to electrolyzers), leading to a change in the trend at this
point. Consequently, the POR increases from 3.49 to 4.29 years with an
increase in current density from 5 to 8.0 kA/m2.

Water, energy, and the environment are among the most essential
needs of human society that exert a direct influence on the quality of life
and
sustainable

of
communities.
Accordingly,
This
research focuses on retrofitting a combined cycle power plant with a
MED-TVC desalination plant and a chlor-alkali process plant. The
innovative integration aims to produce electricity, water, and valuable
chemicals (such as hydrogen, chlorine, and caustic soda) as well as
reduce fuel consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, and environmental
impact while ensuring long-term viability. The results indicate that the
system, with a high energy efficiency of 58.88 %, is capable of producing

736.74 MW of electricity, along with 186 kg/s of desalinated water,
15.24 kg/s of chlorine, 0.59 kg/s of hydrogen, and 31.98 kg/s of caustic
soda. The integration of cooling water of the CCPP into SWDP during the
colder seasons showed promising results, as it not only reduced the
discharge of cooling water into the sea by 3.6 % and prevented thermal
pollution, but also led to a 17.60 % increase in energy efficiency and a
17.16 % rise in the GOR of the SWDP system. A significant advancement
in the proposed system is the utilization of discharged brine from the
desalination unit within the chlor-alkali plant. This integration not only
converts the system into a ZBD configuration, effectively eliminating the
environmental impact of brine disposal into the sea. By eliminating the
previous environmental limit on brine concentration (70,000 ppm), the
system allows for an increase in the salinity of brine up to 150,000 ppm,
the highest technically feasible level. As a result, the SWDP experiences
an 8.44 % increase in GOR and an 8.20 % improvement in energy effi-
ciency. Moreover, this higher brine concentration reduces the amount of
rock salt required for the electrolyzer, leading to annual cost savings of
$42,000. One of the significant achievements of this IEPS is achieving an
exergy efficiency of 41.07 %. This result was obtained through a more
detailed exergy analysis compared to previous studies on CCPP and
MED-TVC desalination plants. Notably, this study includes a compre-
hensive exergy analysis of the chlor-alkali unit for the first time, estab-
lishing a valuable foundation for future research in this area. In the
proposed IEPS, the desalination unit (SWDP) and the chlor-alkali plant
(CAPP) have successfully achieved zero greenhouse gas emissions. The
system’s emissions are remarkably low, with CO at just 0.542 kg/s, CO2
at 379.18 kg/MW, and NOx at 0.0042 kg/s, highlighting its exceptional
environmental performance. The total investment cost for the IEPS
amounts to 438.4 M$, with an annual income of127.09 M$. When
considering the time value of money and income tax, the return on in-
vestment is realized in the fifth year. However, excluding the time value
of money, the payback period is reduced to under four years.
Given the increasing demand for integrated energy and desalination
systems (IEPS) to optimize energy use and minimize environmental
impacts, future studies should focus not only on enhancing system ef-
ficiency and reducing payback periods but also on addressing several
key areas. First, the current study simplified calculations by excluding
heat exchangers, which should be included in future research to improve
accuracy. Additionally, replacing the MED-TVC desalination unit with
alternative technologies like RO and MSF should be explored. Given the
global expansion of desalination plants, a comprehensive metric to
assess environmental impacts (particularly heat and chemical pollution
from brine discharge) is needed. To achieve green IEPS and net-zero
emissions,
integrating
renewable
power
plants
is
recommended.
Furthermore, the potential use of hydrogen produced within the system
as a clean fuel for future energy applications should be investigated.
Lastly, considering the importance of risk analysis in ensuring safety,
operational reliability, environmental protection, and minimizing
financial risks, it is recommended that future studies evaluate the pro-
posed energy system through the lens of risk analysis.
