A B S T R A C T
The increasing deployment of multi-energy microgrids (MEMGs) as aggregators is transforming the operation 
of modern distribution systems, particularly during outage events. However, the growing diversity of MEMG 
architectures and their interdependency on power and water distribution systems introduce significant 
coordination and privacy challenges, particularly with the increasing adoption of green hydrogen. To address 
these challenges, this paper proposes a privacy-preserving distributed coordination framework for multiple 
independent operators and aggregators. MEMGs are categorized based on geographical resource availability 
into coast-based and hydrogen-based configurations. Unlike existing studies that confine MEMG interactions 
to the power distribution system, this work extends their role to coupled power–water operations, where 
coast-based MEMGs support the water distribution system through desalination, while hydrogen-based MEMGs 
can utilize water for hydrogen production to support the power distribution system during outage events. A 
consensus-based alternating direction method of multipliers (C-ADMM) optimization model is developed to 
coordinate multiple aggregators and operators while preserving their autonomy and privacy. The proposed 
framework is validated on an IEEE 33-bus power distribution system interconnected with a 15-node water 
distribution system. Extensive outage case studies shows its effectiveness in enhancing resilience and mitigating 
cascading failures, with diverse MEMGs providing average support of 39% and 22.4% to the power and water 
distribution systems.
1. Introduction
The ongoing transition toward green energy has strengthened the 
interdependence between various energy distribution systems. In par-
ticular, the rapid expansion of green hydrogen production through 
water electrolysis and renewable sources has reinforced the coupling 
between power and water distribution systems (PDS and WDS) [1], 
where green hydrogen supports PDS via electricity generation through 
fuel cells (FC) [2]. However, this interdependence introduces vulnera-
bilities; an outage in one distribution system can cascade into another. 
For example, a disruption in PDS during a natural disaster can hinder 
water pumping in WDS, while pipeline failures in WDS can affect 
hydrogen production and electric power regeneration. The situation 
is further complicated by the growing penetration of distributed en-
ergy resources combined with multi-energy generation units, leading 
to new structures called multi-energy microgrids (MEMGs) [3]. With 
localized control capabilities, MEMGs can enhance the resilience of 
energy systems [4]. Their configurations vary by region; for instance,

Despite the strong interdependence between power and water in-
frastructures, most of the existing studies consider these systems in-
dependently, particularly from the resilience enhancement perspec-
tive [6]. For PDS, the resilience enhancement strategies include dis-
tribution system splitting into microgrids for self-healing, reserving 
storage capacity in the form of hydrogen for long-term storage, and 
network reconfiguration to minimize cascaded failures [7]. Similarly, 
for WDS, strategies include upgrading the capacities of pumping sta-
tions and water storage, and optimizing the pipeline network topol-
ogy. However, given the high impact and low probability of ex-
treme events, these measures entail risks of over-investment and in-
efficient allocation of flexible resources [8]. To address this limi-
tation, recent studies [9,10] have aimed to enhance resilience by 
explicitly considering the interdependence between PDS and WDS.

To enable effective resource management and address the chal-
lenges associated with over-investment, the concept of MEMGs as 
distributed resource aggregators has gained attention. MEMGs provide 
flexible services to integrated distribution networks, thereby avoiding 
financial and operational risks associated [20]. In this context, several 
studies have focused on the resilience-oriented operation of MEMGs 
due to their enhanced control capabilities in managing small-scale dis-
tributed devices [21]. For instance, planning and operation strategies 
have been developed in [12] to enhance the resilience of MEMGs, 
while energy hubs have been incorporated into integrated energy sys-
tems to achieve overall resilience enhancement [22]. MEMGs, with 
their diverse multi-generation units, can serve multiple demand dimen-
sions, including natural gas [13], heat [23], and water [14] distribu-
tion systems. This property makes them an effective solution during 
fault events, as they can provide critical services to the integrated 
distribution systems.
Various types of MEMGs have been explored in the literature, 
differing in their internal component configurations. Recently, there 
has been a growing focus on hydrogen-based MEMGs (H𝜇G), which 
incorporate hydrogen generation units to enhance resilience of PDS by 
converting stored hydrogen into electric power through FC units [15]. 
However, the deployment of multi-generation units within MEMGs is 
highly influenced by geographical conditions. For instance, the concept 
of coast-based MEMGs(C𝜇G) are introduced in [14,16], where the 
system is equipped with a desalination unit (DES) and water storage 
facilities, using its proximity to the sea to procure pure water. This 
design consideration has become increasingly important for hydrogen 
generation in H𝜇G [11]. Different types of MEMGs can coordinate to 
provide cost-effective solutions while easing the burden on integrated 
distribution system. H𝜇G enhances the resilience of PDS by converting 
stored hydrogen into electric power through FC units. Meanwhile, 
C𝜇G can strengthen WDS in affected areas during pipeline disruptions, 
thereby improving the resilience of multiple interconnected infrastruc-
tures. This setup gives rise to a multiple-entity structure consisting of 
different operators and diverse aggregators, each aiming at its own 
objectives; thus, the coordination between them is crucial for efficient 
operation.
Several studies [17] have presented interactive frameworks between 
MEMGs and distirbution system operators (DSO); however, these ap-
proaches focus primarily on the DSO perspective and do not adequately 
consider the private ownership of MEMGs, which operate their own 
energy management systems. To address this limitation, distributed 
algorithms have emerged as a major solution [18]. In the literature, two 
main types of distributed approaches are identified: (i) treating each 
distributed energy resources as an independent system [24], and (ii) 
considering a multi-area energy management system [25]. In the first 
approach, the commonly used method is consensus-based algorithms, 
where each DER acts as an agent that interacts with others to achieve 
coordination. In the multi-area approach, each MEMGs is treated as 
an independent entity that interacts with other MEMGs or the DSO. 
Here, the ADMM is the widely adopted algorithm. For instance, [26] 
proposed a distributed ADMM algorithm for solving the economic 
dispatch problem with general convex cost functions. In [19] proposed 
a collaborative aggregator-agent model for interactions between DSOs 
and MEMGs to enhance system resilience using ADMM. However, these 
require transmitting data from one subsystem to all others, which en-
hances the communication complexity. To accelerate the convergence 
rate, the consensus algorithms in conjunction with ADMM were studied 
in [27], and the distributed consensus problem within the ADMM 
framework was established in [28].
Despite significant progress in integrating multiple energy dimen-
sions into modern energy systems, the autonomy of MEMGs remains 
largely underexplored. Existing studies predominantly treat MEMGs 
as passive service providers, overlooking the implications of private 
ownership and the independent operational objectives of local aggrega-
tors. Moreover, resource availability is inherently location-dependent; 
for instance, C𝜇Gs equipped with desalination facilities can serve as 
an alternative water source for the WDS, while H𝜇Gs may procure 
water from C𝜇Gs for hydrogen production. This diversity in MEMG 
configurations and interdependencies has received limited attention, 
as most studies assume a single homogeneous MEMG architecture 
and investigate MEMGs solely from the PDS perspective, neglecting 
their interactions with other critical infrastructures, particularly the 
WDS. This limitation becomes especially critical during outage events, 
where failures may propagate across interconnected systems. Finally, 
although few studies consider coupled power–water distribution sys-
tems, they adopt centralized optimization frameworks, which suffer 
from scalability limitations and vulnerability to single points of failure.
To address the aforementioned gaps, this study proposes a de-
centralized coordination framework for interdependent PDS-WDS with 
diverse MEMGs. Unlike conventional resilience-oriented approaches 
that primarily focus on the PDS, the proposed framework explicitly 
incorporates the WDS and its interactions with heterogeneous MEMGs. 
The framework enables coordinated operation among multiple local 
aggregators and system operators while investigating the contribution 
of internally diversified MEMGs in both PDS and WDS during out-
age events. Furthermore, extensive case studies under various outage 
scenarios are conducted to evaluate the role of diverse MEMGs in 
enhancing the resilience of the interdependent PDS–WDS. The pro-
posed framework also captures the effects of cascading failures across 
interconnected distribution systems by incorporating both power and 
water flow constraints. A detailed comparison between the proposed 
framework and existing studies is presented in Table 1. The main 
contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• Extended the heterogeneous MEMG framework from [14] to 
an interdependent PDS-WDS, explicitly capturing power–water–
hydrogen interactions among autonomous aggregators and sys-
tem operators.

2. System description and motivation
Fig. 1 presents the conceptual design of the proposed integrated 
system, which comprises interdependent PDS-WDS. Within this frame-
work, several MEMGs are deployed as local aggregators. Motivated 
by high renewable energy penetration and geographical character-
istics, two types of MEMG are considered: C𝜇Gs and H𝜇Gs, whose 
configurations are based on our previous study [14] and described as 
follows.
1. Coastal-based MEMG (C𝜇G): The C𝜇Gs are located near the 
sea and comprise a DES unit, pumped storage, and water load 
as water-side components, alongside RES that include wind tur-
bines (WT) in addition to PVs due to high coastal wind speeds, 
ESS, and electric load on the electrical side. Owing to their 
water procurement capability, C𝜇Gs can engage in both water 
transactions with the WDS and power transactions with the PDS. 
This capability is particularly valuable during fault events in 
the water network, where C𝜇Gs can support the faulted area by 
operating DES or WSS units powered by RES or PDS, thereby 
acting as service providers to the WDS, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
2. Hydrogen-based MEMG (H𝜇G): In addition to electrical com-
ponents similar to those in C𝜇Gs, the PVs are only considered 
as RES. H𝜇Gs are equipped with hydrogen-based components, 
including EL, FC, and HSS. During periods of excess renew-
able energy generation, either generated internally or supplied 
from the PDS, H𝜇Gs absorb the surplus electrical energy by 
converting it into hydrogen via the EL unit, thereby alleviating 
grid congestion. Since the EL unit requires water, H𝜇Gs procure 
the necessary water from the WDS. The stored hydrogen can 
subsequently be reconverted into electrical power via the FC unit 
to support the faulted area of the PDS, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
The PDS and WDS are coupled through two major interdependen-
cies. First, the WDS relies on the PDS electric power to operate its water 
pumps (WP), which overcome elevation differences and pressure losses 
across the water network. Second, the WDS indirectly supports the PDS 
through H𝜇G operations, where water is supplied for hydrogen produc-
tion during periods of excess RES generation to overcome curtailment 
and increase RES utilization via the EL unit, with the stored hydrogen 
later reconverted into electrical power through the FC unit during fault 
events.
Effective coordination is essential for managing such multi-energy 
coupling. Fig. 2 compares the conventional and proposed approaches. 
As shown in Fig. 2(a), existing studies consider MEMGs within the PDS

To address these limitations, this study models the WDS as an active 
coupling element, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b). Hydrogen-water coupling 
within H𝜇Gs enables the WDS to support the PDS during outage sce-

during pipeline disruptions. Since multiple entities coexist within the 
integrated system, each pursuing independent operational objectives, 
a C-ADMM-based distributed coordination framework is proposed, the 
details of which are presented in Section 4.
Within the proposed framework, the power distribution system op-
erator (PDSO) and water distribution system operator (WDSO) manage 
their respective networks, while each MEMG is independently operated 
by its own local aggregator. Each entity solves its local optimization 
problem and coordinates with others only for energy and water trans-
actions, as shown in Fig. 2(b). Specifically, the PDSO coordinates power 
transactions with MEMG operators, the WDSO supplies water to H𝜇Gs 
and C𝜇Gs to fulfill their operational demands, and can also receive 
water from C𝜇Gs as an alternative source during pipeline disruptions. 
In addition, the WDSO procures power from the PDSO to power its 
pumping operations.

The objective function of the PDSO, given in (2), comprises the cost 
of energy transactions with the utility grid, the 𝑀+ 𝑁 MEMGs, and

terms for load shedding and renewable curtailment. Since MEMGs 
act as aggregators, the PDSO trades energy with them using its own 
buy/sell price pair, rather than a separate trading price. Specifically, 
when the PDSO imports power from a MEMG, the transaction is settled 
at the PDSO’s sell price 𝜆𝑝𝑠𝑒𝑙𝑙; conversely, when the PDSO exports 
power to a MEMG, the transaction is settled at the PDSO’s buy price 
𝜆𝑝𝑏𝑢𝑦. This is the reverse of how the PDSO transacts with the external 
utility grid, where it purchases at 𝜆𝑝𝑏𝑢𝑦 and sells at 𝜆𝑝𝑠𝑒𝑙𝑙. Because 
distributed generation, storage, and renewable resources can co-exist 
within the PDS, this pricing scheme is designed to make local exchange 
with MEMGs comparatively more attractive than transacting with the 
utility grid, thereby alleviating congestion at the utility interface while 
promoting localized resource utilization and renewable integration.

3.3. Linear approximation of non-linear constraints
Constraints given in (16), (21), and (23) are inherently nonlinear 
in nature. These constraints may introduce non-convexity in the prob-
lem and computationally intensive for large-scale distribution system, 
therefore, variants of piecewise linear approximation techniques are 
applied as follows: (i) the C𝜇G pump power consumption in (16) is 
linearized using the bi-variate piecewise McCormick relaxation tech-
nique [5]; (ii) the WDSO pump pressure head function in (21) is 
linearized using the bi-variate piecewise linear approximation [9]; 
(iii) the pressure head loss function in (23) is approximated using a 
uni-variate piecewise linear method [9]. The detailed constraints for-
mulation and performance comparison of adopted linear approximation 
technique is presented in our previous study [5] and in [9].

5.3.2. Internal components operation
In this study, MEMG diversification is considered to analyze the 
distinct participation of hydrogen- and water-related equipment in 
H𝜇Gs and C𝜇Gs, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The operating conditions 
under Case 5, representing the worst-case scenario, are examined to 
comprehensively assess the participation of all MEMGs.
• Hydrogen cycle in H𝜇Gs: Fig. 9(a) depicts the operation of the 
EL and FC units in the H𝜇Gs. The hydrogen cycle operates as 
follows: during intervals 15 and 16, excess renewable generation 
triggers intensive EL unit operation, which converts surplus elec-
trical energy into hydrogen, thereby alleviating grid congestion 
on the PDS side. The produced hydrogen is stored in the HSS, 
as shown in Fig. 9(b). Since no hydrogen demand is considered 
in H𝜇G2, the excess PV generation of 558 kW is converted into 
hydrogen through the EL unit. The stored hydrogen is later re-
converted into electricity through the FC unit during periods of 
low renewable generation. Particularly, in interval 17, the FC unit 
provides an additional 219 kW, in addition to the 530 kW PV 
generation in H𝜇G2, to support the electric demand and provide 
services to the PDS. This closed hydrogen cycle, from excess 
power to hydrogen storage and back to power, represents the role 
of H𝜇Gs as active energy buffers within the integrated system.
• Water-hydrogen coupling: Since EL operation requires water, 
H𝜇Gs receive an increased water supply from the DES units of 
C𝜇Gs (Fig. 9(c)) during intervals 15–18 to support hydrogen 
production. In particular, H𝜇G2 consumes 0.09 m3 of water at 
interval 16 to minimize the excess energy curtailment of 558 kW, 
while H𝜇G4 consumes about 0.19 m3 of water for hydrogen 
production to minimize the curtailment of 1401.2 kW of excess 
energy from the PDSO and local PV generation and support its 
hydrogen demand. This demonstrates the water-energy coupling 
between C𝜇Gs and H𝜇Gs, where water produced by the DES 
units directly enables hydrogen production and subsequent power 
generation via the FC units.
• Water equipment operation in C𝜇Gs: Fig. 9(b) shows that the 
WSS of C𝜇G1 remains inactive due to its higher-capacity DES unit 
(Fig. 9(c)), which operates continuously at 800 kW, providing 
sufficient water supply without the need for storage discharge. In 
contrast, the WSS of C𝜇G2 is discharged to meet the overall water 
demand, owing to its lower DES unit capacity of 500 kW. The 
overall water demands served by C𝜇G1 and C𝜇G2 are 1000.1 m3
and 552.7 m3, respectively, with their PV and WT generation 
supplying the electric power required by the DES units to provide 
water to the WDSO, which serves the water demands of MEMGs 
and WDSO.

6. Conclusion and future works
This paper presents an operation model for integrated power–water 
distribution systems comprising multiple independent operators and 
aggregators. Two types of MEMGs are considered as aggregators based 
on their geographical resource availability: coast-based MEMGs (C𝜇Gs) 
near the coast equipped with desalination units and pumped stor-
age, and hydrogen-based MEMGs (H𝜇Gs) equipped with electrolyzers, 
fuel cells, and hydrogen storage systems. A decentralized coordination 
framework is developed using the consensus-based alternating direction 
method of multipliers (C-ADMM) to model the independent operations 
of each operator and aggregator while preserving their autonomy and 
privacy. Simulations are performed on the IEEE 33-bus power distribu-
tion system interconnected with a 15-node water distribution system, 
with MEMGs connected on different buses and nodes. The proposed 
C-ADMM framework achieves near-optimal solutions with minimal 
deviation from the centralized approach, confirming the viability of 
privacy-preserving distributed coordination as a scalable alternative to 
centralized management. The results demonstrate that C𝜇Gs and H𝜇Gs 
exhibit complementary resilience contributions: C𝜇Gs support water 
distribution system operations and supply desalinated water for H𝜇G 
electrolysis, while H𝜇Gs absorb excess renewable energy through hy-
drogen production and subsequently reconvert the stored hydrogen into 
power via fuel cells to mitigate power distribution congestion during 
low renewable generation periods. Furthermore, the cascading failure 
analysis reveals a directional asymmetry in cross-system fault propaga-
tion, where faults in the power distribution system disproportionately 
reduce C𝜇G contributions to water distribution operations, highlighting 
the importance of power system fault mitigation in integrated infras-
tructure planning. Under worst-case scenarios, MEMG participation 
reaches 32.8% and 73.1% in the power distribution system and 59.0% 
and 19.2% in the water distribution system, respectively, quantitatively 
confirming the significant resilience value of heterogeneous MEMG 
integration.
Future work will focus on dynamic penalty parameter tuning and 
the development of resilience metrics for cascading failure assessment 
in coupled power–water distribution systems.
