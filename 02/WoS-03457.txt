A B S T R A C T
Wildfire risk to critical infrastructure is an increasing concern as the frequency and inten-
sity of wildfires continue to increase. Existing research has focused primarily on electrical 
infrastructure and watershed contamination, leaving other infrastructure sectors comparatively 
understudied. In this systematic literature review, we analyse the types of infrastructure that 
have been examined and summarise the methodologies used to characterise wildfire hazard, 
exposure, and vulnerability across power, water, transport, and highly hazardous industrial 
infrastructure. This review provides a consolidation of state-of-the-art approaches in the analysis 
of wildfire risk to critical infrastructure.
Key gaps identified include lack of hazard characterisations of non-flame wildfire hazards 
(smoke and firebrands), limited and asset-focused physical vulnerability analysis and related 
damage thresholds, poor linkages between regional hazard mapping, and limited consideration 
of critical infrastructure interdependencies. These gaps directly motivate methodological syner-
gies. For instance, detailed thermal and vulnerability modelling developed for water distribution 
pipes can be transferred to similar components in other sectors. Dynamic wildfire and smoke 
models for power and hazardous industrial infrastructure can be extended to sectors currently 
using static or occurrence-based metrics, and publicly produced regional hazard products 
could be integrated with sector-specific exposure and vulnerability models to create consistent, 
multi-scale risk assessments.
By systematically comparing risk characterisation approaches across sectors, this review 
highlights that critical infrastructure systems share sufficient commonalities, vulnerability 
mechanisms, and spatial scales to enable transfer and adaptation of wildfire risk assessment 
methodologies. Coordinated development and sharing of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability 
characterisation tools across sectors and the development of proactive adaptation plans would 
substantially improve the assessment and management of wildfire risk to critical infrastructure.

characterisation, analysis, and prediction on CI systems is a growing interest (Forzieri et al., 2018; Huddleston et al., 2022; 
Kumar et al., 2021). CI risks from flooding, earthquakes, windstorms, landslides, and volcanic hazards have been extensively 
investigated (Nirandjan et al., 2024; Wilson et al., 2014). In contrast, wildfire-related risk assessments have largely concentrated on 
power system infrastructure (Arango et al., 2023b; Bandara et al., 2023; Vazquez et al., 2022; Muhs et al., 2020). Although research 
interest is growing in recent years due to increasing impact and damages, there has still been significantly less research on wildfire 
impacts to other infrastructure sectors. Addressing this gap requires a clearer understanding of how wildfires affect different CI 
sectors and improved evaluation methodologies.
This review summarises and discusses the state of the art in wildfire risk assessment for power, water, transport, and highly 
hazardous industrial infrastructures (including the chemical and nuclear industries). These sectors were selected because of their 
societal relevance as CI systems and their documented vulnerability to wildfire events. Institutional frameworks also recognise 
several of these systems as critical infrastructure. For example, the European Commission’s Critical Entities Resilience Directive 
identifies Energy, Transport, Drinking Water, and Wastewater as key CI sectors (European Commission DG HOME, 2025), while in 
the United States the Chemical and Critical Manufacturing sectors are also classified as critical infrastructure (Cybersecurity and 
Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), 2025). In addition to their institutional selection as critical systems, these infrastructure 
sectors have been significantly impacted by wildfires. This section briefly introduces relevant recent wildfire impacts to each 
considered infrastructure sector.
Among all CI systems, the interactions between electrical infrastructure and wildfires are the most researched. Power grid 
ignitions have been linked to several destructive wildfires. The 2009 Victorian Bushfires Royal Commission found that five major 
Black Saturday fires were caused by power lines; due to electric arcs, ejected molten metal particles, and contact between live lines 
and vegetation (Victoria State Government, 2011)). In California, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) declared bankruptcy in 2020 after 
claims for over US $30 billion in wildfire-related damages (Jazebi et al., 2020). More recently, fallen power lines were attributed 
as likely ignition sources for the destructive 2023 Maui wildfire in Hawaii, and damaged power lines sparked the largest wildfire 
in Texas history in 2024 (Arbaje, 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025). Data suggest that power grid ignitions tend to occur during high wind 
conditions, producing disproportionately large and destructive fires (Mitchell, 2009).
Wildfire events have been associated with degraded water quality in the USA and Australia. Contamination of drinking-water 
supplies can result from increased sediments, nutrients, and organic pollutants in reservoirs and catchments due to post-fire erosion, 
as well as from direct damage to water supply infrastructure. In 2017 elevated concentrations of benzene and other volatile organic 
compounds (VOCs) were detected in the drinking water of a neighbourhood affected by the Tubbs Fire in California (Solomon et al., 
2021). Following the 2018 Camp Fire in California, VOC contamination was found in water distribution pipes (Proctor et al., 2020). 
During the 2019–2020 Australian wildfire season, the drinking water supply for 5.5 million people was affected (Robinne et al., 
2021).
Beyond water systems, various transport infrastructure have also experienced significant wildfire related impacts; causing both 
physical damage and operational interruptions. In road networks, major fires have compromised evacuation routes, reduced travel 
reliability, and isolated communities. In Canada, the Railway Extreme Heat and Fire Risk Mitigation Rules issued by Transport Canada 
require railway companies to reduce speeds during heat waves, increase inspections, and maintain wildfire-mitigation plans during 
elevated risk periods (Transport Canada, 2022). Airports face increasing disruptions due to wildfires. In 2025, Marseille Airport in 
France temporarily suspended flights as a wildfire approached the airport (Reuters, 2025) and İzmir Adnan Menderes Airport in 
Turkey closed temporarily, cancelling or diverting flights due to nearby wildfires (Mayling, 2025).
Wildfires can also trigger high-impact events in hazardous industrial facilities; accidents in these infrastructures can result in 
chemical releases, explosions, and prolonged secondary effects. In South Korea a grass fire ignited caused a gasoline storage tank 
explosion that burned for 17 h in 2018, resulting in an estimated economic loss of 3.33 million USD (Park et al., 2023). During 
the 2022 Estepona wildfire in Spain, several warehouses in the local industrial area were severely damaged (Planas et al., 2023). 
The same year, during the Hanul wildfire in South Korea, the Hanul Nuclear Power Plant experienced an automatic activation of 
its emergency generator due to the loss of offsite power caused by the impact of the wildfire (Lim et al., 2024). More recently, 
the 2024 wildfires in Viña del Mar, Chile affected an industrial area and caused explosions in a paint factory (EFE News, 2024). 
These examples highlight the range of potential severe consequences of wildfire-triggered accidents in highly hazardous industrial 
infrastructure.
1.2. Wildfire hazard, exposure, and vulnerability
Risk can be described as a function of three interrelated components: hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. These risk components 
are illustrated in a propeller diagram adapted from IPCC (2023) in Fig. 1, with wildfire-specific illustrations. Hazard refers to 
the potential occurrence and intensity of the potential harm. Exposure concerns the location of assets that may be affected by 
the identified hazard. Vulnerability describes the susceptibility of exposed elements to damage, influenced by material properties, 
design characteristics, and operational fragilities (Cardona et al., 2012; United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2017). 
Comprehensive risk analysis considers these three aspects together.

terminology, it is important to note that the term exposure is applied differently depending on the field and scale of analysis. In

Wildfire hazard is particularly complex to quantify and predict. Wildfires are highly transient in space and time and consist 
of multiple different hazard mechanisms, summarised in Table 1. Propagation occurs through radiative heat transfer, direct flame 
contact, and firebrand transport (Caton et al., 2017), while smoke, extreme heat, and high winds impose additional operational 
and physical stress to infrastructure. These mechanisms are interdependent and collectively determine the intensity, location, and 
evolution of wildfires, which complicate the accurate prediction and quantitative characterisation of wildfire risk.
Wildfire development involves three main phases: ignition, initial growth, and spread, which are each governed by distinct 
dynamics (Wang et al., 2024). Once fully developed wildfire spread occurs, there are a variety of possible behaviours (e.g: wind 
driven, plume dominated) that may occur, influencing the intensity and characteristics of different wildfire hazard mechanisms. 
Environmental, physical, and weather parameters influence these processes, making prediction inherently challenging.
Wildfires have the most direct impacts on human populations in interface areas, where the built environment is exposed 
to potential wildfires. Johnston and Flannigan (2018) expanded the widely studied Wildland–Urban Interface (WUI) concept 
(areas where wildlands meet residential development) to include the Wildland–Industrial Interface and the Wildland–Infrastructure 
Interface (defined as areas where wildlands intersect with oil and gas and mining industry and railways, roads, and power line 
infrastructure networks, respectively). Johnston and Flannigan (2018) found the Wildland–Infrastructure Interface to be more than

Infrastructure vulnerability refers to the conditions and characteristics that influence the system’s susceptibility to damage or 
impact due to hazard (Cardona et al., 2012; United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2017). In this paper we classify 
vulnerability as either physical and operational. Physical vulnerability refers to the intrinsic properties of assets; for example, 
material composition, structural design, and maintenance condition, which determine how infrastructure responds to wildfire hazard 
mechanisms. In contrast, operational concerns the functional consequences that arise once infrastructure performance is impaired; 
for example, criticality of roads, emergency response accessibility, and preparedness of employees to disaster, which determines 
how the operational organisation of the infrastructure system responds to exposure to wildfire hazard. These characteristics shape 
the likelihood and severity of direct damage, in line with Cardona et al. (2012) vulnerability definition as the predisposition of an 
asset to be adversely affected.
1.3. Scope and objectives of the review
This paper presents a systematic literature review of the growing body of research analysing various aspects of wildfire risk to four 
classes of critical infrastructure (CI): power, water, transport, and highly hazardous industrial systems, covering assets located within 
both the Wildland–Industrial Interface and the broader Wildland–Infrastructure Interface. The objective of the review is to identify 
current research gaps and potential synergies across infrastructure types. Section 2 describes the methodology of the systematic 
literature review and provides an overview of the selected studies. Sections 3, 4, and 5 then examine the main components of wildfire 
risk assessment identified in the literature: Section 3 reviews approaches used to characterise wildfire hazard, Section 4 analyses 
methods used to assess infrastructure exposure, and Section 5 discusses assessments of physical and operational vulnerability, 
including infrastructure interdependencies and cascading effects. Section 6 summarises the limited approaches identified in the 
literature that address the full wildfire risk concept across critical infrastructure and include methods for its estimation. Finally, 
Section 7 synthesises the main findings of the review and discusses implications for future research and wildfire risk assessment of 
critical infrastructure.
2. Methodology
2.1. Systematic methodology
We followed PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (Moher et al., 2015)) to 
conduct a systematic review of the literature using the Scopus and Web of Science Core Collections (WoS) online databases Fig. 2. 
Both searches were conducted on the 9th December 2024 through the University of Birmingham’s library subscription. The search 
criteria were: Scopus: TITLE-ABS-KEY((‘‘Wildfire’’ OR ‘‘Bushfire’’ OR ‘‘Forest fire’’) AND (‘‘Critical Infrastructure’’ OR ‘‘Infrastructure’’ 
OR ‘‘Industrial’’ OR ‘‘Power’’ OR ‘‘Transport’’ OR ‘‘Utility’’)) WoS: TOPIC(‘‘Wildfire’’ OR ‘‘Bushfire’’ OR ‘‘Forest fire’’) AND (‘‘Critical 
infrastructure’’ OR ‘‘Infrastructure’’ OR ‘‘Industrial’’ OR ‘‘Power’’ OR ‘‘Transport’’ OR ‘‘Utility’’), where TOPIC in WoS searches the 
title, abstract, key words plus and author key words.
The search strategy prioritised general infrastructure-related keywords combined with wildfire-related terms in order to 
maintain a consistent search approach across infrastructure sectors. As a result, sector-specific terminology referring to individual 
infrastructure assets (e.g., pipelines, railways, or wastewater facilities) was not systematically included in the search query. While this 
approach ensured comparability across sectors, it may have excluded some studies that focus on specific infrastructure components 
without explicitly referring to broader critical infrastructure terminology. This limitation should therefore be considered when 
interpreting the scope of the reviewed literature.
The search returned 7072 and 3783 studies in Scopus and WoS, respectively. We did not apply a filter to publication date. We 
opted to exclude studies that were not written in English or Spanish due to the constraints of the authors, resulting in 6859 and 
3759 studies, respectively. After resolving duplicates (3138 studies), we were left with 7480 studies. We opted to only include 
peer-reviewed articles with a DOI and excluded conference article publication types, which often have less rigorous or no peer 
review. We used Rayyan software to screen the titles and abstracts of the remaining 5991 studies (Ouzzani et al., 2016) using 
predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria (Fig. 3). We only included water infrastructure studies that explicitly considered physical 
infrastructure or utilities as comprehensive reviews and special issues focusing on watershed transport of pollutants due to wildfire 
already exist (Rhoades et al., 2019; Moazeni and Cerdà, 2024). The majority of studies were excluded after this stage. We added 
a further ten studies retrospectively that we deemed to have high relevance but were not included in the initial web searches, 
including one recently published power infrastructure systems review, one water study, two cross-sectional studies, and six studies 
related to high hazardous industries. This culminated in 237 studies selected for full text assessment.

2.2.1. Infrastructure covered
Many of our critical infrastructure networks are reliant on electricity. Power system infrastructure can be split into three 
main categories: generation facilities (including large centralised plants or dispersed throughout the network), transmission, and 
distribution networks. Transmission and distribution networks include the lines (overhead and underground) as well as substations. 
Heat from fires can damage or degrade overhead lines and poles, and smoke can contaminate insulators, leading to increased faults 
while limiting solar generation production (Vahedi et al., 2025). Power system infrastructure can also ignite fires, and such wildfires 
in Australia and California have led to increased attention to fire prevention and risk minimisation whether through advanced 
detection and protection studies or through pre-emptive power shut-offs during periods of high risk (Vahedi et al., 2025). As a result, 
there have been a number of recent extensive literature reviews covering power system infrastructure and wildfires (Zainuddin et al., 
2020; Arab et al., 2021; Sadat et al., 2022; Vazquez et al., 2022; Fernando et al., 2023; Bandara et al., 2023; Chatti and Randle, 
2023; Jiaqing et al., 2024; Shah et al., 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025).
Drinking water infrastructure can be broadly divided into raw water extraction, water treatment, and water distribution systems. 
The raw water extraction system transports water from natural resources to water treatment plants (WTPs) through pipelines. After 
treatment, the potable water distribution system delivers safe drinking water to end users via a network of mains (the principal 
underground pipes) and service laterals (smaller pipes connecting individual buildings to the mains, typically through a water 
metre). In contrast, the wastewater or sewage system, which collects and conveys used or contaminated water to treatment facilities, 
is functionally distinct from the potable water distribution system. Although the sewage network could also be vulnerable to wildfire 
impacts, it has received little to no attention in the wildfire literature to date. This review focuses on wildfire impacts to the physical 
components of the potable water system; including pipelines, water treatment facilities, and water distribution infrastructure.
Transport infrastructure represents another critical network potentially affected by wildfires. It can encompass roads (including 
local roads, highways, and forest roads), railway systems (tracks, trains, train stations), and air transport facilities (airports, runways, 
and associated buildings). Some reviewed studies concentrate on the vulnerability of forest roads, particularly regarding access for 
firefighting and emergency response. Others consider main roads and highways, often in the context of evacuation planning and 
traffic flow under wildfire conditions. Railways have been examined both as potential wildfire ignition sources and as assets at risk. 
A smaller number of studies have also explored urban rail or metro systems, particularly their use in large-scale evacuations during 
wildfire events.

Wildfire hazard analysis in the selected papers applies a variety of methods to characterise potential or past wildfire heat and

3.1. Wildfire occurrence
There are a multitude of factors that can lead to the ignition of a wildfire (Ganteaume et al., 2013; Keeley and Syphard, 2018). 
However, unlike many other hazards, power and transportation infrastructure can also ignite wildfires. Power system infrastructure 
can ignite fires in many different ways, including 1) tree and vegetation-driven faults; 2) contact between overhead lines; 3) asset 
failure (e.g., poles, transformers, switches, or other electrical components) (Vahedi et al., 2025). Evidence from fires in California 
between 1960 and 2009 found power line-ignited fires to be on average around ten times larger (Mitchell, 2013). As a result of power 
line induced fires (such as those already mentioned across Australia and the United States), further research efforts have focused on 
improving wildfire occurrence prediction and fire-inducing fault capabilities in the power system infrastructure sector (Arab et al., 
2021).
In certain locations and conditions, anthropogenic fires are more likely to start near transportation corridors (Robinne et al., 
2016); several articles applied statistical analysis to investigate relationships between infrastructure characteristics or location and 
historical wildfire occurrence data. Kuklina et al. (2023) considered the interactions between wildfire ignition, location, perimeters, 
and fire radiative power during the 2016 wildfires in Irkutsk, Russia, with forest roads using data from remote sensing sources 
(MODIS and VIIRS) and local observer interviews. MODIS-detected wildfires were also used by Podolskaia et al. (2020), who 
developed a GIS-based method for mapping and analysing ground access routes to forest fires to support firefighting. In another 
analysis of the relationship between forest road locations and wildfire occurrence, Mostafa et al. (2024) considered historical 
wildfires and burned area from the local civil protection department in Apuglia, Italy. Arienti et al. (2009) analysed regional 
government data on wildfire cause, date, and location, and found statistical correlations between wildfire occurrence and forest 
road density in Western Canada.
To analyse rail-sparked wildfires, Nezval et al. (2022) used local rail administration data on wildfire occurrence location. 
Similarly, Belikova et al. (2022) used wildfire location data and cluster analysis to analyse their relation to forest rail locations in the 
Zaigraevsky district of the Republic of Buryatia. Garmabaki et al. (2024) presented a study on the impact of extreme temperatures 
on the Swedish railway system, considering vegetation fires as a failure mode of the rail system caused by extreme temperatures; 
data on climate conditions and railway failure modes were compiled from a number of railway and meteorological databases. 
With the exception of González et al. (2024), all the identified papers using wildfire occurrence data are in either the power or 
transport sectors. Wildfire occurrence indicators are useful in characterising hazard for network infrastructure spread over wide 
areas, although in the transport sector analyses, most lack wildfire intensity and hazard probability information, limiting their 
usefulness in determining overall risk. This is an area where research and operational advances in the power sector to improve 
situational awareness and forecasting capabilities (Vahedi et al., 2025) could be extended into other sectors.
3.2. Wildfire behaviour
Wildfire behaviour analyses focus on modelling how fires ignite, spread, and transfer heat to surrounding infrastructure, 
providing a basis for quantifying intensity, rate of spread, and thermal effects. The reviewed studies are classified into two main 
groups: wildfire spread models and thermal impact analyses.
3.2.1. Wildfire spread models
The use of wildfire spread models in a power system infrastructure context are well established. An overview of wildfire spread 
modelling applied to the power sector is provided in Vahedi et al. (2025), which details 16 distinct fire models used in a power 
system wildfire risk analysis context.
For nuclear infrastructure, Okano and Yamano (2015) used FARSITE wildfire simulations to generate fireline intensity, rate of 
spread (ROS), and arrival time around nuclear sites under conservative weather scenarios. Okano and Yamano (2016b) expanded this

using a four-step logic-tree and Monte Carlo framework with FARSITE simualtions-based response surfaces to derive hazard curves 
of wildfire fireline and reaction intensity. Lim et al. (2024) combined Monte Carlo FARSITE simulations with fragility analysis to 
characterise hazard curves for loss-of-off-site-power (LOOP) events for nuclear sites. Kim et al. (2024) employed repeated Monte 
Carlo FARSITE simulations to calculate probabilistic burn-area overlay maps near nuclear power plants.
Across the three studies by Khakzad (Khakzad, 2018; Khakzad et al., 2018; Khakzad, 2019) proposing wildfire risk analysis for 
oil and gas facilities, wildfire characterisation followed a three-stage framework considering ignition probability, burn probability 
(BP), and fire intensity. Each study quantified the likelihood of ignition using environmental parameters (temperature, humidity, 
wind speed, and vegetation moisture) often informed by empirical and regression-based models. The BP, which is the conditional 
probability the ignited wildfire will spread to a given location, is modelled through spatial or stochastic wildfire spread simulations. 
Finally, fireline intensity is used as the principal indicator of hazard severity, sometimes used to estimate the radiant heat flux 
using the solid flame model. The three papers differ in focus and analytical depth. Khakzad et al. (2018) integrated wildfire 
behaviour models with a dynamic Bayesian network (DBN) to evaluate wildfire-triggered domino effects in oil facilities, emphasising 
how external heat fluxes could escalate within industrial systems. Khakzad (2018) adapted the same hazard characterisation to a 
regional context, coupling it with Canada’s Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) and Forest Fire Behaviour Prediction (FBP) systems 
to obtain real-time estimates of ignition likelihood, ROS, and head fire intensity. This data-driven framework relied on operational 
fire indices rather than process simulation or probabilistic network modelling, offering a practical national-scale application. In 
contrast, Khakzad (2019) advanced the framework by explicitly modelling wildfire propagation. The landscape was represented 
as a two-dimensional grid, and a spatio-temporal DBN was employed to identify the most probable path of fire spread based on 
ROS and intensity predictions from the FBP system. Compared with the earlier studies, this model mechanistically coupled physical 
fire behaviour with probabilistic reasoning, transforming wildfire hazard characterisation from a static risk quantification exercise 
into a dynamic, spatially explicit predictive tool. Collectively, the three papers depict a progressive methodological evolution—from 
empirical assessment, to probabilistic risk integration, to full dynamic modelling of wildfire behaviour and industrial impact.
Sfetsos et al. (2021) is the only identified study, of the papers we reviewed in detail, that coupled wildfire spread and smoke 
transport simulations. In the study, considering both electrical power system infrastructure networks and highways in Southern 
France, the wildfire spread model FIRETACTIC was applied to estimate wildfire perimeters, ROS, and fireline intensity values. 
FIRETACTIC is a wildfire spread estimation tool designed for emergency responders. As previously mentioned, the large power 
system infrastructure literature and their investment in detailed wildfire spread simulations may include additional studies that 
consider coupled wildfire–smoke modelling, although these were not identified within the subset of papers reviewed in detail.
For the power sector, substantial advances have been made in the use of wildfire simulation models to integrate these models with 
prediction and risk assessments. Across the other sectors, most wildfire spread modelling studies target energy (nuclear and oil and 
gas) infrastructure, with several probabilistic applications. From the analysed transport, water, and multiple infrastructure papers; 
only one study applied wildfire behaviour analysis (FIRETACTIC in Sfetsos et al. 2021). This is a clear gap where other sectors could 
benefit from the modelling integration work demonstrated by power system infrastructure operators as part of wildfire mitigation 
plans (Vazquez et al., 2022).
3.2.2. Heat transfer analysis
At smaller, single component, spatial scales, hazard characterisation can involve heat transfer analysis, which quantifies the 
heat fluxes, temperatures, and exposure durations imposed by a wildfire on specific infrastructure units. This small-scale thermal 
characterisation not only defines the local wildfire hazard, but also provides the basis for deriving component damage thresholds 
in physical vulnerability assessments. The thermal impacts to infrastructure depend on attributes of the fire itself as well as the 
materials of each infrastructure asset. While some studies exist, fire and asset heterogeneity make generalised statements of impact 
challenging. The number, size, temperature of firebrands deposited, as well as smoke particulate matter deposition amounts, would 
be included in this hazard characterisation scale; however, such specific firebrand hazard analyses are not yet present in literature.
Baranovskiy et al. (2021) computed the impact of wildfire flames on a wooden railway building, considering six possible exterior 
walls materials. A 2D heat transfer model was developed, estimating radiative heat flux as a function of flame length and distance 
from the fire front by solving the partial differential equations governing radiative and conductive heat transfer through the wall. To 
characterise the flaming heat source, three wildfire scenarios were considered: a low-intensity surface fire, a high-intensity surface 
fire, and a crown fire, characterised by flame lengths of 1 m, 1.5 m, and full tree height.
Richter et al. (2022) presented a 1D conductive heat transfer model of wildfire heat through the soil targeting a buried water 
supply pipe, testing varying influencing wildfire hazard parameters (wildfire surface temperature, surface heat flux, and burning 
duration), to estimate at what burial depth the threshold temperature is reached for each pipe material. The wildfire surface heat flux 
tested ranged between 15 and 30 kW/m2, the surface temperature between 100 ◦C and 900 ◦C, and the heating duration between 
15 min and 4 h. Metz et al. (2023) conducted both laboratory tests and numerical modelling to test wildfire hazard scenarios with 
varying heat fluxes, burn durations, and burial depths and their impact on different water supply pipe materials.
Laboratory experiments conducted by Vaz et al. (2022) tested the protection offered by rigid panels around a telecommunication 
cabinet. The fire was induced in a 16 m2 fuel bed using 1.5 kg/m2 of dry Mediterranean shrub mixtures with a moisture content 
between 12.6% and 15.1%. Three wind speeds ranging between 0 and 4.5 m/s at 10 m height were introduced. The maximum heat 
flux measurements on the outer surface of the protection panel facing the approaching flames were 9, 5, and 1 kW/m2 for the three 
wind speeds, respectively.
Heymes et al. (2013) estimated radiative heat transfer from crown fires using the solid-flame model and validated predicted 
fluxes through large-scale experiments. Scarponi et al. (2018) conducted full-scale LPG-tank tests and used CFD simulations (Ansys

scenarios. All of these approaches included estimating the heat transfer from wildfire flames under different conditions.
Heat-transfer analyses and fire impact experiments appear in only a few papers overall and are most common for storage 
tanks, and service pipelines in the chemical and water industry, respectively. These methods provide the most physically explicit 
characterisation of thermal hazard (radiative and conductive fluxes, temperature thresholds, and heating duration), which can be 
used in other sectors involving critical components.
3.2.3. Wildfire indicators
Several papers apply indicators or predefined categories to represent potential wildfire hazard levels. These indicators translate 
physical or weather variables into simplified hazard metrics, often used for large-scale or multi-sector assessments.
Both Arango et al. (2023b,a) conceptualised wildfire hazard through seven ROS categories, ranging from 5–15 m/min (surface 
fire) to greater than 300 m/min (extreme wildfires). In Arango et al. (2023a), these categories underpin the Equivalent Fire Distance 
(EFD) and Fire Arrival Time (FIRAT) metrics, defining how quickly a fire could reach a road link based on surrounding fuels and 
barriers. Hazard is therefore expressed as ROS-based fire severity classes. In Arango et al. (2023b), the same ROS-based framework 
was used to define escalating hazard scenarios, each corresponding to specific performance expectations for safety, reliability, and 
connectivity. Both papers represented hazard as categorical fire-intensity regimes derived from ROS values, Arango et al. (2023a) 
used them to spatially compute the duration of hazard exposure, while Arango et al. (2023b) linked them to performance degradation 
and resilience evaluation.
Fraser et al. (2020) identified the relevant debris stream flows and characterised each associated watershed by a wildfire hazard 
metric and post-fire flooding vulnerability indicators (percentage of basin slope greater than 30%, basin ruggedness, storm rainfall 
intensity, clay content, and liquid limit). The wildfire hazard was defined based on landscape conditions using the previously 
developed Wildfire Hazard Potential (WHP) metric. All factors were combined to model debris-flow probability. To characterise post-
fire debris-flow hazard, Sfetsos et al. (2021) calculate the annual average number of days with a Fire Weather Index (FWI) above 50 
as a wildfire hazard indicator. Li and Chester (2023) quantified post-wildfire debris-flow hazard in California by estimating the 15-
minute rainfall threshold required to trigger debris flows using the empirical model of Staley et al. (2017), incorporating watershed 
slope, simulated burn severity, soil erodibility, and projected burned-area increase. Burn severity was predicted from an empirical 
dNBR model (Staley et al., 2018) based on vegetation type and fire threat level. The rainfall threshold was compared with local 10-, 
50-, and 100-year design storm intensities to classify debris-flow threat levels under four climate scenarios. The statewide hazard 
results were validated with USGS post-fire data and combined with roadway network criticality to identify vulnerable corridors.
Robichaud and Padowski (2024), in a water utility vulnerability analysis, created a simplified wildfire risk indicator by adding the 
local topographical slope, change in mean summer precipitation, change in mean summer temperature, and land-cover classification. 
This indicator was expanded in a more detailed form incorporating aspect, road density, past fire regimes, land use, wind velocity, 
and relative humidity. Lee et al. (2022) also analysed the vulnerability of water utilities in California; hazard was considered by 
creating a wildfire severity indicator by dividing the total number of acres burned by wildfires by the number of wildfires for each 
water utility and time period considered. Schulze and Fischer (2021) assessed potential wildfire-caused water contamination risks 
in WUI areas by calculating a burn-severity indicator based on wildfire damage in the City of Santa Rosa and the Town of Paradise, 
California. Burn severity was defined as the density of damaged buildings.
Khakzad (2019) used a DBN to express how varying wildfire intensity modified conditional probabilities of system failure. John-
ston and Flannigan (2018) definec wildfire hazard mainly through fuel type and fuel connectivity, using a fuel-weight system 
based on vegetation and aggregation of burnable land, in his mapping of Canadian Wildland–Industrial Interface and Wildland–
Infrastructure Interface. Planas et al. (2023) developed a global map of the Wildland–Industrial Interface areas, also characterising 
potential wildfire hazard by different vegetation classes based on their associated potential hazard if burned. Vegetation type was 
used to indicate potential wildfire hazard.
Indicator-based hazard characterisations were the most widely applied approach across transport and water infrastructure studies. 
They enable integration of topography, climate, and vegetation data into composite hazard metrics suitable for regional or multi-
hazard studies and are particularly used for network type infrastructure. These methods typically prioritise spatial coverage and 
interpretability over detailed hazard characterisation.
3.3. Smoke
Smoke hazard analyses focus on quantifying wildfire-generated particulate matter to estimate its impact on infrastructure 
functionality. Determination of this impact requires a spatio-temporal understanding of the smoke production and evolution during 
and after the fire. Obtaining precise historical information around the amount of smoke present in a particular area to estimate 
exposure remains a challenge. Prior research has indicated a number of proxy measurements that are used across infrastructure 
domains to reflect the amount of smoke. Daily wildfire-caused PM2.5 concentrations were used by Han et al. (2024) to estimate 
wildfire smoke impact on local Metrorail usage. Sfetsos et al. (2021) also used PM2.5 concentrations as smoke hazard quantification 
by coupling wildfire spread predictions with the Fire Emission Production Simulator (FEPS) and the HYSPLIT model to calculate 
smoke plume dispersion. FEPS and HYSPLIT were combined to estimate hourly PM2.5 concentrations in the area of interest.

3.4. Infrastructure as a source of wildfire risk
While this review primarily focuses on wildfire impacts on critical infrastructure, it is important to recognise that infrastructure 
systems can also act as ignition sources for wildfires. This dual role distinguishes wildfire risk from many other natural hazards, 
where infrastructure is primarily considered as an exposed asset rather than a potential contributor to hazard occurrence. In 
particular, electrical infrastructure has been widely associated with wildfire ignitions. Faults in overhead power lines, conductor 
contact with vegetation, and equipment failures have been identified as important ignition mechanisms in several major wildfire 
events (Mitchell, 2009; Vahedi et al., 2025). For example, investigations of the 2009 Victorian ‘‘Black Saturday’’ fires found that 
multiple large fires were initiated by failures in power transmission infrastructure (Victoria State Government, 2011). Similar 
concerns have been raised in recent events such as the 2023 Maui wildfire and the 2024 Texas wildfires, where fallen or damaged 
power lines were identified as likely ignition sources (Arbaje, 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025). These examples illustrate how infrastructure 
systems can contribute directly to wildfire hazard generation.
Other infrastructure systems may also contribute to wildfire ignition under certain conditions. Transport infrastructure has been 
associated with anthropogenic fire starts, particularly along railway lines and road corridors where sparks, mechanical failures, or 
human activity may initiate fires (Robinne et al., 2016; Nezval et al., 2022; Belikova et al., 2022). Industrial facilities located in 
wildfire-prone areas may also contribute to fire ignition or escalation through the presence of flammable materials or accidental 
releases during wildfire events (Planas et al., 2023). Recognising this dual relationship between infrastructure and wildfire hazard is 
important for risk assessment frameworks, as infrastructure can both influence wildfire occurrence and experience wildfire impacts. 
Consequently, wildfire risk assessments should consider not only the vulnerability of infrastructure to wildfire exposure, but also 
the potential role of infrastructure systems in wildfire ignition and propagation.

4. Exposure
Building on the wildfire hazard characterisation methodologies, the next stage is to identify the specific infrastructure that may 
be exposed. Determining the extent of infrastructure exposure includes identifying the hazard-prone infrastructure locations (The 
United Nations, 2015). Table 8 summarises the main methodological approaches identified in the reviewed literature for assessing 
infrastructure exposure to wildfire hazards, together with representative references for each approach.
Analyses of exposure are often limited to a single region, such as that associated with a given electric utility (Vazquez et al., 
2022) or individual countries (Jiaqing et al., 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025). Arango et al. (2023b,a) quantified exposure through a 
proposed FIRAT metric, which translated the weighted distance to surrounding fuel sources and fire barriers into a time value 
indicating how long a wildfire of a given intensity would take to reach each road segment. Arango et al. (2023a) analysed exposure 
at the asset level using GIS to automate EFD/FIRAT calculations for all road links, with inputs including distances to vegetation, 
industrial facilities, fuel stations, water bodies, and firefighting resources, weighted by their rates of spread. In Arango et al. (2023b), 
FIRAT similarly defined link-level exposure but fed directly into the resilience model, determining when a road becomes unsafe and 
unavailable as fires approach. Nezval et al. (2022) assessed wildfire exposure along the Czech railway network using the KDE+ 
software, which applies Kernel Density Estimation to identify statistically significant vegetation fire hotspots along railways. Each 
hotspot was characterised by the number of fire incidents, cluster strength, density, and collective risk. Logistic regression was used 
to explore explanatory factors influencing wildfire occurrence, including traffic intensity, electrification, line geometry, proximity 
to forests, roads, and built-up areas, population density, and elevation. Belikova et al. (2022) evaluated exposure of forest rail 
infrastructure by clustering historical wildfire locations and measuring spatial proximity to rail alignments in the Zaigraevsky district 
(Republic of Buryatia).
In Mostafa et al. (2024), wildfire exposure was assessed as the spatial association between forest–road network characteristics 
and wildfire occurrence in Apulia, Italy (2000–2021). The study overlaid a 1 km2 grid and computec three road metrics—density, 
number of links, and access percentage—within two buffer distances. Using Ordinary Least Squares and Geographically Weighted 
Regression, road metrics were modelled as response variables against wildfire variables to identify where accessibility co-varies 
with fire occurrence. Han et al. (2024) assessed wildfire exposure of urban Metrorail stations in the Washington metropolitan area 
by comparing daily PM2.5 levels (10 km resolution) with station locations, where non-zero values indicated exposure to wildfire 
smoke. Modaresi Rad et al. (2023) assessed exposure of roads and power lines by overlaying national infrastructure maps with annual 
wildfire perimeters (MTBS,2000–2019). For each year, the total lengths of roads and transmission lines intersecting fire perimeters 
were calculated to quantify direct exposure, showing increases of 58% and 70% for roads and power lines, respectively. Freiria 
et al. (2015) analysed exposure by mapping how road disruptions affected access between parish centroids and district hospitals, 
calculating changes in average travel time and the number of parishes each hospital could serve under disrupted conditions. Fraser 
et al. (2020) combined post-fire debris flow risk, hydrological assessment, and roadway network analysis to identify stream flows 
intersecting transportation infrastructure, integrating physical and network dependencies beyond simple co-location. Kuklina et al. 
(2023) combined interviews with remote sensing data to describe wildfire–road interactions in Russia, using MODIS, VIIRS, Landsat, 
and Sentinel-2 to map burned areas and road proximity. Roads were classified by function and access regime, and their relationship 
with wildfires was analysed by the proportion of roads within fire boundaries and distance from ignitions. Li and Chester (2023) 
quantified roadway exposure to post-wildfire debris flows by computing watershed-level debris-flow threats under current and future 
climates, assigning each threat to road–stream crossings. Exposure was taken as the threat rating of intersected watersheds, with 
non-intersecting segments assumed unexposed.
To examine the exposure of both roads and transmission power lines in the USA, Modaresi Rad et al. (2023) used the 2000–2019 
wildfire perimeters, filtering wildfires with burned area greater or equal to 400 ha in the Western USA and 200 ha in the Eastern 
USA, from the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity program (Eidenshink et al., 2007). González et al. (2024) conducted a historical 
trend analysis of over 100,000 oil and gas wells located in wildfire-prone areas of the western United States between 1984 and 
2019. The study identified temporal evolution in wildfire occurrence and its overlap with critical energy infrastructure, including 
demographic exposure of nearby populations. Johnston and Flannigan (2018) defined wildland fuels located close to human-built 
structures, including roads, railways, and oil and gas industries, and mapped the associated interface areas in Canada within a 
variable-width buffer of hazardous fuels.
At regional and national scales, Planas et al. (2023) and González et al. (2024) presented exposure methodologies for highly

5.1. Physical
Physical vulnerabilities describe the direct damage, degradation, or loss of functionality of assets exposed to wildfire heat, smoke, 
firebrands or secondary physical effects.
Even in the power sector, this is a relatively under-explored area with limited research articles relating the failure thresholds 
of various types of equipment. Fragility curves were developed by Nazemi et al. (2024) to characterise the failure likelihood of 
overhead power line conductors. However, such parametrisations are dependent on numerous interdependent and often stochastic 
assumptions. Sfetsos et al. (2021) defined threshold values for electricity and road infrastructure under wildfire conditions. For power 
system infrastructure, structural failure of steel components was expected between 500–600 ◦C, while zinc corrosion risk began near 
420 ◦C. Smoke-induced flashovers occurred when PM2.5 concentrations exceeded 350 μg/m3, establishing clear physical vulnerability 
thresholds for power system infrastructure under heat and smoke exposure. Lim et al. (2024) developed a vulnerability assessment 
for wildfire-induced loss of offsite power (LOOP) at nuclear plants by combining Monte Carlo wildfire simulations (FARSITE) with 
fragility analysis of transmission infrastructure. Computational fluid dynamics modelling determined yield and critical temperatures 
for power line materials, which served as fragility thresholds. Integrating these curves with wildfire thermal-load frequencies 
quantified the probability of power failure under specific exposure conditions. However, although there is widespread understanding 
of the range of vulnerabilities, work to define measures of power system infrastructure vulnerability with respect to smoke and heat 
remains limited (Nazemi et al., 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025).
Wildfires can thermally degrade materials and contaminate water systems. Belongia et al. (2023) found that only 2.3% of 
studies on wildfire–water interactions explicitly addressed built infrastructure, highlighting contamination from heat-damaged 
plastic plumbing and backflow of polluted air and water. Proctor et al. (2020) and Whelton et al. (2023) demonstrated that 
thermally degraded pipes release volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and that depressurisation and backflow from building damage 
enable intrusion of contaminated vapours. Laboratory studies by Draper et al. (2022) identified organic compounds released during 
polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and polyethylene pyrolysis, determining threshold temperatures for contaminant release from PVC (194 ◦C) 
and HDPE (254 ◦C). Metz et al. (2023) examined the thermal degradation and contaminant release potential of commonly used 
service water supply pipe materials by combining laboratory tests and numerical modelling to determine the threshold temperatures 
at which copper, galvanised steel, PVC, and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) begin to release volatile organic compounds (VOCs). 
Experimental tests exposed different configurations of pipe samples to elevated temperatures. Richter et al. (2022) modelled how 
surface fires can elevate buried pipe temperatures above safe limits depending on burial depth and exposure duration. Results

At the network scale, Schmidt et al. (2022, 2023) showed that exceedance of VOC maximum contaminant levels correlated

integrity.
Garmabaki et al. (2024) embedded vulnerability assessment within a risk-modelling framework evaluating extreme temperatures 
on railway assets in northern Sweden. Using historical failure and maintenance records, meteorological data (SMHI, ERA5), and 
expert elicitation, the study quantified vulnerability through a multi-attribute risk model combining likelihood and consequence 
rankings for hazards such as track buckling, breakage, and wildfire exposure. The resulting vulnerability-adjusted risk index 
differentiated hazards by exposure intensity and asset sensitivity. Baranovskiy et al. (2021) modelled ignition of wooden structures, 
specifically to analyse the impact of wooden structures as part of the rail infrastructure, finding that wall surfaces reached ignition 
temperature even under low-intensity fires and within one minute under crown fires. Surface temperatures exceeded 800 ◦C within 
425 s, underscoring the need for improved fire design standards and protective treatments for combustible components.
Heymes et al. (2013) defined vulnerability as the thermal resistance of LPG tanks under wildfire-like radiative heating, 
comparing solid-flame model predictions and full-scale tests against steel critical temperature thresholds. Although exposure 
was severe, measured fluxes indicated tanks remained below failure temperatures, suggesting low vulnerability under the tested 
scenario. Scarponi et al. (2018) advanced this work with a coupled experimental–numerical framework for LPG tank vulnerability, 
combining large-scale experiments with transient CFD simulations (Ansys) to reproduce observed pressure–temperature evolution. 
Vulnerability was expressed as susceptibility to pressure build-up and thermal weakening leading to containment loss. Khakzad 
(2018, 2019) introduced a probabilistic approach to assess vulnerability of atmospheric and pressurised storage vessels exposed 
to wildfires. Using the solid-flame model and Probit correlations (Landucci et al., 2009), they quantified tank-failure probabilities 
as a function of wildfire intensity and distance, embedding these into a DBN to capture cascading and conditional failures across 
interconnected units. Vulnerability was defined as the conditional likelihood of structural or thermal failure given a defined exposure 
scenario. Ricci et al. (2021) characterised wildfire vulnerability of industrial tanks through time-to-failure models derived from 
incident radiation levels and Probit-based correlations, defining how quickly tanks reach critical temperatures under sustained 
exposure. The models informed safety distances, ensuring failure time exceeds expected emergency response time. Ricci et al. 
(2024) extended this to a dynamic event-tree framework simulating sequential tank ignition, heating, and failure within a storage 
installation, conceptualising vulnerability as a time-dependent property governing cascading failures.
A series of studies by Okano and Yamano (2015, 2016a,b) evaluated wildfire vulnerability of nuclear power plants (NPPs). Their 
framework linked fireline intensity and rate of spread (from FARSITE) to potential degradation of safety systems. Wildfire smoke 
transport simulated via ALOFT-FTTM was used to estimate particulate accumulation on decay heat removal filters, representing 
progressive functional degradation. Later work combined logic-tree analysis, FARSITE-based response surfaces, and Monte Carlo 
simulations to generate hazard and exceedance curves describing how often component thresholds are surpassed. Together, these 
studies defined vulnerability as the progressive or probabilistic loss of component performance under wildfire thermal and particulate 
stress. Lim et al. (2024) performed a CFD-based vulnerability analysis to determine when a wildfire’s heat flux would cause a 
transmission line (responsible for power at the NPP) to lose mechanical integrity. Using ANSYS Fluent, the authors modelled 
convection and radiation from an idealised fire beneath the conductor. By incrementally increasing imposed heat fluxes, they 
identified the threshold at which the conductor reached its assumed yield temperature (200 ◦C), establishing the critical heat 
flux level required for failure. This threshold became the fragility limit used to quantify the probability of wildfire-induced 
transmission-line failure.
Efforts to reduce physical vulnerability centre on material protection and structural resilience. Vaz et al. (2022) tested 
magnesium-oxide–fibreglass panels as low-cost fire protection for telecommunications cabinets, maintaining internal temperatures 
below 30 ◦C under exposure (versus 60 ◦C unprotected). Yasoda et al. (2023) evaluated wire-arc-sprayed Zn–15Al coatings on steel, 
showing protective properties up to 500 ◦C due to beneficial phase transformations, though integrity was lost at higher temperatures. 
These studies demonstrate that fire-resistant materials and coatings can reduce structural vulnerability when adequately maintained 
and replaced after severe exposure.
Across infrastructure sectors, most studies of physical vulnerability focused on heat transfer processes and material or component 
response to wildfire exposure. These include analyses of thermal degradation thresholds for metals and coatings in power and 
communication systems (Sfetsos et al., 2021; Yasoda et al., 2023), radiative and convective heat effects on industrial storage tanks 
and process vessels (Heymes et al., 2013; Ricci et al., 2021; Khakzad, 2018), and temperature-induced weakening of construction 
materials and buried utilities (Baranovskiy et al., 2021; Richter et al., 2022).

Robichaud and Padowski (2024) analysed water-utility vulnerability using indicators such as ownership, management type,

among government-owned, surface-water-dependent, and locally sourced systems, especially in southern and coastal California.

isolating depressurised sections, and rapid sampling to reduce contamination risk. Becker et al. (2018) provided guidance for 
maintaining treatment performance during wildfire crises through adaptive chemical dosing, filtration management, and disinfection 
control. Gutierrez and Lagunas (2023) recommended improved coordination with fire agencies and aerial operations to safeguard 
assets and sustain supply.
Several studies linked vulnerability to network criticality, defined as the importance of a network component to overall 
system connectivity and performance. Arango et al. (2023a) equated functional vulnerability with criticality, using a stochastic 
traffic-assignment model to estimate increases in travel time from link failures, ranking roads by system-level importance. Li and 
Chester (2023) identified road segments exposed to post-fire debris hazards and used betweenness centrality to evaluate network 
importance. Fraser et al. (2020) combined debris-flow hazard assessment with hydrological and mobility analyses to identify critical 
transportation links. Freiria et al. (2015) used the Local Regional Scale Risk Model (LRSRM) with biclustering of network indicators 
(e.g., 𝛼 index, degree, Bonacich power, clustering coefficient) to assess connectivity between settlements and hospitals. Results 
showed vulnerability to be scale-dependent: roads redundant regionally could be critical locally and vice versa, with highly central 
and weakly redundant roads identified as most vulnerable.
Most assessments of operational vulnerability, apart from power, concentrated on transport networks, where wildfire-related 
disruptions were examined through metrics of criticality, connectivity, and accessibility (Arango et al., 2023a; Li and Chester, 
2023; Fraser et al., 2020; Freiria et al., 2015). These studies consistently highlighted the sensitivity of road systems with 
limited redundancy, while functional loss was modelled using centrality-based network metrics or capacity-reduction analyses. 
Visibility impairment from wildfire smoke, despite being an important operational constraint, was only explicitly quantified in 
two studies (Sfetsos et al., 2021; Ismanto et al., 2019). Water-utility analyses, by contrast, focused on comparative or index-based 
approaches (Robichaud and Padowski, 2024; Lee et al., 2022), linking operational vulnerability to ownership, source type, and 
preparedness. Overall, the literature emphasises network performance and criticality as key indicators of operational vulnerability, 
with limited attention to dynamic hazards such as smoke-induced service disruption.
5.3. Infrastructure interdependencies and cascading effects
Critical infrastructure systems are highly interdependent, and wildfire impacts affecting one system may propagate to others 
through functional dependencies and shared operational requirements. Real wildfire events illustrate these cascading dynamics. For 
example, during the 2022 Hanul wildfire in South Korea, damage to transmission infrastructure led to a loss-of-offsite-power event 
at the Hanul nuclear power plant, requiring the activation of emergency generators (Lim et al., 2024). Similar dependencies exist 
between energy and water infrastructure, where wildfire-related power outages can disrupt pumping and treatment operations that 
rely on electricity supply (Belongia et al., 2023). In the transport sector, wildfire-related road closures and smoke conditions may 
reduce accessibility and delay emergency response or infrastructure repair, further amplifying system disruptions (Mahajan and 
Kim, 2020; Florath et al., 2024). These examples demonstrate that wildfire risk cannot always be adequately evaluated by analysing 
infrastructure sectors independently.
Although interdependencies are increasingly recognised, modelling approaches that explicitly represent these interactions remain 
relatively limited in the wildfire literature. Some studies reviewed in this work implicitly capture interdependencies within 
infrastructure systems. For example, Khakzad (2018, 2019) applied dynamic Bayesian networks to model wildfire-induced domino 
effects within oil and gas facilities, representing probabilistic dependencies between process units and the potential escalation 
of accidents triggered by wildfire exposure. In the transport sector, network-based analyses examined how disruptions propagate 
through infrastructure systems; for example, Mahajan and Kim (2020) and Florath et al. (2024) analysed road network structure 
and accessibility to evaluate how wildfire-related disruptions affect connectivity and evacuation capacity. In the energy sector,

6. Risk
6.1. Risk assessment frameworks
Risk assessment frameworks generally aim to capture both how likely hazardous events are and what their consequences might 
be incorporating the hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. When it comes to wildfires, this is particularly difficult (Finney, 2005), as 
wildfire risk is shaped by complex interactions between environmental, climatic, and human factors, making it a classic ‘‘wicked’’ 
problem. As a result, when compared to more established approaches for hazards such as floods or earthquakes (e.g., Wong and 
Ho (2000), Escuder-Bueno et al. (2012), Arvidsson and Johansson (2024)), fully developed quantitative risk frameworks are still 
relatively scarce in the wildfire field.
That said, a number of studies do attempt to frame wildfire risk using the classical idea of combining likelihood and consequences. 
In these works, likelihood is usually described in terms of the probability of ignition and/or fire spread, while consequences are 
linked to how fire intensity interacts with exposed and vulnerable assets. Examples include Scott et al. (2013), Johnston et al. 
(2020), and work in the Wildland–Urban Interface such as Alcasena et al. (2017), where fire behaviour and potential impacts are 
brought together. However, even in these cases, consequences are often treated in a simplified or aggregated way. Alongside these 
more quantitative efforts, many studies rely on semi-quantitative approaches. Here, risk is approximated by combining indicators of 
hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, rather than modelling each component in detail. This is particularly common at larger spatial 
scales (e.g., Bergonse et al. (2022), Oom et al. (2022)), where detailed probabilistic modelling is simply not practical. In these 
cases, consequences tend to be represented indirectly, through proxy indicators such as asset density, preparedness, or vulnerability 
indices, rather than through explicit estimates of damage or loss.
Within the subset of studies on critical infrastructure reviewed in this work, only a limited number of studies bring likelihood and 
consequences together in a consistent way. A key exception is the work of Khakzad et al. (2018), Khakzad (2019), who proposed fully 
quantitative frameworks for oil and gas facilities exposed to wildfires. In their approach, risk was explicitly defined as the product 
of wildfire probability and consequence. Likelihood was broken down into ignition and burn probabilities, while consequences 
were derived from physically based models that relate fire intensity and thermal radiation to equipment damage. By combining 
fire behaviour models with probabilistic tools (e.g., probit models, Bayesian networks, and event trees), these frameworks are able 
to capture not only direct damage but also cascading effects, such as domino scenarios, and to estimate both component-level 
and system-level risks. Similarly, Ricci et al. (2024) provided a framework for the quantitative assessment of the vulnerability of 
industrial sites. The methodology calculated the probability of damage (vulnerability) for assets exposed to a wildfire of a given 
intensity (hazard), accounting for direct damage and cascading consequences such as domino effects.
Other quantitative studies take slightly different routes. For example, Okano and Yamano (2016a) developed a hazard curve 
representing the quantitative relationship between frequency (probability) and intensity (hazard). The full risk assessment framework 
includes evaluating the conditional core damage probability, which represents the consequences to a nuclear power plant given a 
fire challenge. Lee et al. (2022) assessed wildfire risk to water utilities by combining historical wildfire data—such as frequency and 
burned area—into a severity metric at the service-area level. In this way, risk was captured indirectly through observed relationships 
between wildfire severity and infrastructure or operational features, rather than through an explicit probabilistic formulation of 
likelihood and consequences.
In semi-quantitative approaches applied to infrastructure systems, consequences are usually captured in a more indirect 
way. Robichaud and Padowski (2024) proposed an approach to assess wildfire risk by combining a biophysical representation 
of hazard at the watershed scale with indicators of system vulnerability and response capacity. Hazard was characterised using 
composite indices based on key fire drivers such as climate, fuels, and topography, while consequences were reflected through 
a preparedness index that captures how well water utilities can cope with post-fire impacts (e.g., treatment capacity, source 
redundancy, and planning). Although the approach was not expressed as a formal probabilistic risk equation, it effectively captured 
risk as the interaction between wildfire likelihood and potential impacts on the system.
Overall, the literature reflects several approaches to representing wildfire risk, but only a limited number of studies attempt to 
address it in a fully integrated quantitative way by consistently combining likelihood and consequences. As observed in this review, 
most papers focus on individual components of risk, such as hazard, exposure, or vulnerability-rather than bringing them together 
into a comprehensive framework. While this has led to important advances within each component, it also results in an uneven 
treatment of consequences, which are often simplified or represented through proxies and not always clearly linked to likelihood. 
This highlights the need for more integrated approaches that better connect these elements and provide a more complete and 
consistent representation of wildfire risk, particularly for critical infrastructure systems.

6.2. Risk adaptation and mitigation
Regular evaluation and identification of wildfire risks enables infrastructure owners to proactively respond to the identified 
risk. As risks are not static, it is important to recognise the importance of both adaptation and mitigation measures in addressing 
wildfire risk. In the water sector, Robinne et al. (2021) highlighted the importance of both grey (e.g., infrastructure retrofitting) 
and green (e.g., watershed restoration) mitigation measures as well as adapting these measures to account for future risks. In the 
power sector, significant attention has been given to wildfire risk mitigation, including several reviews dedicated to this aspect. For 
example, Muhs et al. (2020) classified mitigation techniques according to their ability to prevent failures/faults, prevent arc-ignition, 
and mitigate wildfire impact. Vazquez et al. (2022) grouped mitigation options across a wider range of categories: system hardening, 
vegetation management, asset inspection, forecasting/situation awareness, public safety power shut-offs, and operational response 
(in order from longer term to shorter term options). Arab et al. (2021) also included wildfire simulators and suppression tactics 
to better proactively understand wildfire behaviour to inform the response. Similar options are discussed in Vahedi et al. (2025). 
In some areas, regulators have also set out requirements for Wildfire Mitigation Plans, further expanding the expectations for such 
efforts (Vazquez et al., 2022). Examples include the California Public Utilities Commission for the power sector (Vazquez et al., 
2022) and Transport Canada for transportation (Transport Canada, 2022). Irrespective of infrastructure sector, by first identifying 
the hazard, exposure, and vulnerability of a given sector, infrastructure owners can better identify the how a specific mitigation or 
adaptation measure will impact overall risk.
7. Discussion and conclusion
Current research on wildfire risk to infrastructure reveals fragmentation across sectors and scales, which creates several research 
gaps, as well as indicates potential synergies in future research methodologies between infrastructure sectors.
Specific wildfire characterisation methodological approaches are used in each infrastructure sector. Transport systems most often 
rely on occurrence mapping or statistical indicators based on wildfire occurrence to represent wildfire hazard and exposure, while 
hazardous industries and power sectors employ detailed wildfire spread and radiative heat flux models to quantify the flaming 
front intensity and exposure. For water infrastructure risk analysis, wildfire is either characterised with empirical indicators or 
experimental and analytical heat transfer analysis.
There is substantial untapped potential for cross-sector methodological synergies in hazard identification. Water infrastructure 
risk literature has identified that WUI building damage density is a good indicator for water utility contamination (Schulze and 
Fischer, 2021), to predict impact on a larger scale; and conducts detailed experimental and heat transfer analysis to define specific 
damage threshold of pipes and scientifically and systematically select the most resistant piping materials and burial depth. This 
approach can be applied by other infrastructure systems as well: to identify the most important indicators or wildfire factors to best 
assess wildfire exposure on a landscape scale, and then conduct more detailed thermal impact analysis on a singular component 
scale. Another key opportunity lies in extending the use of wildfire spread models to water and transport systems, where dynamic 
fire behaviour is rarely represented. Conversely, heat-transfer models developed for buried water pipelines could be adapted to 
natural gas or other linear utilities where similar thermal mechanisms apply, but this transfer has not yet been explored. The solid 
flame model often applied to estimate radiative heat flux to storage tanks based on estimated adjacent flames could be applied to 
other components (e.g., water purification plant units, railway tracks, or roads).
Furthermore, there is a gap in analysing wildfire exposure mechanisms besides flames. In the power system infrastructure sector, 
effects of smoke on breakdown voltages and solar photovoltaic generation have received significant attention. However, few other 
sectors consider wildfire smoke disruptions, and no papers outside of the power sector were found to assess the impact of firebrands 
or fire environment (smoke, elevated ambient temperatures, strong winds, often associated with intense wildfire conditions). Future 
work should explicitly define which wildfire hazard modes (flames, firebrands, or smoke) are relevant to each infrastructure 
component or system; this explicit mapping is currently missing from most sectoral studies and represents a cross-cutting research 
gap. Specific hazard analysis can then be conducted for each relevant damage pathway.
From a modelling perspective, methodological diversification remains limited. Most studies outside of the highly hazardous 
industrial sector still depend on a narrow set of wildfire simulation tools, particularly FARSITE, which constrains the diversity of 
hazard representation and leads to under-representation of certain processes. Broader adoption of models such as ELMFIRE, or those 
developed in the building-safety domain, offers a clear synergy by enabling integration of smoke transport, firebrand propagation, 
and complex fuel–structure interactions into infrastructure risk assessments.
In parallel, physical vulnerability remains insufficiently constrained for many infrastructure components and wildfire conditions. 
While some experimental and numerical studies have proposed damage thresholds, these thresholds currently exist only for a limited 
set of components and fire scenarios. Future work is needed to expand the empirical and modelling basis for physical vulnerability, 
defining damage thresholds for a broader range of components and wildfire hazard modes and explicitly accounting for time-varying 
exposure. Such studies would provide essential input to quantitative risk assessments that go beyond binary failure assumptions or 
generic fragility curves. In terms of operational vulnerability assessment, the hazardous industries sector categorically only considers 
physical vulnerability. A significant gap is research and assessment of the operational disturbances to safety management and 
emergency response in case of an approaching wildfire.
Beyond these, this review also highlights the limited consideration of infrastructure interdependencies in wildfire risk research. 
While disruptions to one infrastructure system may propagate to others—for example through dependencies between energy supply, 
water services, and transport accessibility—most studies still analyse infrastructure sectors independently. As a result, wildfire

risk assessments may not fully capture cascading impacts across interconnected infrastructure networks. Future research should 
therefore focus on developing integrated wildfire risk assessment frameworks capable of representing interactions between multiple 
infrastructure systems. Such approaches could improve the identification of systemic vulnerabilities and support coordinated risk 
mitigation and resilience planning across infrastructure sectors. Regarding the overall consideration of risk, despite growing interest 
in this matter, in the context of critical infrastructure, only a limited number of studies bring together likelihood and consequences 
within a consistent framework. Most works tend to focus on individual components of risk, such as hazard, exposure, or vulnerability, 
often in isolation. While this has advanced understanding within each area, it has also led to a fragmented picture of risk, where 
consequences are frequently simplified or represented indirectly and are not always clearly linked to wildfire likelihood. Bridging 
this gap will be an important step forward, requiring approaches that better connect these elements and support a more complete 
and practical representation of wildfire risk for infrastructure systems.
From a planning and policy perspective, these findings suggest that wildfire risk management for critical infrastructure should 
move beyond sector-specific assessments towards integrated planning frameworks. Regional wildfire hazard products—such as fuel 
maps, wildfire spread simulations, and fire-weather forecasts—could be systematically incorporated into infrastructure planning, 
asset management strategies, and infrastructure resilience assessments. For example, transmission system operators, transport 
authorities, and water utilities could use shared regional hazard datasets to identify high-risk corridors or facilities and prioritise 
mitigation actions such as vegetation management, asset hardening, or redundancy planning.
In addition, regulatory frameworks could encourage the integration of wildfire risk considerations into infrastructure design 
standards and risk management procedures. This may include incorporating wildfire exposure metrics into infrastructure siting 
decisions, requiring wildfire-resilience assessments for new critical facilities in wildfire-prone regions, or integrating wildfire 
scenarios into existing safety and emergency-planning regulations. Such measures would allow infrastructure operators to move 
from reactive response to proactive risk reduction strategies.
In terms of spatial scale, most sectors remain confined to single-scale analyses, which limits understanding of cascading and 
cross-scale effects. Water studies concentrate on pipe or utility systems, hazardous industries studies on individual units or facilities, 
power system studies on generation or transmission/distribution, and power/transport studies on regional networks. Few studies link 
regional hazard mapping and exposure analysis with detailed, unit-level heat-transfer and material-response modelling. Advancing 
the field will require explicit frameworks for linking these scales—combining regional hazard products maintained by public 
authorities with sectoral models that define vulnerability (physical, operational, and critical) for specific assets. Developing robust 
methods to propagate regional hazard metrics down to component-level vulnerability assessments is therefore a central research 
gap, and a key synergy point between public agencies and infrastructure operators.
Finally, significant infrastructure categories and interactions remain largely unaddressed. Important gaps include storage of 
explosives, natural gas networks, wastewater treatment plants, and airports, as well as interdependencies among transport, power, 
and communication systems. These omissions are critical given the potential for cascading failures during wildfire events. Overall, 
recent progress towards dynamic, multi-scale, and data-driven models of exposure and vulnerability provides a foundation for more 
integrated and actionable wildfire risk management across infrastructure sectors.
Our review shows that, despite sector-specific histories and tools, critical infrastructure systems share common exposure 
pathways, vulnerability mechanisms, and relevant spatial scales. These commonalities provide a robust basis for transferring and 
adapting wildfire risk assessment methodologies across sectors, rather than developing entirely new approaches in isolation. Progress 
towards dynamic, multi-scale, and data-driven models of exposure and vulnerability, combined with stronger integration of wildfire 
risk considerations into infrastructure planning and regulatory frameworks, can support more integrated and actionable wildfire 
risk management. Coordinated development and cross-sector sharing of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability characterisation tools 
should therefore be a central priority for future wildfire risk management research and practice of critical infrastructure.
