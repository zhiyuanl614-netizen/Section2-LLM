A B S T R A C T
Concentrating solar power (CSP) installations for large-scale power generation face challenges related to the low 
yield and high-water consumption to ensure the cooling process, which make them less competitive compared to 
conventional thermal power plant. To solve these two issues, a reference CSP plant with an output of 19.9 MW is 
retrofitted by integrating a waste heat recovery system (WHRS) based on an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) to raise 
the dispatchability of the installation and reduce both water and power consumption in its cooling tower. The 
obtained results show that by integrating this new solution, the new plant achieved a 15.30% increase in 
generated electricity, a 15.13% improvement in global efficiency, and 15.35% increase in capacity factor, while 
reducing wasted heat by 16.90%, water consumption by 23.37%, and cooling power demand by 24.63% 
compared to the Gemasolar reference plant. Furthermore, this novel solution enhances the long-term sustain­
ability of CSP plants, as evidenced by a reduction in water consumption of nearly 1.04 Mm3 at the Gemasolar 
plant over its 30 years old-life cycle.
1. Introduction
The world faces serious challenges to ensure clean and stable power 
generation linked to global warming and CO2 gas emissions. Thus, 
moving from a fossil-fuel dominated energy sector to new one based on 
clean and sustainable energy sources is an obligation. In this regard, 
concentrating solar power (CSP) plants can play an important role in this 
transition. These systems concentrate solar radiation using large solar 
fields, and transfer it to a useful heat by the mean of a working fluid, 
which in turns, generates steam via steam generators, and finally elec­
tricity by the means of turbines. Four layouts of these systems are 
commonly designed: two utilize linear receivers, namely parabolic 
trough collectors and linear Fresnel reflectors, while two employ focal 
point receivers, specifically solar towers and dish systems.
However, these systems face challenges that reduce their techno- 
economic competitiveness compared to conventional power genera­
tion installations, including high capital costs and limited dispatch ca­
pacity. Furthermore, their high-water consumption, necessary for 
cooling, poses a significant additional challenge. Consequently, many 
researchers across the globe are working on this fertile topic to model 
and assess the water consumption of these large-scale power plants. In 
this regard, Rohani et al. [1] presented a tool to simulate the perfor­
mances of a CSP installation including its water consumption based on 
measured data of an operating CSP plant. Moreover, they proposed an 
approach to manage this indicator while keeping the same cost of 
electricity of the plant. Damerau et al. [2] selected four different sites in 
North Africa as a case study, and analyzed this performance indicator. At 
the end, they urge the integration of alternative cooling processes such 
as dry cooling one. Furthermore, Ben Fares and Abderafi [3] assessed the 
water usage of Noor commercial parabolic trough solar thermal power 
plant (PTSTPP) on an hourly basis and throughout the twelve months of 
the year. They performed mathematical modeling using System Advisor 
Model (SAM), and compared the numerical data with real one to pro­
pose some recommendations to minimize the water consumption in this 
installation. While Boukelia et al. [4] compared the water and power 
consumptions in the cooling system integrated into different configu­
rations of CSP plants. Moreover, they established several models based 
either on regression or ANN models to estimate these two parameters 
over the year. In another work, Ehtiwesh et al. [5] combined exergetic 
analysis with a Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) approach to evaluate three 
dimensions (exergetic, economic, and environmental) of a 50 MW 
parabolic-trough solar power plant throughout its life cycle. While, 
Duvenhage et al. [6] explored the availability of water resources to

direction, a new dry cooling strategy was proposed by Marug´an-Cruz

better than a conventional air-cooling system and uses no water. Due to 
convection and radiation, waste heat is transferred to the chilly night­
time temperatures of desert regions. However, very few studies have 
explored this direction.
Exploring the works presented in the literature so far, most of them 
address the issue of minimizing water consumption in CSP plants by 
proposing dry or hybrid cooling systems as solutions. While these ap­
proaches reduce water usage, they often come at the cost of lower plant 
efficiency, higher parasitic power demands, and increased capital costs, 
ultimately decreasing the techno-economic competitiveness of CSP 
plants. Consequently, further research is needed to maintain high power 
generation levels while minimizing water consumption. On the other 
hand, the cooling system's water demand exhibits direct correlation with 
condenser heat flux, where increased thermal loading proportionally 
elevates water consumption. Consequently, this work introduces an 
innovative alternative by integrating a Waste Heat Recovery System 
(WHRS) based on an Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) into a CSP plant. 
Unlike conventional dry cooling solutions, this approach not only min­
imizes water consumption but also enhances the plant's overall perfor­
mance. The WHRS recovers waste heat from the steam power block, 
converting it into additional electricity while simultaneously reducing 
the thermal load on the cooling system. Thus, the primary objective of 
this study is to evaluate the energy yield and cooling performances 
including both water and power consumptions of a solar thermal power 
plant equipped with an ORC-based WHR system.
2. Data and methodology
The main objective of the proposed solar installation for large-scale 
power generation is to decrease water consumption in a wet cooling 
system integrated into a solar thermal power plant while keeping its 
techno-economic competitiveness, including its high yield and low in­
vestment costs. To achieve this aim, firstly, the main data and inputs of 
the whole installation, including the solar field, power block, and 
cooling system, are gathered and classified. Secondly, the mathematical 
model of the investigated system is established and presented; then, the 
model will be validated. At the end, the results of this proposed 
approach, including hourly and annual techno-economic performances, 
will be compared to a conventional plant and simulated.
2.1. Studied installation
The proposed concentrated solar power plant is comprised of four 
sub-blocks; a solar field (SF), an energy storage system (ESS), a power 
block (PB), and waste heat recovery system (WHRS). The solar field of a 
total surface of more than 305,000 m2, is based on solar tower tech­
nology operating on molten solar salt as the heat transfer medium under 
a temperature range 290-565 ◦C. Furthermore, the PB is a regenerative 
Rankine able to produce a net output of 19.9 MW. The design of this 
installation is similar to that of the operational Gemasolar plant erected 
in Spain. However, unlike Gemasolar plant which incorporates a cooling 
tower within the steam PB's condenser to assure the cooling process, the 
investigated configuration integrates a WHRS. The WHRS operates on 
an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) to recover the wasted heat from the PB, 
generating electricity at a low-temperature range. The main motivation 
behind the incorporation of this system is to reduce the water con­
sumption by minimizing heat rejection. Thus, in this layout, the cooling 
tower is equipped with the WHRS condenser. The schematic of the 
proposed installation is given in Fig. 1. Moreover, its main data and 
inputs are summarized in Table 1.
2.2. Considered assumptions
Before demonstrating the mathematical model developed to simulate 
the cooling performances of the studied installation in terms of yield and 
water consumption, considering specific assumptions is critical. These 
assumptions are divided into two sets. The first is related to the whole 
installation, including the solar field, the topping and bottoming power 
blocks, as well as the cooling block. The other set of assumptions is 
dedicated only to the cooling system, including the WHRS's condenser 
and cooling tower. The first set, associated with the whole power gen­
eration system, is summarized as: 
• All components of the whole installation work steadily.
• Any heat lost or pressure drops in the turbines, pumps, heat ex­
changers, and pipes are ignored.
• Any changes in kinetic energy and potential energy for the whole 
system are omitted.
• The working fluids (water and organic fluid) are considered to be in 
the saturated liquid phase at the condenser and the economizer 
outlets, and at the saturated vapor phase at the outlet of the 
evaporators.
• The thermo-physical properties of the working fluids change only 
with pressure and temperature.
On the other hand, in addition to before-mentioned assumptions, the

3.1. Validation
In this subsection, the validation of the developed model is presented 
to ensure the accuracy of the established model's results. However, it 
should be pointed out that extracting real data from operational large- 
scale CSP power plants to verify the model's reliability is very difficult 
due to the fact that most of these installations belong to the private 
sector. Thus, validation was achieved based on existing models from the 
literature. Starting with the solar field, where the model was developed 
under the System Advisor Model (SAM) software [38], which is vali­
dated against real data of Gemasolar [32] and Andasol 1 [48] power 
plants. Then, for the power block based on a steam Rankine regenerative 
cycle, it was already validated against real data of an operating plant 
[49]. What follows, the accuracy of the WHRS operating within an ORC 
power block was already verified with real data of a geothermal power 
plant [50]. Finally, to validate the accuracy and reliability of the cooling 
tower model, its predictions were compared against estimates obtained 
by SAM software. Two key performance indicators were evaluated: 
water consumption for cooling and cooling power consumption, where 
only stand-alone plant without integrated WHRS is considered. The 
comparison is presented visually in Figs. 4 and 5, and quantitatively in 
Table 5.
Fig. 4 illustrates the comparison between water consumption pre­
dicted by the model and that estimated by SAM. The scatter distribution 
shows an acceptable correlation, indicating a close agreement between 
the two datasets. Similarly, Fig. 5 presents the comparison for cooling 
power consumption, where the distribution of points across distinct 
operating bands suggests a moderate predictive capability of the model. 
On the other hand, Table 5 summarizes the statistical indicators used to 
quantify the accuracy of the model (their equations are given in the 
Annex): For water consumption, the MPE was −8.57%, indicating a 
slight underestimation by the model on average. The MBE was −0.3899 
kg/s, with a RMSE of 1.6702 kg/s. The correlation coefficient R was 
found to be 0.9709, confirming an acceptable agreement between the 
model and SAM estimations. However, as illustrated in Fig. 4, two 
different operating regimes can be clearly identified. The first cluster 
(~9–15 kg/s) corresponds to operation under design conditions, where 
the model exhibits excellent agreement with the SAM. The second 
cluster, located at lower mass flow rates, represents off-design and low- 
load operating conditions. The larger deviations observed in this regime 
can be attributed to inherent differences in the modeling approaches. In 
the present study, well-established correlations are used for estimating 
heat transfer coefficients in the heat exchangers and efficiencies of the 
turbomachinery, whereas SAM relies on internal correlations and treats 
the steam power block as an integrated unit. Although the overall cor­
relation coefficient remains high (R = 0.971), a higher relative uncer­
tainty is observed at low flow rates. This behavior is commonly reported 
in transient modeling of solar thermal systems.

showed an MPE of −9.54%, an MBE of −0.0023 MW, and an RMSE of 
0.0089 MW, indicating a slight tendency of the model to underpredict 
the cooling requirements. The corresponding correlation coefficient R 
was 0.6960. While this represents a moderate correlation, it is important

3.2. Comparative analysis
In this subsection, three performance indicators are compared and 
analyzed: generated power, cooling water flow rate, and power con­
sumption required for the cooling process. In this regard, Gemasolar is 
taken as a reference plant to investigate the enhancements in these pa­
rameters by integrating the WHRS. Consequently, the annual 
comparative analysis of these two layouts is presented in Table 6 and 
Figs. 5 and 6. Moreover, the annual mean hourly variations of these 
performances for the studied plant with (new plant) and without WHRS 
integration (reference plant) are plotted in Fig. 7.
Based on the provided Figures (5 and 6) and Table 6, a clear 
enhancement in performance is observed for the new plant integrated 
with a WHRS compared to the conventional Gemasolar plant. The WHRS 
significantly boosts the energy output and overall efficiency while sub­
stantially reducing the cooling burden. Specifically, the new plant 
demonstrates a 15.30% increase in generated electrical power over the 
year EGPlant (from 42.49 GWh for Gemasolar to 48.99 GWh) and a 
15.13% improvement in plant efficiency (from 5.42% to 6.24%). This 
higher yield directly translates to a 5.29% increase in CF, reaching 
25.66% for the WHRS-integrated plant compared to 24.37% for Gema­
solar. These improvements are primarily attributable to the ORC bot­
toming cycle, which recovers otherwise wasted heat from the steam 
power block exhaust, converting it into additional electricity and thus 
optimizing the utilization of the solar thermal input. The integration of 
the WHRS also leads to remarkable improvements in the cooling per­
formance indicators. The rejected heat energy ˙Qrej from the plant is 
reduced by 16.90%, decreasing from 79.76 GWh in the conventional 
Gemasolar plant to 66.28 GWh in the new plant. This reduction in heat 
rejection directly alleviates the load on the cooling system. Conse­
quently, the cooling water flow rate ˙mcw for cooling sees a substantial 
23.37% decrease, falling from 147,810 m3 for Gemasolar to 113,262 m3 
for the new plant. Similarly, the required power to drive the pump and 
fan in the cooling tower is reduced by 24.63%, from 0.2123 GWh to 
0.1600 GWh. This lower energy demand for cooling process further 
contributes to the net efficiency gain of the integrated plant. The specific 
consumed water also declines significantly by 21.66%, from 3.4786 m3/ 
MWh to 2.7251 m3/MWh. These numerical reductions underscore the 
environmental and operational benefits, as less water is consumed, and 
less parasitic power is needed to dissipate waste heat, enhancing the 
overall sustainability of the whole installation operation. On the other 
hand, considering the economic dimension of the present study, repre­
sented by two performance indicators including total investment cost 
and LCOE to assess the techno-economic viability of the plant's retrofit, 
and as presented in Table 6, an increase of less than 1.6 M$ (+0.74%) is 
observed for the plant in question compared to the reference Gemasolar 
plant, which corresponds to the cost related to the integrated WHRS 
operating with an ORC block. This cost is relatively low compared to the 
generated power, owing to the low energy flux received from the solar 
power block; thus, it reduces the size of the incorporated heat ex­
changers and turbomachinery. On the other hand, due to the pivotal role 
of this block in boosting the generated power, the LCOE has decreased 
by about 12.50% (from 0.48 $/kWh for the Gemasolar reference plant to 
0.42 $/kWh for the new one). This highlights the techno-economic 
feasibility of the proposed solution to simultaneously increase the 
dispatch capacity of CSP plants and reduce their water consumption at 
low cost.
This can be confirmed by Fig. 7, where the enhancements due to 
WHRS integration are clearly hour-dependent, with negligible im­
provements observed during early morning and late evening hours due 
to low solar irradiance, thus, heat rates and heat rejected rate by the 
steam power block condenser. Notably, the maximum enhancement 
occurs around solar noon (12:00–14:00) when solar input is highest: 
electricity generation improves by up to 19.5%, cooling water flow rate 
reduces by approximately 17%, and cooling power demand decreases by 
around 7%. This performance gain is attributed to the improved thermal 
recovery and reduced cooling burden provided by the WHRS during 
peak thermal load periods, thereby improving overall system efficiency 
when solar energy is most available.

3.3. Performances assessment
In this subsection, the performances of the solar tower plant with 
integrated WHRS are further assessed on an hourly basis. The analysis 
focuses on the correlation between the thermal load in the topping solar 
block, indicated by its generated power, and the cooling tower's per­
formances, encompassing both water flow rate and cooling power de­
mand. The annual mean hourly heat rate and power generation profiles, 
alongside the cooling performance profiles, are plotted in Figs. 8 and 9. 
Furthermore, Figs. 10 and 11 present the time series data for hourly 
water mass flow rate versus hourly generated power, and hourly power 
consumption in the cooling tower versus hourly generated power, 
respectively, for the studied plant with integrated WHRS.
The annual mean hourly energy flux shown in Fig. 8 shows power 
generation peaking at 17–18 MW (14 MW from the solar block, and 3-4 
MW from the WHRS) during midday, closely tracking solar irradiance, 
while nighttime values drop to near zero due to absent DNI. This diurnal 
pattern underscores the system's reliance on solar input, with WHRS 
recovering ~2–3 MW of otherwise wasted heat during peak hours, 
evident in the elevated afternoon flux. Fig. 10's time-series data further 
quantifies the water-power nexus: water consumption flow rate surges 
to ~40 m3/h at 18 MWh electrical output, reflecting increased evapo­
rative cooling demand to dissipate heat from WHRS-enhanced thermal 
loads. The linear correlation between water uses and power output 
highlights the cooling tower's critical role in maintaining thermal 
equilibrium.
Figs. 9 and 11 dissect the cooling tower's operational physics. Annual 
profiles (Fig. 9) demonstrate that electricity demand for cooling scales 
with water use, averaging 0.06–0.07 MW at ~45 m3/h consumption—a 
~1:750 ratio (power-to-water), consistent with fan and pump power 
requirements for evaporative cooling. Fig. 11's hourly data reveals 
transient spikes: cooling energy briefly hits 0.08 MW during rapid load 
shifts (e.g., cloud cover), exposing the system's inertia in balancing 
thermal mass. Notably, WHRS integration reduces water consumption 
mass flow rate by ~20% during off-peak hours (e.g., 9 m3/h vs. 11 m3/h 
in conventional systems), as recovered waste heat displaces auxiliary 
heating needs.
A crucial observation to highlight is that the peak values for key 
performance metrics, including generated power, water consumption 
flow rate, and cooling power demand, differ significantly between the 
annual mean hourly profiles and the time series data. Specifically, the 
peaks presented in the annual mean hourly profiles (Figs. 8 and 9) are 
18 MW for generated electricity, 44 m3/h for water consumption, and 
0.065 MW for cooling power demand. These are notably lower than the 
peaks observed in the time series data (Figs. 10 and 11), which reach 19 
MW, 45 m3/h, and 0.07 MW, respectively. This discrepancy arises 
because the annual mean profiles represent averaged performance 
throughout the whole year, smoothing out extreme values, whereas the 
time series data directly captures the highest instantaneous values, 
particularly those occurring during peak solar radiation periods in 
summer days.
4. Conclusion
In the present study, the operating solar power plant -Gemasolar-has 
been retrofitted by integrating a waste heat recovery system (WHRS) 
based on an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) to raise the dispatchability of 
the installation and reduce both water and power consumption in its 
cooling tower. First, the developed model was validated to confirm its 
accuracy. Then, the yield and cooling performances of the new layout 
were compared to those of the conventional Gemasolar plant. Finally, 
the hourly cooling performances, including water consumption and 
energy demand in the cooling tower were analyzed and discussed. The 
following findings were highlighted: 
• The developed model for the cooling system has been successfully 
validated against SAM estimations, demonstrating sufficient reli­
ability for the modelling of the studied plant with the integrated 
cooling system. The model with high correlation coefficient of R =
0.9709 for water consumption flow rate and a moderate correlation 
of R = 0.696 for cooling power demand, along with low MPE, MBE 
and RMSE values, confirming its accuracy in predicting cooling 
performance.
• From an annual performance perspective, the integration of WHRS 
significantly improves plant efficiency and sustainability. The new 
plant achieved a 15.30% increase in annual generated power (from 
42.49 to 48.99 GWh), 15.13% improvement in global efficiency, and 
15.35% increase in capacity factor, while reducing wasted heat by 
16.90%, water consumption by 23.37%, and cooling power by 
24.63% compared to the Gemasolar reference plant.
• Hourly analysis reveals that WHRS benefits are time-dependent, 
peaking around solar noon (12:00–14:00), where electricity gener­
ation increases by 19.5%, water flow rates drops by ~17%, and 
cooling power demand is reduced by ~7%, owing to optimal heat 
recovery during high solar input periods.
• Performances assessment based on annual mean hourly profiles 
shows that power generation peaks at ~18 MW, with corresponding 
water consumption and cooling energy requirements reaching 44 
m3/h and 0.065 MW, respectively—demonstrating a consistent 
operational trend that follows solar resource availability.
• Time-series analysis provides further insight into system dynamics, 
showing maximum instantaneous values of ~19 MW for power 
generation, 45 m3/h for water flow rate, and 0.07 MW for cooling 
power demand. These results highlight the system's responsiveness 
to real-time solar variations and the WHRS's role in mitigating peak 
thermal loads while improving overall efficiency.
• By integrating this WHRS to Gemasolar plant, the long-term sus­
tainability of these installations was enhanced, as evidenced by a 
reduction in water consumption of nearly 1.04 Mm3 at the Gema­
solar plant over its 30 years old-life cycle.
