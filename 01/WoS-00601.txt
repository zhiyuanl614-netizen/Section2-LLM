In the nuclear accident which occurred in Japan on March 11, 2011, several units of Fukushima con-
ventional BWR experienced a total loss of power and water supply triggered by a heavy earthquake and
subsequent Tsunami which were outside design models. In the past, when an accident occurred, operators
in nuclear power plants (NPP) followed emergency operating procedures (EOPs) or severe accident man-
agement guidance (SAMG). However, EOP and SAMG are symptom-based procedures to cope with severe
transients and accidents, depending on real-time operational parameters. Ultimate response guidelines
(URG), a plant speciﬁc interim remedy action plan, was developed to manage accidents caused by com-
pound disasters which exceed design models. The URG guides the plant operators’ conduct of reactor
depressurization, core cooling water injection, and containment venting. This study adopts NUREG/CR-
6883 (Standardized Plant Analysis Risk Human Reliability Analysis, SPAR-H) to evaluate human error
probabilities (HEPs) of action and diagnosis in the current URG procedures. We found the human reli-
ability of URG procedures analyzed by SPAR-H is about 85% (depending on different decision makers).
Upgrading the training level of staff or enhancing plant managers ability to decide whether to execute
URG will enhance the human reliability of URG procedures.

1. Introduction
A recent nuclear accident occurred at the Fukushima Daiichi
power plant after a magnitude 9.0 earthquake triggered tsunami
waves upwards of 14 m on March 11, 2011. It was the most power-
ful earthquake ever to hit Japan, and one of the ﬁve most powerful
earthquakes recorded since modern record-keeping began in 1900.
After earthquake, reactors of Fukushima Daiichi power plant have
scrammed immediately. However, the design basis for tsunamis
was 5.7 m and the plant was struck by waves reaching 14 m.

2. Severe accident management guidance
In the past, when severe accidents occurred, operators in NPP
followed emergency operating procedures (EOPs) or severe acci-
dent management guidance (SAMG).
For managing severe accidents, there are currently several levels
of guidance for the operating staff of a commercial nuclear power
station (Fig. 1). The ﬁrst level, termed Operating Procedures (OPs)
focuses on plant operation during the time that plant parameters
are within an acceptable range. The second level, termed Abnormal
Operating Procedures (AOPs) focuses on restoring the function of
systems which could impact overall plant operating margins. The
third level, termed EOPs, is aimed at bringing the plant to a safe,
stable state following a reactor trip or safety injection signal. These
procedures represent the initial phase of utility accident manage-
ment and have been formulated around essential safety functions
such as reactivity control, adequate core cooling and removal of the
heat generated in the reactor core (Nuclear Services/Engineering
Services). In some accidents, uncertainties may exist in both plant
status and in the outcome of actions. Therefore guidance for those
situations should not be prescriptive in nature but rather should
propose a range of possible actions and should allow for additional
evaluation and alternative actions. Such guidance is usually termed
SAMG (IAEA, 2009).
However, EOP and SAMG are symptom-basis procedures to
cope with severe transients and accidents, depending on real-time
operational parameters. One lesson learned from the Fukushima
accident which exceeded the design basis, is that operational
parameters may well be unreliable. When a compound disaster like
the Fukushima accident occurs, EOPs or SAMG cannot be performed
effectively without adequate information about the reactor. The key
parameters are readiness of the water supply, Reactor Pressure Ves-
sel (RPV) Depressurization and Containment Venting with limited
manpower and time. URG, a set of plant speciﬁc interim remedy
actions, were developed to manage accidents caused by a com-
pound disaster beyond the design basis. The core concept of URG
is treating accidents beyond design basis as off-nominal or abnor-
mal events (black blocks in Fig. 1). Expect developing procedures,
URG also needs to build redundant channels of water injection. The
main difference among URG, EOPs and SAMG is that when the plant
status does not recovery in time after severe accident, the URG has
to be executed without most information of unit. When a severe
accident happened with loss of AC power and coolant water, EOP,
SAMG and URG will be initiated at the same time. EOPs and SAMG
focus on not only maintaining reactor core cooling and preventing
the release of radioactive material, but also protecting assets of NPP.
However, URG is an irreversible choice and may inﬂict permanent
damage on the reactor, so it needs senior manager such as the vice
president or the plant manager to make the decision. The decision
to execute URG requires someone with sufﬁcient authority who is
able to shoulder the burden. In URG, the priority decision maker
is the vice president followed by the plant manager and the shift
supervisor. The switch point of executing URG is that the decision
maker determine external support could not arrive in time because
the support system of government or the trafﬁc system have bro-
ken down, and NPP would rather shoulder the loss of NPP’s assets
than take the risk of the core melt and the release of radioactive
material.
The URG guides plant operators to conduct reactor depressur-
ization, core cooling water injection, and containment venting.
These guidelines provide procedures to follow for loss of the reactor
building service water (RBSW) system or reactor building cool-
ing water (RBCW) system to remove decay heat, dropping reactor

The URG procedures include several measures to be performed:

to 1.45 MPa by regulating system safety/relief valve; to assure

2. Prepare alternative water supply which might include raw or sea
water powered by gravity or any non-designed pumps within the

3. If plant critical status cannot be restored before passive reac-
tor core isolation cooling system of BWR or auxiliary feedwater
pump of SG becomes inoperable, perform emergency depressur-
ization to further lower down system pressure to containment
pressure by opening automatic depressurization system of BWR
or SG power operated relief valve of PWRs.
4. Inject raw or sea water into BWR reactor of PWR SG after the
system fully depressurizes.
5. Perform containment direct venting if containment pressure is
beyond design to maintain containment integrity.
3. Human reliability analysis
Many studies have demonstrated the feasibility of URG under
conditions similar to the Fukushima accident (EPRI, 1992). How-
ever, it is yet to be veriﬁed whether operators and staff in NPP
can follow URG without human error in such extreme situations.
It is well established that the human contribution to the risk of
operating complex technological systems is signiﬁcant, with typi-
cal estimates lying in the range of 60–85% (Trager, 1985). Human
reliability analysis (HRA), an essential component of a methodology
to provide a realistic evaluation of severe accident management
strategies (Kelly and Boring, 2011), is the means by which the
human contribution to risk is assessed, both qualitatively and quan-
titatively (Ang et al., 2001). After the Fukushima accident, the new
human error database such as SACADA (Scenario Authoring, Char-
acterization, and Debrieﬁng Application) is developed by the U.S.
Nuclear Regulatory Commission to address the data need in the
estimation of human error probabilities (HEPs) (Chang et al., 2014).
Although the program of building a new database is in commission,
the new HRA method has to hold until that sufﬁcient human per-
formance data have been accumulated. The most often used HRA
methods in NPPs are still Technique for Human Error Rate Predic-
tion (THERP), Accident Sequence Evaluation Program (ASEP) and
Standardized Plant Analysis Risk Human Reliability Assessment
(SPAR-H) after Fukushima accident (Hickling and Bowie, 2012).
SPAR-H was born out of THERP and ASEP as a further simpliﬁcation
and generalization of these two approaches (Gertman et al., 2005;
Varde et al., 2011; Kamyab and Nematollahia, 2012). The important
aspect of the SPAR-H method is that human activity is assigned to
one of two general task categories: action and diagnosis. The anal-
ysis method is based on eight performance shaping factors (PSF)
that encompass the majority of the contributors to human error.
These eight performance shaping factors are available time, stress
and stressors, experience and training, complexity, ergonomics and
human machine interface, procedures, ﬁtness for duty, and work
processes. Within different task categories, each PSF features a list
of levels and associated multipliers (Table 1).
After evaluating PSFs, HEPs are determined by a multiplicative
calculation (1). In the formula, nominal human error probability
(NHEP) is 1E−2 on diagnosis analysis and 1E−3 on action analysis.
PSF is the product of all PSF multipliers.
HEP = NHEP · PSFcomposite
(1)
In previous versions of the SPAR-H method, it was possible to
assign PSF levels that result in calculation of a mean that would be
numerically larger than one. When three or more negative multiple
PSFs are present, the worksheet includes an adjustment factor to
avoid probability estimates greater than one (2).
HEP =
NHEP · PSFcomposite
NHEP · (PSFcomposite −1) + 1
(2)
Dependency should be calculated for each task except the ﬁrst
task in the sequence.
3.1. HRA in severe accident management
In this section, this study collected information about manage-
ment of the Fukushima Daiichi accident. Based on those results, the
performance of URG in similar accidents was evaluated. Tradition-
ally, accounting for the inﬂuence of multiple shaping factors having
multiple levels of inﬂuence without imposing a high degree of
expert consensus judgment on the HRA process has proven difﬁcult.
SPAR-H makes the assignment of human error probability a more
repeatable function and less dependent on the analyst performing
the HRA. SPAR-H has clear criteria to determinate the multipliers on
most of PSFs except the PSF of ergonomics/HMI which need human
factors experts to make judgments. The requirements of experts in
this study include a professional background in human factors and
operating knowledge of NPP.

4. Results and discussion
Following the SPAR-H method, expert assessments were per-
formed to determine whether the task contain a signiﬁcant amount
of diagnosis activity, to evaluate levels of each PSF, to note speciﬁc
reasons for PSF level selection, and to ﬁnd the multiplier for diagno-
sis or action of each PSF. For example, the task of operator diagnoses
event (the ﬁrst task after reactor water level keeps dropping or SBO
in Fig. 3) contains a signiﬁcant amount of diagnosis activity with-
out any action activity, so we only need to evaluate PSFs for the
diagnosis portion of the task. On the basis of similar conditions in
the Fukushima Daiichi accident, where operators were under stress
as such a severe accident could endanger public safety, only a few
plant parameters were available to monitor, but the serious situa-
tion with SBO was obvious. Thus, the evaluation of PSF levels are:
stress/stressors is extreme, ergonomics/HMI is poor, diagnosis is
complex, and other factors are normal. The associated multiplier of
each PSF level appears in Table 3. Because there are fewer than 3
negative multiple PSFs in this task, the result of the diagnosis fail-
ure probability is calculated using formula (1). The ﬁnal diagnosis
HEP of this task is: 1.0E−2 × 1 × 5 × 0.1 × 1 × 1 × 10 × 1 × 1 = 0.05.
After calculating the probability of each task in the URG pro-
cedures determined by the SPAR-H method, the net probability of
state X is the HEP of the procedures. An example of HEP calculation
in URG procedures is presented in Fig. 4. Each task has two values,
the top one is human reliability, and the bottom one is HEP. The
results of event analysis with different conditions are presented in
Table 4 and Fig. 5.

The results of event analysis show that raising the training level
of staff from low to normal would increase the human reliability
about 9%, and raising the level from normal to high would increase
the human reliability about 3%. In other words, the current design
of URG still requires adequate training to maintain human reliabil-
ity. In general, a licensed operator has at least reached the normal
level of training. However training scenarios with those abnormal
conditions still do not appear in the design base. When a compound
disaster such as the Fukushima accident occurred, the current train-
ing level probably was inadequate in NPP to handle such a severe
accident.
Another issue is the effect caused by different decision mak-
ers executing URG. In the event analysis, a decision by the plant
manager would increase human reliability 1–3% over a decision
by the vice president. Based on the analysis, the main differences
between the plant manager and the vice president are the com-
plexity of diagnosis and situational awareness. The plant manager’s
scope of consideration is limited to the factory. Unlike the plant
manager, the vice president has too many variables such as disas-
ter situations, social responsibility, company assets, and external
assistance, involved in concurrent diagnoses. Another factor is situ-
ational awareness deﬁned as perception of the elements in the
environment within a volume of time and space, comprehension
of their meaning, and projection of their status in the near future.
Generally, an on-site manager has more ability to recognize the
situation than an off-site one.
The human reliability quantiﬁcation method used in this study
was applied in a case study, which was carried out in close co-
operation with NPP. The human reliability quantiﬁcation method,
SPAR-H has so far been applied only to preventive accident man-
agement. For advance evaluation the reliability in severe accident,
it has to be modiﬁed to mitigate measures in harsh environ-
ments. Current PSFs of SPAR-H did not consider the effect caused
by environment with abnormal conditions, such as a conﬂagra-
tion, radioactive contaminated, the aftershock or the aftermath of
a tsunami. Those conditions not only generate stress or reduce
available time, but also may cause task interruptions. For exam-
ple, because operators need to take refuge with aftershocks, they
may not execute accident management continuously. Such situ-
ation should be involved in future research and development of
HRA.
5. Conclusion and future research
A new concept of severe accident management, URG, provides
generic and safety procedures to follow when severe accidents
occur. Under current conditions, the training level of staff and the
decision maker in NPP, the human reliability of URG procedures
analyzed by SPAR-H is about 85%. As an example, if core damage
frequency (CDF) of a NPP loss of offsite power is 1.47E−7, it can be
reduced to 2.21E−08 with adding URG. URG, provides an additional
protection parallel with SAMG, is a feasible alternative to manage
severe accidents beyond the design basis. However, forming a com-
plete set of scenarios is necessary to reduce the HEP. Upgrading
the training level of staff or aiding the plant manager in making a
decision to execute URG will enhance the human reliability of URG
procedures. Nevertheless, other factors such as improving proce-
dures or extending available time also have potential to contribute
to improved HEP and human reliability. SPAR-H, as an HRA method,
is suggested to evaluate HEP and human reliability with limited
information. If URG turns into a formal procedure in NPP, a com-
plete veriﬁcation and validation will be indispensable for safety
analysis. Thus, a speciﬁc model and more factors should be involved
in future research.
