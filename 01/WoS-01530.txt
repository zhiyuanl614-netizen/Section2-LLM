Implementing multiple-effect distillation and reverse osmosis thermal 
coupling to improve desalination process performance in combined water

A B S T R A C T  
Cogeneration has recently been an appropriate engineering approach to supply increasing energy demands. The 
present study demonstrates a significant influence on increase in productivity and reduction of utility con­
sumption of thermal desalination, considering a novel internally coupled multiple-effect distillation and reverse 
osmosis desalination process in a cogeneration of water and power plant. The proposed power generation unit, 
consisting of two SGT600 gas turbines as prime movers, two heat recovery steam generators and one back- 
pressure steam turbine, has been applied. The objective is to propose a modified multiple-effect distillation 
process, due to availability of sufficient cooling water, provided by reverse osmosis reject water. The basic 
configuration comprises a hybrid desalination using a dual purpose condenser, results of which showed a 45% 
reduction in electrical energy consumption of multiple-effect distillation, accompanying a 67% decrease in in­
take water cost. In modified configuration, condenser temperature decreases from 48 to 45℃, allowing number 
of effects to increase from 5 to 7. Therefore, produced water rate and gain output ratio of the modified multiple- 
effect distillation increase by 30% and 31%, respectively, while specific energy consumption decreases by 6%, 
causing a two-year reduction in cogeneration of water and power plant pay-back period compared to the basic 
configuration.   
1. Introduction 
Cogeneration of water and power (CWP) has recently been a new 
engineering approach for increasing efficiency, reducing energy con­
sumption and environmental impacts, along with reducing carbon di­
oxide emission. The energy-saving potential is considered from aspects 
including waste heat recovery, available heat re-use, as well as exergy 
loss utilization. To enhance the overall performance of the system and 
reduce energy consumption, several researchers proposed the concept 
of polygeneration. Maraver et al. [1] assessed the energy feasibility and 
performance of the organic Rankine cycle for heat and power genera­
tion through the fuel energy saving ratio. Increase in the efficient use of 
natural resources in the process integration is evidenced by in­
vestigating various examples of polygeneration systems [2]. Adopting 
significant methods to recover waste heat can improve the energy ef­
ficiency, energy-saving, and operation safety of cogeneration [3]. Di­
mensionless parameters including cost-based characteristics and power 
and water gain ratio are considered to optimize cogeneration power 
and desalting plants [4]. Accordingly, studies were conducted to ana­
lyze gas distribution networks [5] and the impact of adding thermal 
storage to a combined heat and power (CHP) plant [6]. The impact of 
adjustable-practical operating mode on the efficiency of cogeneration 
for CHP and non-CHP parts of a plant was assessed in another research  
[7]. 
The benefits of cogeneration include improvement in fuel con­
sumption, decrease in environmental pollution caused by fossil fuel 
combustion and prevention of useful heat waste from the power plant 
prime mover exhaust [8]. Environmental benefits and sustainability can 
introduce other aspects of cogeneration [9]. To investigate some of the 
problems regarding the design, construction, and management of co­
generation plants for district heating networks, Franco and Bellina [10] 
proposed a multi-objective optimization from energy and economic 
viewpoints. They confirmed that the optimum design can be obtained 
using a cost function that minimizes the operational cost of the system,

internal combustion engines, steam turbines, and gas turbines are the 
most commonly used prime movers while fuel cells, Stirling engines,

alternative prime movers based on geothermal, solar, photovoltaic en­

including multi-effect distillation and solar collectors was proposed as

particular case studies considered some aspects of geothermal plants

a grid-connected hybrid renewable energy integrated with a reverse 
osmosis (RO) desalination plant through three various sources of energy 
which comprised photovoltaic module along with a wind turbine, either 
battery or hydrogen storage system, as well as a diesel generator. Deniz 
and Cinar [15] as well as Zubair et al. [16] proposed economic models 
of the water-heated humidification-dehumidification desalination 
system using solar energy to consider characteristics including ther­
modynamic performance, water production, and the system cost. 
Many studies were conducted to investigate and analyze water and 
power cogeneration plants in terms of thermodynamic modeling [17], 
as well as parametric optimization [18]. Multi-stage flash (MSF), mul­
tiple-effect distillation (MED) and reverse osmosis (RO) are the most 
conventional desalination methods for medium and large-scale water 
desalination. Among the conventional thermal units, the MED has a 
lower initial investment cost, lower corrosion rate, higher lifetime, and 
lower pumping power consumption [19]. Cardona et al. [20] evaluated 
a small-scale hybrid CHP including a reciprocating engine, multi-effect 
evaporation (MEE) and a single-stage SWRO as desalination units. Wu L 
et al. [21] performed the optimization of a dual-purpose plant including 
a power plant and MSF and RO units by genetic algorithms. Design 
constraints in this study consisted of the demand for power and water 
and the minimum annual cost. Results showed that a combined water 
and power plant based on an extraction-condensing steam turbine with 
an MSF is suitable for the capacity lower than 8000 m3/hr; however, 
cogeneration based on a back-pressure steam turbine along with an 
MED and RO desalination is suited for the capacity higher than 
8000 m3/hr.Salimi and Amidpour [22] assessed a small-scale MED in­
tegrated with an internal combustion engine. This parametric study 
demonstrated that integration could be a preferable solution in terms of 
economic. Abdulrahim and Chung [23] modeled nine different con­
figurations of power and water conversion technologies to propose an 
ideal reversible cogeneration power and desalting plant with respect to 
power and water ratio. Tamburini et al. [24] designed a dual-purpose 
configuration for a large MED existing with a capacity of 36,000 m3/ 
day including a steam power plant based on an extraction-condensing 
steam turbine, and conducted a sensitivity analysis to investigate the 
impacts of several design and operation parameters on the energy 
fraction and efficiency of cogeneration. 
Saie et al. [25] considered the cogeneration based on a combined 
cycle power plant including MED. To determine the optimal config­
uration, they considered the capacity of the gas turbine, the heat re­
covery steam generator (HRSG) and the type of steam turbine at a fixed 
demand for water and power. To achieve an optimal model of cogen­
eration, Nashar [26] studied the impact of various prime movers on the 
MSF units. Employing the exergy analysis technique, initial investment 
cost as well as water and electricity costs associated with the extraction- 
condensing steam turbine were lower. Shakib et al. [27,28] performed a 
techno-economic analysis on a cogeneration system including a gas 
turbine with/without air preheater, and HRSG and the MED unit. They 
showed that the overall cost of the product is reduced by 19% and the 
exergy efficiency increases by 19% using the optimization approach 
and air preheater. Shakouri et al. [29] considered the feasibility of

Mokhtari et al. [34] conducted a technical analysis of a hybrid 
system consisting of a SGT400 gas turbine and MED and RO desalina­

Salimi et al. [35] considered a configuration between MED and RO 
units in a hybrid plant for which the performance in various cases was 
shown based on R-curves. Khoshgoftar et al. [36] performed an exergo- 
economic analysis on the optimization of coupling of site utility steam 
network with MED-RO desalination units and evaluated cogeneration 
potential, considering fuel demand as well as power and water pro­
duction. 
Filippini et al. [37] developed a mathematical model in the MED-RO 
hybrid desalination systems and conducted a performance sensitivity 
analysis considering variable seawater properties and steam demand. 
The purpose of the study was to investigate the productivity of the 
various configurations, the purity of produced water, and the energy 
consumption in several MED design cases integrated with RO unit. 
Almutairi et al. [38] developed a comprehensive model of cogeneration 
based on combined cycle power plant (CCPP) and desalination using 
real operation data. They considered the performance of the cogen­
eration plant in terms of different ambient temperatures, pressure ratio, 
feed water temperatures, number of effects and entrainment ratios, and 
suggested numerous possibilities to improve the proposed system. 
Despite various researches carried out to optimize cogeneration of 
water and power (CWP) processes, there is still a lack of studies on the 
reduction of energy consumption and water cost, especially in thermal 
desalination units. The above mentioned literature review, revealed the 
influence of the full-hybridization of MED or RO desalting systems 
along with power generation units. Additionally, several studies ex­
amined the optimal configuration of MED and RO in an integrated 
plant. However, the influence of internal coupling between thermal 
(MED) and membrane (RO) desalination processes has not yet been 
thoroughly considered. In the present study, a novel internally coupled 
MED-RO desalination process is proposed by employing a dual purpose 
condenser. Generally, the thermal coupling of RO and MED can be 
classified into two main approaches; MED condenser cooling (MCC) and 
RO feed preheating (RFP). In the MCC approach, the RO unit reject is 
utilized as a portion of condenser coolant water for MED to reduce the 
required seawater flow rate for cooling of the condenser. In other 
words, increase in the productivity and decrease in the MED energy 
consumption stems from the reduction of the volume of sea water en­
tering to MED condenser. Another approach in the thermal coupling of 
MED and RO desalination is the RO feed preheating by the MED brine 
and product streams. Variation in the temperature of feed water en­
tering the RO module leads to change in specific energy consumption, 
purity of the produced water, and inlet feed water pressure so that the 
highest and lowest amounts of the specific energy consumption are 
required in the coldest and hottest months of the year, respectively. 
Thus, available heat energy in the MED brine and product streams can

MED-RO thermal coupling based on the MCC approach is beneficial 
in the CWP, especially, in hot seasons. One of the design constraints of 
MED is its high cooling water requirement during hot months of the 
year, so that more intake water should be supplied (up to 10 times the 
produced water, in some situations) to compensate for the influence of 
increase in inlet water temperature on the performance of the MED

thermal coupling method (TCM), the reject water of RO in hot seasons 
is utilized as the cooling water for the MED condenser cooling. In the 
present study, the MED condenser cooling approach has been in­
vestigated to evaluate the influence of using TCM on desalination 
economics and performance. Process characteristics of MED including 
the number of effects and condenser temperature have been assessed 
based on a dual-purpose condenser approach.

and coldest months of the year, respectively. A dual-purpose condenser

2. Thermodynamic model 
Power plant simulation is the first step to investigate the influence 
of thermal coupling on cogeneration performance. The produced steam 
flow rate is then calculated and the MED unit capacity is determined 
accordingly. As mentioned earlier, the thermal coupling based on the 
MED condenser cooling (MCC) approach is implemented in the present 
study. Hence, the RO capacity is determined so that its reject stream 
could supply the maximum cooling water required by MED. To de­
monstrate this phenomenon, two steps are followed. Preliminary, the 
desalination plant characteristics along with its economics are studied 
in a basic configuration (with a five-effect MED at a condenser tem­
perature of 48 °C) applying the thermal coupling via the MED con­
denser cooling method. To benefit the availability of cooling water 
provided by RO reject, the condenser temperature is decreased in the 
next step, so that the number of effects of MED units can be increased to 
achieve a higher gain output ratio, and consequently, a higher plant 
performance in a modified configuration. Fig. 4 represents the flow­
chart of the cogeneration of water and power plant simulation in this 
study. Techno-economic results are presented according to the basic 
and modified design configurations. 
To demonstrate the performance of the proposed model, a power 
plant consisting of two SGT600 gas turbines, each with a capacity of 
24.60 megawatts, as prime movers followed by two HRSG units and a 
3.5 bars back-pressure steam turbine was considered. Thermo-flow 
software was used to simulate the power plant. Table 1 represents the 
gas turbines technical specifications acquired from the GT PRO 20.0 
library. In the conducted simulation, the average annual ambient 
temperature and relative humidity were assumed equal to 17 °C and 
84%, respectively.

4.2. Influence of applying the thermal coupling method on desalination units 
Table 10 represents water properties, the MED utility consumption 
and water costs for MED and RO units in the CWP plant without 
thermal coupling and considering the MCC approach. As shown in  
Table 10, the specific heat of brine water is 2.5 percent lower than that 
of seawater. Regarding the technical challenges of using high salinity 
brine as a condenser coolant in the cogeneration of water and power 
plant, it is worth noting that titanium tubes with a proper thickness 
were used in both the basic design and the modified dual-purpose 
condenser with a high-grade stainless steel shell material according to 
the RO brine water. Additionally, a fouling factor of 1.5 times the 
routine design value (equal to 0.00015 m2.K/W(was assumed in the 
modified dual-purpose condenser which led to a 1.3 percent increase in 
condenser specific heat transfer surface value (from 317 to 321 m2s/ 
kg). Consequently, due to a very slight increase in MED condenser cost 
relative to its total cost, the change in the investment cost as a result of 
the change in the condenser fouling factor was ignored, although the 
tube and shell materials were included. According to Table 9, in each 
MED unit with a capacity of 250 ton/hr, seawater entering the con­
denser, cooling water and total feed water flow rate were 2075, 1240 
and 835 ton/hr, respectively. By thermal coupling of MED and RO 
based on the MCC approach, the seawater flow required in the MED 
unit is reduced to the required feed water flow rate to the effects (ac­
cording to Fig. 7). Thus, in this configuration, the seawater flow con­
sumption of 250 ton/hr MED declines from 2075 to 835 ton/hr. The 
remaining required seawater for condenser cooling is supplied by RO 
reject. In the present study, the intake unit volume has been determined 
according to the total required MED feed-water flow rate plus 25% of 
the total rejects flow of the SWRO to tolerate a 25% malfunction of RO 
trains in full capacity operation of MEDs. Accordingly, the intake vo­
lume of a MED unit with a capacity of 250 ton/hr is reduced from 2075 
to 1146.50 ton/hr. 
As presented in Table 10, in the basic configuration design con­
sidering thermal coupling method (TCM-MCC), the electrical energy 
consumption of the MED units is reduced from 3.82 to 2.11 kWh/m3, 
suggesting that a 45% reduction occurs in the electrical energy con­
sumption of the MED units. In this study, the energy consumption of the 
intake unit was assumed to be 0.46 kWh/m3. The water production cost 
of the MED and RO units determined without thermal coupling were 
1.10 and 0.58 $/m3, respectively. Applying TCM, the initial investment 
costs of the plant are slightly affected by additional control and piping 
equipment required for hybridization of RO and MED units. In the 
present study, the increase of control and piping equipment in the UIC 
of the MED unit has been ignored. 
Implementing TCM considering the MCC approach, the MED unit 
water cost is reduced by 18% due to a 45% reduction in the intake unit 
volume. In fact, the intake water cost reduces from 0.30 to 0.10 $/m3, 
leading to the reduction of MED unit product cost from 1.10 to 
0.90 $/m3.

of effects. While applying thermal coupling, an MED product water cost 
of 0.90$/m3 was estimated in the basic configuration, it is minimized 
to 0.81 $/m3 in the seven-effect MED unit. As a result, the seven-effect 
MED with a capacity of 325 ton/hr under a condenser temperature of 
45 °C can be proposed as the modified design of the MED unit. Table 12 
represents a comparison of the process characteristic parameters of the 
MED units in the basic and modified designs. 
The results show that the capacity of the MED unit increases from 
250 to 325 ton/hr by reducing the condenser temperature from 48 to 
45˚C and, accordingly, increasing the number of effects from 5 to 7. 
Considering the difference between the modified and basic design, it 
was concluded that distillate water capacity, GOR, and specific heat 
transfer surface would increase by 30%, 31% and 28%, respectively. 
Increase of 30% in the MED unit production capacity in the mod­
ified design compared to the basic design, results in the increase of the 
required seawater flow rate. Since the cooling water from RO reject is 
sufficiently available in a cogeneration process, increase in the required 
cooling water does not impose a significant influence on water cost. The 
distillate water capacity of MED and RO determined in the cogeneration 
of water and power (CWP) plant based on the modified MED were 650 
ton/hr and 1842.50 ton/hr, respectively. To provide this performance 
improvement in the configuration of the basic SWRO unit, 3 SWRO 
units each producing a water flow rate of 167.50 ton/hr were added to 
the basic CWP plant. Fig. 12 shows the configuration of the CWP plant 
in the modified design using thermal coupling (2GT/2HRSG/1ST/ 
2MED/11SWRO). 
Table 13 represents the characteristics of the CWP plant in the 
modified design. As previously described, the intake unit volume in the 
present study has been determined according to the total required MED 
feed-water flow rate as well as 25% of the total rejects flow of the 
SWRO to tolerate a 25% malfunction of RO trains in full capacity op­
eration of MEDs. According to Table 13, the MED intake volume is 
reduced from 2820 to 1411.50 ton/hr, equivalent to an almost 50% 
reduction. Consequently, the produced water cost of the modified MED 
unit decreases by 10% due to 30% and 28% increase in the desalinated 
water flow rate and GOR, respectively.

5. Conclusion 
The improvement of an MED unit performance using the thermal 
coupling based on MED condenser cooling (MCC) approach, in a co­
generation of water and power (CWP) plant was evaluated. First, a 
basic configuration of the CWP plant was considered from a techno- 
economic point of view with and without thermal coupling method 
(TCM). A small-scale combined cycle power plant (CCPP) based on 
SGT600 gas turbine and a back-pressure steam turbine as a power 
generation unit was simulated using Thermo-flow software. Results 
showed that the proposed power plant under a process pressure of 3.50 
bars, could be able to generate gross power and steam of 58 MW and 70 
ton/hr, respectively.

consumption of MED is reduced by 45% accompanied by a 67% re­
duction in the intake water cost (from 0.30 to 0.10 $/m3 according to  
Table 10)so that MED water cost is reduced from 1.10 to 0.90 $/m3. To 
benefit the availability of cooling water provided by RO reject, the 
condenser temperature is decreased in the next step, so that the number 
of effects of MED units can be increased to achieve a higher gain output 
ratio and consequently, a higher plant performance in a modified 
configuration. In the modified CWP, considering the proposed com­
bined cycle power plant (2GT/2HRSG/1ST/2MED/11SWRO), the pro­
duced water flow rates of 650 and 1842.5 ton/hr were obtained for the 
MED and RO units, respectively. The simultaneous impact of decrease 
in the condenser temperature from 48 to 45 °C and increase in the 
number of effects from 5 to 7 resulted in a significant change in the 
plant characteristics including total water production, intake volume, 
electrical energy consumption, MED performance, CapEx and OpEx. 
In the modified configuration, the total desalinated water produc­
tion and intake, as well as energy consumption increased by 35, 32% 
and 35%, respectively, compared to the basic configuration design 
(Table 14). The economic analysis of the CWP plant showed that de­
spite the increase of 9% and 14% of CapEx and OpEx in the modified 
design, the MED performance (GOR) and annual revenue of the CWP 
plant have been improved by 31% and 23%, respectively. Conse­
quently, a two-year reduction (twenty-percent reduction) in the CWP 
plant pay-back period occurred in the modified design compared to the 
basic one.
