A B S T R A C T  
Recent studies have shown indisputable evidence indicating water and energy networks are two interdependent 
cyber-physical networks and should be operated cooperatively to maximize their efficiency and resiliency. 
However, current state-estimation models of water and energy infrastructures are implemented independently in 
their respective control centers. Such independent state-estimation in energy or water network might not be able 
to detect false data injections in the other network. In this paper, an integrated state-estimation framework is 
proposed to process the consistency of the data and estimate the parameters of a combined water and energy 
network. Compared to standalone state-estimation, an integrated framework proposed in this work makes it 
more difficult for the attackers to launch stealthy false data injections, as more parameters are involved in the 
state-estimation that need to be modified for a successful attack. The findings of this research confirm that by 
accounting for interlinks between hydrological and energy systems, the security of both systems will be im­
proved. The interconnection between the state variables and measurements of the water-energy network is 
reflected in the energy consuming components of the water system, and is achieved through the measurements 
of pump’s active power consumption in the proposed integrated estimation framework. The framework includes 
Newton’s iterative method for an alternating current (AC) state-estimation procedure for the energy network. 
The state-estimation of the energy network includes the estimation of voltages using measured parameters of the 
energy network such as active/reactive power flow/injection or voltages. Moreover, a three-tier state-estimation 
process based on Newton’s iterative method for the water network is contemplated to attain nodal heads, nodal 
demands, and the flow rate of the pipes without pumps. An integrated bad data detection algorithm is also 
supplemented to the proposed framework to detect potential bad data in water distribution system or energy 
network and enhance the resilience of the integrated water energy systems.   
1. Introduction 
It is a well-established fact that water and energy sources are each 
indispensable to our modern economic systems. The water and energy 
sectors are increasingly under rapid reform to meet the three essentials 
of security of supply, sustainability, and economic efficiency. In con­
ditions where water and energy systems rely on each other, the most 
challenging problems are before engineers and policymakers (Endo 
et al., 2017; Smajgl et al., 2016; Zeng et al., 2020). However, it will be 
demonstrated in this paper that such interdependence can be used to­
wards the advantage of the two systems, to improve the accuracy of the 
state-estimation and bad data detection algorithms. 
Cybersecurity has become one of the key challenges of the water 
and energy supply infrastructures in the recent years. Security agents 
have warned both water and energy sectors of potential false data in­
jection (FDI), also known as cyber-physical attacks. For instance, in 
March and April of 2018, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) 
and Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) apprised the water utilities of 
Russian government’s efforts to manipulate critical infrastructure in­
struments involved in the water utility operations (Koehler, 2018). In 
2015, a power grid in Ukraine was taken down by an attacker, and a 
year later, Russian hackers targeted a transmission level substation 
resulting in a black out at some parts of Kiev (Knake, 2017). State- 
estimation is a technique that is used to identify potential measurement

In the water networks, the measurements often include the flow of 
the pumps and main pipes, pressure measurements collected via tele­
metry systems, the total water usage recorded by district meters, and 
some less accurate predictions of water usage at random nodes, also 
known as “pseudo-measurements” (Arsene and Gabrys, 2014). These 
measurements are collected by smart meters installed only on the pipes, 
nodes, and pumps of importance, since a typical size water distribution 
system includes hundreds to thousands of pipes and nodes and it is 
economically infeasible to measure the pressure head, demand, and 
flow rate of every single node and pipe in the system. Accordingly, 
state-estimation algorithms are developed to estimate the parameters 
that are not recorded by smart meters yet essential to the decision 
making in water distribution systems. As in water systems, state-esti­
mation is a key tool in power systems, which incorporates real and 
reactive power flows, bus power injections, and voltages phasor mea­
surements to estimate voltage magnitudes and phase angles as state 
variables (Primadianto and Lu, 2016). Due to the high importance of 
state-estimation in both water and energy networks, and the challenges 
they introduce to both systems, various state-estimation methods in 
water and energy systems are discussed in the following subsection. 
1.1. Literature review 
Several studies have proposed state-estimation models for water 
distribution networks or electrical power systems (EPS) individually. 
Prior efforts in state-estimation in water distribution system (WDS) can 
be summarized into three main techniques: least square, least absolute 
value, and min–max methods, each of which provides some advantages 
with particular errors. For instance, the least-square method is known 
to be sensitive to large errors, whereas the least absolute value and 
min–max methods can cause computational issues due to their non- 
differentiable objective functions (Tshehla et al., 2017). For instance, 
the weighted least square method was used as an implicit state-esti­
mation method for water systems with very low measurement re­
dundancy (Andersen and Powell, 2000). A study predicted nodal de­
mands by solving an objective function with constrained weighted- 
least-squares of the measured data (Tabesh et al., 2011). Others used 
the squared summation of residuals between the measured and esti­
mated data as the objective function (Rozos et al., 2010). Some studies 
used Kalman filtering technique to enhance the water quality mon­
itoring system (Krutys et al., 2019) or simulations (Arsene and Gabrys, 
2014) to solve for the unknown parameters. In another study, the Di­
vidon-Fletcher-Powell algorithm was used to estimate nodal demand in 
a WDS (Zhang et al., 2018). One of the difficulties reported in devel­
oping the state-estimation method for water systems was computational 
issues in pipes with small flow rates, in which the Jacobian matrix 
became singular (Bargiela, 1984; Arsene and Gabrys, 2014). To tackle 
this problem, other methods such as simulations were combined with 
iterative methods (Arsene and Gabrys, 2014). 
Unlike in WDS where state-estimation is considered to be a modern 
problem, in EPS, these studies date back to over forty years ago. One of 
the pioneers, Schweppe (Schweppe, 1970) used a nonlinear weighted 
least squares optimization method in their estimation. Other studies 
incorporated non-iterative approaches that considered the data from 
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems (Jiang 
et al., 2015; Dobakhshari et al., 2019). Other studies incorporated 
phasor measurement unit (PMU) and remote terminal unit (RTU) 
models into the state-estimations (Lavorato et al., 2007; Pandey et al., 
2018; Jovicic et al., 2018). A recent study developed a linear model for 
the power system state-estimation with PMU and RTU models (Wagner 
et al., 2019). 
Nevertheless, in all these studies, state-estimation has been con­
sidered independently for water or energy systems, and the interlinked 
nature of water-energy networks has been out-of-scope. The main 
challenge of independent state-estimation for water or energy network 
is that it treats the other counterpart as a black box and fails to fully 
observe the interlinked system as a whole to protect it from interrup­
tions or threats. For example, if FDIs occur in one infrastructure, they 
may not be detected in the counterpart network, causing damage to the 
core operation of the nexus. Several studies focused on modeling and 
detection of FDIs in water or energy networks. For instance, the 
Environmental Protection Agency Network (EPANET) (the name of a 
hydraulic simulation software) and a machine-learning algorithm were 
used to detect cyber-physical attacks in WDS in Ramotsoela et al., 2019. 
In another study, EPANET and mixed integer linear programming 
(MILP) were used to identify cyber attacks in a looped WDS (Housh and 
Ohar, 2018). Some studies considered gravitational-based WDSs, which 
cannot be implemented in the looped pressurized systems (Amin et al., 
2012; Amin et al., 2012). One study developed a simulation toolbox, 
i.e., epanetCPA, to emulate a physical- and cyber-components of WDSs 
(Taormina et al., 2017). However, none of these studies considered the 
energy network in the state-estimation or detection process. 
In power systems, however, bad data detection has been well stu­
died (Kosut et al., 2011; Teixeira et al., 2010; Eder-Neuhauser et al., 
2017; Ganjkhani et al., 2019). Several studies have identified cyber 
attacks with mathematical models (Liu et al., 2011; Hug and Giampapa, 
2012; Giani et al., 2013), while others have developed detection algo­
rithms by enumerating the attack vectors (Dan and Sandberg, 2010; 
Teixeira et al., 2010). However, the studies on bad data detection of 
energy systems consider WDSs as one of the loads, mixed with other 
loads, and thereby treat it as a measurement unit. As such, any FDI on 
the load terminal will be treated as false data readings on power con­
sumption of the water distribution system. However, in reality, the false 
data readings on the water distribution terminal could stem from FDIs 
in a variety of readings such as nodal pressure, tank level, or the pump’s 
speed. Accordingly, only an integrated state-estimation and bad data 
detection system can precisely detect the origin of bad data in a com­
bined water-energy network, and this motivates the contributions of 
this research. 
There has been a limited number of efforts in detecting bad data in 
integrated water-energy systems. For example, optimization methods 
were developed to enhance the resilience of an integrated water-energy 
system in (Zuloaga et al., 2019). However, these models were specifi­
cally developed for limited water and/or energy availability scenarios, 
while other potential anomalies, such as FDIs in the pressure head of 
the tank or possible attacks on flow of the pumps or reservoir, were not 
considered. Moreover, state-estimation was not considered in energy 
nor in water distribution network. Another study developed a sub­
observer-based fusion filter to detect bad data in a combined water- 
energy system inside a power plant (Khalid et al., 2019). In this system, 
the interdependence of water and energy was defined within the power 
plants in the form of cooling water basins, desalination unit, and con­
densers. Hence, the energy system and the water distribution system 
were disconnected, and water distribution system was not within the 
scope of the study. Furthermore, the energy network was limited to a 
power plant only, and the state-estimation of an energy network was 
not considered. Therefore, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, prior 
research did not focus on developing a comprehensive framework for 
state-estimation and bad data detection of integrated water-energy 
systems.

To address the limitations of the existing research including detailed 
mathematical state-estimation and bad data detection models that 
contemplate both water and energy equations, as well as avoiding the 
computational difficulties that prior research reported in terms of de­
veloping the water state-estimation due to the existence of the non­
linear equations, this paper proposes an integrated state-estimation and

2. Problem formulation 
Given the topology and parameters of the system, the state-esti­
mation requires the readings from the measurement devices (including 
phasor measurement units (PMUs), remote terminal units (RTUs), or 
smart meters) in the integrated water-energy network and is started 
once all the measurements have been received. The framework for in­
tegrated state-estimation will be implemented in supervisory control 
and data acquisition (SCADA) center of the integrated system and is one 
of the main functions of the SCADA center. The structure of the system 
(such as topology of the system, parameters of the lines, size of the 
loads and generation units) is known and cannot change during the 
estimation process. 
An overview of the proposed framework is demonstrated in Fig. 1, 
in which a 3-tier state-estimation of the water system and one-step 
estimation of energy network parameters are designed to simulta­
neously process measurement inconsistencies in the water-energy 
system. One of the challenges in developing the state-estimation 
method for water systems is the nonlinear correlations between the 
nodal pressure head, head-loss of the pipe, and pump’s head gain with 
the corresponding flow rate, expressed in Bernoulli, Hazen-Williams, 
and pump’s characteristic equations, respectively. These nonlinear 
equations generate singular Jacobian matrix, especially when flow rate 
is small in some of the pipes. To resolve this issue, previous researchers 
tried to obtain the nodal heads (associated with the nonlinear equa­
tions) from other methods such as simulations (Arsene and Gabrys, 
2014) and then combined them with iterative methods such as New­
ton–Raphson to acquire other state variables like nodal demands, which 
can be derived from the linear mass continuity theorem (14). In the 
present work, a coherent state-estimation framework for both water 
and energy systems is designed to facilitate the mergence of the water 
and energy systems state-estimation algorithms. For this reason, the 
water system state-estimation is designed in three steps of New­
ton–Raphson, each step consisting of linear equations, to obtain all state 
variables within one single consistent model without the need for 
contemplating a simulation model. First, loop corrective flows are es­
timated in the state-estimation 1 block by calculating the flow rates of 
the pipes ( Q

3.1. N-R1: Loop corrective flows as state variables 
As demonstrated in Fig. 2, the first Newton–Raphson calculations 
(N-R1) are developed to acquire loop corrective flows ( Ql), to be used 
to calculate the flows of the pipes (Qf p
, ). This is included because when 
nonlinear Hazen-Williams equation for head-loss in nodal head esti­
mation is applied, N-R method has been shown to have computational 
issues (Bargiela, 1984). In that case, the inverse of the Jacobian matrix 
approaches singularity when flows in the pipes are small. Hence, N-R1 
is incorporated to solve for loop corrective flows, representing the flow 
of each loop independent from the pipes, to compute total head loss of 
the loop. This is based on the conservation of energy theorem, which 
states that the difference between the energy head of any two points in 
a WDS is equal to the energy gained (via pump) minus the energy lost 
through head-loss between the two points (Houghtalen et al., 2016). As 
such, in a loop where the initial and final node is the same, the sum­
mation of the head-loss of the pipes belonging to the loop is equal to 
zero, unless there exists a pump in which case the summation of the 
head-losses is equal to the energy head of the pump, expressed in (17). 
Loop corrective flows estimation is essential to obtaining state variables 
of nodal heads and nodal demands because of the following. The water 
system’s state variables are functions of pipes’ flow rates through 
nonlinear Bernoulli’s Eq. (12) and linear mass continuity theorem (14), 
respectively. However, flow rate measurements are only available for 
the main arteries and the pipes with pumps, due to the economic bur­
dens that installation of smart meters on every pipe can impose on 
water systems. Herein, loop corrective flows will allow for a primary 
estimation of nodal heads, in which flow rates are replaced with loop 
corrective flows in calculations. The outcome of the N-R1 will be used

requiring drastic measures with a trivial bad data occurrence that could 
wrongfully be mistaken as critical and does not call for dire decisions. 
The SCADA on the standalone energy system might bypass bus No.3 
resulting in a blackout in loads connected to bus 3, but in the integrated

5.5. Remarks 
This subsection summarizes some of the advantages of the proposed 
integrated state-estimation framework for interdependent water and 
energy systems. The main idea behind an integrated state-estimation 
framework is to enhance the security of the system. Currently, water 
and energy networks are treated separately. For example, assume an 
energy network that is run independently. The loads in an energy 
network are normally considered as constant power loads and smart 
meters are dedicated to monitor loads in different buses (Nodes). An 
attacker can directly inject false data into load measurements without 
being detected by state-estimation. However, for a successful attack, the 
attacker needs to manipulate measurements in vicinity of targeted node 
as well (Yuan et al., 2011). In an integrated framework, an attacker 
needs to manipulate more measurements in order to have a successful 
attack. Fig. 9 has been added as an example to visualize this concept. 
Consider the left-side plot, an attacker tries to cause harm on an in­
dependent energy network by injecting false data on a load terminal. To

In addition, it was demonstrated through case studies that an in­
tegrated state-estimation framework can improve the state-estimation 
and bad data detection process in water and energy networks. Using 
available measurements in water distribution networks and existing 
Phasor Measurement Units (PMUs) in electricity distribution networks, 
the proposed framework can easily be incorporated in Supervisory 
Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) centers to run in real-time, en­
hance the resilience of water-energy networks, and detect anomalies. 
6. Conclusion 
An integrated framework was proposed in this paper to estimate the 
parameters of a combined water-energy network and detect potential 
false data injections. The main novelty of the proposed integrated state- 
estimation framework is that a central control system is able to not only 
detect bad data across the integrated water-energy system, but also 
identify the exact location of the fault event occurrence. The findings of 
the paper indicate that in the absence of such integrated detection 
framework, the power of the entire water distribution, as well as other 
residential loads, depending on the scale of the compromised bus, could 
be disconnected due to bad data detection in the power network. 
However, the proposed framework can identify the location of the 
tampered data and the component responsible for the false data injec­
tion. This hypothesis is tested with several case studies and the results 
demonstrate that a standalone energy system fails to distinguish be­
tween the false data injections on the tank’s water level and flow of 
pumps in the system, and thus the operator may decide to disconnect 
the power to the load entirely (water distribution in this case) resulting 
in a blackout in a critical infrastructure. On the other hand, the pro­
posed integrated system detects false data injections on pumps differ­
ently from those on the tanks’ water levels such that the operator could 
avoid blackouts by isolating the attacked equipment/readings, there­
fore, the resilience of the integrated water-energy network will sig­
nificantly increase using the proposed integrated framework. The out­
come of this work will help developing the future research focusing on 
various types of false data injections including cyberattacks and de­
tection of threats in an integrated water-energy network. One of the 
limitations of the proposed integrated model is that it may not be 
computationally efficient for large systems, as it would involve nu­
merous iterations. In future research, the models need to be scaled up 
and adjusted to detect stealthy false data injections in large scale water- 
energy system using big data analytic methods.
