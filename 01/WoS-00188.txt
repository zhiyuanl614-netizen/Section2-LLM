Hybrid systems in seawater desalination- practical design 
aspects, present status and development perspectives 
Heinz Ludwig 
Fichtner Water Technology, Sarweystasse 3, 70191 Stuttgart, Germany 
Tel. +49 (711) 89950 667; Fax +49 (711) 8995 459; email: ludwigh@fichtnerde 
Received 26 August 2003; accepted 1 September 2003 
Abstract 
Ever since seawater desalination has been applied on an industrial scale, and particular in the countries of the 
Arabian Gulf, the application of desalination processes in dual-purpose facilities -- water and power-- as a hybrid 
configuration has been discussed in many feasibility investigations and also planning concepts. It is above all the 
combination of reverse osmosis with thermal processes that has found increasing interest with the aim of ensuring, 
as economically as possible, uniform water supplies under the specific, greatly varying load conditions in the Gulf 
countries. Such design concepts for hybrid configurations encompass straightforward structures with a low degree 
of coupling between membrane and thermal desalination processes, but range up to very complex configurations 
with strong interconnections on both the water side and thermally, as well as with several desalination processes 
connected in series orin parallel. Classical hybrid concepts in which the permeate from anRO desalination component 
is mixed with distillate from thermal desalination have already been implemented in Sandi Arabian dual-purpose 
plants, like Jeddah and Yanbu-Medina. Although hybrid systems of greater complexity have been addressed in 
many design studies and publications, up to now none has been brought to fruition. Coming into consideration 
asthe design basis for determining the capacity shares of the various desalination processes operated in a hybrid 
configuration are: awangement of thermal cycle of the power plant component; water/power ratio of the dual- 
purpose seawater desalination and power plant; provision of undiminished water production of the desalination 
plant as electricity generation varies; provision of a specified drinking water quality with regard to composition 
and salt content; combination of all these aspects. Also gaining in importance are concerns of environmental pollution 
and sustainable development when selecting seawater desalination and power plant configurations, as well as their 
optimization when considering desalination and electricity generation as a whole. In the practical design of hybrid 
membrane and thermal systems, aspects come to light, though, that restrict linking of the two systems and joint 
utilization of facilities, as conceived in studies and conceptual design investigations. This applies both for common 
utilization of intakes and the use of heated up cooling water from thermal processes as a feed stream for the RO part 
of the desalination process. Additionally, requirements of drinking water composition, particularly chloride content, 
TDS and compliance with a specified residual content of boron, influence specifically the design of the 
membraneprocess part and its share in the total desalination capacity. Such practical aspects have greatly influenced 
thedesign and configuration of the Fujairah hybrid plant for which, from a total desalination capacity of 100 MIGD

1. Introduction 
Linked production of electricity and drinking 
water in dual-purpose seawater desalination and 
power plants today is still the foremost concept 
for winning drinking water from the sea. This tie- 
in of thermal seawater desalination processes into 
the heat cycle of a power plant is characterized, 
depending on power plant configuration, by a 
certain relationship of generated electrical power 
to water production capacity, fixed primarily by 
the power/heat ratio of the power plant. The 
quantity of water that cmn be produced from the 
heat made available by the power plant is a func- 
tion of the efficiency of heat utilization in the thermal 
desalination process. These relationships result 
from the specific design of the dual-purpose 
seawater desalination and power plant and they 
can only be varied within relatively narrow band- 
widths to avoid unfavorable impacts on the 
operating economics of the power plant. But in 
Gulf region countries in particular, electricity and 
water consumption vary greatly depending on 
time of year, with maxima for both water and 
electricity in the summer months and markedly 
reduced electricity consumption in winter, although 
water consumption does not fall to the same extent. 
The monthly system load curves for Abu Dhabi 
show, for electricity consumption in winter, a 
reduction of 30-50% as against summer (Fig. 1).

Due to these drawbacks of the classical dual- 
purpose concept, since the start of industrial 
application of seawater desalination around the 
Arabian Gulf, hybrid configuration of such plants 
has been a topic of many feasibility studies and 
planning concepts. In particular, combining 
reverse osmosis (RO) with thermal processes has 
found increasing interest to ensure, under the 
differing toad situations between summer and 
winter, uniform supply of water as economically 
as possible. 
As aprocess that consumes energy in the form 
of electricity only, it is intended that reverse 
osmosis will be increasingly applied also in winter 
for baseload supply over the medium term. But 
even under short-term peak load aspects, RO 
technology possesses advantages thanks to its 
quick start-up characteristics: a reverse osmosis 
train on standby can become operational in less 
than one hour and shut down again in even less 
time. This means it is also possible to match plant 
operating schedules to the specific electricity tariff 
situation, for example R0 can produce water for 
storage using out-of peak load electricity when 
available. However, such an operating regime 
would have to be taken into consideration when 
designing a desal plant under aspects of surplus 
RO as well as storage and water supply capacities. 
In the following, this paper will describe the 
technical parameters and criteria to be considered 
when designing a hybrid system made up of 
thermal processes and seawater reverse osmosis 
(SWRO), so as to obtain a plant configuration that 
matches as far as possible the demand profile of 
the region to be supplied with water and electricity, 
while taking due account of water quality, re- 
sources conservation and environmental impacts. 
2. Power plant thermal cycle specifics and 
power/heat ratio 
Like for a conventional dual-purpose plant, in 
a hybrid system first the power plant configuration 
has to be brought in line with the demand structure 
by specifying electricity and water generation 
capacities and their inter-relationship. In the 
following, various power plant configurations are 
considered, these being: 
• combined cycle/backpressure steam turbine 
(Variants CCPm~ and CCP~) 
• combined cycle/backpressure steam turbine 
with supplementary firing (Variants CCPSm~ 
and CCPsm~ ) 
• gas turbine/heat recovery steam generator with 
and without supplementary firing (Variants 
HRSG and HRSGs) 
• steam generator with backpressure steam 
turbine (Variants BPSTm and BPSTx ), 
These configurations differ essentially in the 
ratio of generated electricity to available heat, that 
is the heat/power or power/heat ratios (Fig. 3). 
The combined cycle exhibits the highest electricity 
generation in relationship to heat made available, 
whereas the boiler and backpressure turbine 
combination generates more heat than electricity. 
By incorporating supplementary firing it is possible 
to shift this ratio towards both higher electricity 
and heat generation for the combined cycle and 
to higher heat generation in the gas turbine/heat 
recovery steam generator system (Table 1). Elec- 
tricity generation efficiency varies for power 
station configurations common in dual-purpose

3. Power plant/desalination process optimiza- 
tion and power/water ratio 
The power/water ratio, that characterizes how 
much water can be produced by the seawater 
desalination and power plant in relationship to the 
electricity it generates, depends on the perform- 
ance ratio of the thermal desalination process that 
is coupled to the power plant (Fig. 4). Today's 
multi-stage flash (MSF) evaporators are designed 
commonly with performance ratios of 8 to 9. Also 
usually lying within the same bandwidth are 
multiple effect distillation (MED) processes, al- 
though these in the form of MED-TVC (thermal 
vapor compression) could certainly be dimensioned 
for higher performance ratios of up to 12 or higher. 
Power/water ratios of from 17 to 23 are attained 
within this performance ratio range with today's 
combined cycle systems. The lowest P/W ratio of 
between 2 and 4 is exhibited by the BPST 
configuration. Lying between these extremes are

4. Hybrid desalination configuration and 
reverse osmosis/total product capacity ratio 
Design concepts for the hybrid configuration 
encompass structures of membrane technology 
and thermal desalination processes that are 
completely segregated or with just a small degree 
of coupling, and range up to very complex con- 
figurations that are strongly intermeshed on both 
the water and thermal sides. 
Hybrid concepts in which just permeate from 
RO desalination is blended with distillate from 
thermal desalination have already been realized 
in Saudi-Arabian dual-purpose plants (Jeddah, 
Yanbu-Medina). The Fujairah seawater desalina- 
tion and power plant currently under construction 
is the biggest hybrid configuration of thermal 
processes and reverse osmosis to be implemented 
up to now. Although hybrid systems that go even 
beyond this have been the subject of many design 
studies and publications, none have yet been 
realized. 
There are various possibilities for combining 
the power plant with thermal desalination and a 
membrane process in a hybrid configuration, 
ranging from parallel operation of the SWRO with 
no connection at all to the thermal desalination 
part and power plant up to close tie-in of mem- 
brane technology to the thermal process (Fig. 6). 
Regarding seawater feed, the SWRO can 
obtain its seawater supply from a seawater intake 
used in common with the desalination and power

6. Water quality, reverse osmosis configuration 
and consequences for RO/total product 
capacity 
When selecting the SWRO share of a hybrid 
plant, however, it is not just the power/water ratio 
and energy consumption that have to be con- 
sidered. Also important is the prescribed quality 
of the generated drinking water. Requirements of 
drinking water composition, particularly chloride 
content, TDS and compliance with a stipulated 
residual boron content, influence greatly the 
design of the membrane process part and its con- 
tribution to overall plant desalination capacity. 
Frequently, when specifying drinking water 
quality for seawater desalination, the limits laid 
down by WHO or other national water standards 
are applied (Table 2). 
For the main inorganic constituents like sodium, 
chloride and sulfate, the maximum values laid down 
by WHO and in other drinking water guidelines 
are of comparable orders of magnitude. In practical 
instances, though, the parameters for these con- 
stituents should be specified depending on the 
water quality in the local drinking water system 
into which the seawater desalination plant feeds, 
and also guidelines are to be laid down to ensure 
an adequately low water corrosiveness, also when 
it is blended with the existing drinking water. In 
common TDS range in drinking water is of 200- 
500 mg/1 with a chloride content at around 50- 
60% of these values. Also to be considered in the 
SWRO plant design is that, during potabilization, 
TDS will rise by 100-150 mg/l, depending on how 
much the water must be hardened. 
However, there are substantial differences 
between the international guidelines for boron, 
with the values for human consumption stated in 
the WHO guideline of 0.5 mg/l and in national 
drinking water codes from various countries lying 
between 1 and 5 mg/1. In Canada, the maximum 
boron content for drinking water is specified as 
5 mg/l, but depending on the use to which the

8. I. Usage of joint plant facilities and higher 
temperature SWRO feed 
Under capital cost aspects, naturally it would 
be desirable to make joint use of the seawater 
intake within a hybrid system for the power plant, 
the thermal desalination process and the SWRO. 
However, a crucial aspect is reduction of suscep- 
tibility to biofouling in the membrane process if 
chlorination at the inlet to the SWRO is done 
continuously. As noted above, for the design of 
the Fujairah plant a decision was taken not to 
supply the reverse osmosis part with seawater feed 
that had been continuously chlorinated. The back- 
ground to this was experience from the Gulf region 
which showed that continuous chlorination in- 
creased the susceptibility to biofouling of RO mem- 
branes, and that it is only possible to limit this by 
intermittent, chlorination. Although in many plants 
operational experience shows that intermittent 
chlorination is essential for reliable operation of 
RO plants inthe Gulf region, it would be desirable 
to accumulate experience to a greater depth on 
how specific operating practices could so adjust 
operating conditions that it would be possible to 
supply the SWRO with heated up cooling water 
from the reject section or the condensing part of 
the thermal process. When considering the 
influence of temperature on the energy consump- 
tion of an RO plant (Fig. 29) it becomes apparent 
that there is a noticeable potential for reducing 
the energy demand of SWRO plants. 
This holds true not only for the operating 
conditions in the Gulf where energy can be saved

8. 2. Membrane development for hybrid plants 
Up to now, research and pilot plant testing for 
membranes particularly suitable for hybrid 
processes was focusing on high specific permeate 
flow with salt rejection that is more on the low 
side in comparison with normal seawater desali- 
nation membranes. But also in hybrid systems 
membranes with a high boron rejection efficiency 
are required in the first pass if the second pass 
capacity is not to become too excessive. As 
upgrading of boron rejection goes hand-in-hand 
with enhancing the total rejection rate of the 
membrane, it would be necessary to investigate 
whether it would even be worthwhile to pursue 
or alter this objective for selection and develop- 
ment of membranes for hybrid systems. 
8. 3. Hybrid process configuration optimization -- 
operation management and control 
As the design aspects for hybrid plants as 
described above show, even just from technical 
aspects, numerous optimization considerations 
and steps are needed to develop a complete, tech- 
nically mature system. This applies for both power 
plant optimization and selection of the best suited 
thermal desalination process -- MSF, MED or 
MED-TVC -- as well as configuration of the 
SWRO process. Not even included in the above 
considerations are approaches for optimizing the 
costs of systems and components for interactions 
of the power plant with the desalination processes, 
which also substantially influence the technical 
design of the processes. 
More advanced and complex hybrid systems

configurations and with a relatively straight-
