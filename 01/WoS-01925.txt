A B S T R A C T  
This study synthesizes and optimizes a polygeneration system for a dairy industry located in Northeast Brazil. 
Based on the energy demands (steam, hot water, electricity, and chilled water), a superstructure was built to 
represent all the possible processes and connections of the energy supply system, using commercially available 
equipment and locally available energy sources. The optimization model is based on mixed-integer linear pro­
gramming and its objective function is to minimize the total annual costs. The solution of the optimization 
problem determines the optimal combination between pieces of equipment, connections, and energy resources 
within the superstructure. A reference system is established, which does not include the possibilities of cogen­
eration nor biomass. The annual cost of the reference system is R$ 2,780,550/year. When the optimization model 
is freely solved, the optimal economic system yields an annual cost of R$ 2,074,793/year (25% lower). This 
reduction is reached by using diesel and natural gas cogeneration modules to substitute electricity from the grid 
at peak hours. Only 10% of the cogenerated heat is employed within the system (due to low thermal energy 
demands), and the remainder is evacuated by a cooling tower, Sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess the 
system behavior under variations of energy demands, fuel costs, and capital recovery factor, and the optimal 
economic solution presented good performance under fuel price variations, demonstrating robustness in most 
scenarios.   
1. Introduction 
The U.S. Energy Information Administration (2017) shows that the 
world energy demands in 2040 will be 28% higher than in 2015. In this 
scenario, the industrial sector represents 54% of the global energy 
consumption (Pablo-Romero et al., 2019). In Brazil, the industrial sector 
accounted for 37.5% of the electricity consumed in 2018 (Brazil, 2019). 
Within the Brazilian industrial sector, the food and beverage industry 
presents the highest rate of electricity consumption (Brazil, 2019), and 
dairy processing is considered one of the most energy-intensive sectors 
within the food industry (Ladha-Sabur et al., 2019) along with breweries 
(Diniz et al., 2021). Dairy industries require different processes 
depending on the final products (milk, cheese, butter, etc.). According to 
Tomasula et al. (2013), the energy demands for fluid-milk processing 
plants usually include electricity (to power the unit operations) and 
thermal energy (for pasteurization, cleaning, wastewater treatment, 
etc.). 
Dairy industries are good candidates for polygeneration systems 
because of their high energy demands and their need for reliable and 
flexible energy supply systems. Polygeneration is the simultaneous 
production of two or more energy services or products that use process 
integration schemes to achieve higher energy utilization (Romero et al., 
2014). Polygeneration can collaborate to reduce environmental impacts 
and the improvement of energy efficiency and economic indicators 
(Jana et al., 2017), and can combine different types of fuels and tech­
nologies. The energy sources can include fossil fuels (e.g., natural gas, 
coal, or diesel) and renewable energy sources, such as biogas, solar, 
biomass, etc. Some of the technologies widely used are gas turbines, 
internal combustion engines, fuel cells, boilers, and chillers. The proper 
selection of technologies must consider the type of fuels available, the 
application, and related costs (Agrisani et al., 2012). Considering that a 
large part of the energy consumed in the world is generated by fossil 
fuels (BP, 2020), the combination of multiple energy sources by poly­
generation is a feasible option for an energy transition towards sus­
tainable energy systems (Rong; Lahdelma, 2016). The energy efficiency 
of industrial processes can be improved by process modeling and sub­
sequent optimization, as mentioned by Gonçalves et al. (2018), who

The optimal configuration of polygeneration systems is a complex 
problem because of the wide variety of available energy conversion 
technologies, fuel sources, and fluctuations in tariffs and energy de­
mands (Delgado et al., 2018a). According to Carvalho (2011), the 
optimal design of a polygeneration system must take into account two 
important aspects (1) the system configuration (e.g., number and ca­
pacity of equipment for each technology): and (2) the operational 
planning (e.g., energy flow rates, purchase of fuels, and the operational 
state of the equipment). Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) has 
been widely applied to the synthesis of polygeneration systems (Pinto 
et al., 2020) as it establishes the optimal design of the many acceptable 
designs available for the objective function (Rao, 2019). As stated in 
Sioshansi and Conejo (2017), MILP is a particular case of linear opti­
mization problems where some or all decision variables are limited to 
admit integer values. MILP can effectively capture the complexity of 
polygeneration systems and according to Montastruc et al. (2019), 
comprehends the development of a superstructure of technologies and 
utilities, the formulation of a mathematical model that represents all 
possible options of operation by discrete variables and applies contin­
uous variables to represent economic and energy flows, and the reso­
lution of the mathematical model, from which the optimal solution is 
determined. 
The optimization of polygeneration systems aims to search for a 
design that minimizes or maximizes an objective function, which can be 
economic costs, emissions, or thermodynamic parameters (CARVALHO 
et al., 2012). The majority of optimization studies have adopted a 
financial objective function, such as net present value (Tokos et al., 
2010), annual profit (Tan et al., 2019), and yearly cost (MELO et al., 
2020). The optimization search process is bound by system constraints, 
expressed by equality and inequality mathematical restrictions. These 
constraints represent real restrictions of the system, such as equipment 
capacity. 
The optimization of polygeneration systems has been investigated 
for different applications, such as the industrial, residential, and tertiary 
sectors. Focusing on recent studies, Carvalho et al. (2019) carried out 
bicriteria optimization for an energy system installed in a hospital 
located in Northeast Brazil, and significant reductions in annual costs 
could be obtained if the yearly carbon footprint was partially compro­
mised. Pina (2019) optimized the energy system for a multi-family 
building in Zaragoza, Spain. The optimal economic solution installed 
cogeneration modules, gas boilers, heat pumps, and absorption chillers, 
besides hot and chilled water tanks. Solar thermal collectors and 
photovoltaic panels were only part of the solution when an environ­
mental viewpoint was applied. Romero et al. (2020) used MILP to search 
for cost-effective energy supply solutions for a remote off-grid under­
ground mine located in Canada. Diesel imports, wind energy, and 
hydrogen (as a storage solution for wind energy) were the utilities 
available to meet electricity, heat, and cooling demands. Eastick (2020) 
carried out a MILP-based optimization to investigate the viability of 
implementing a small modular nuclear reactor in a remote mine in 
Northern Canada; however, due to the economic characteristics of the 
energy system, nuclear energy was not part of the optimal economic 
solution. 
Although research has been carried out on the optimization of pol­
ygeneration systems for the industrial sector, there is a lack of studies in 
the food and beverage industry. Considering the great importance of the 
sector for society and its high energy demands, it is essential to inves­
tigate the benefits that an integrated energy supply system could provide 
to the sector. 
Dairy industries have been the focus of optimization studies, but the 
majority carried out optimizations for batch and production scheduling: 
for example, a two-stage stochastic MILP model was presented by Ste­
fansdottir and Grunow (2018), integrating the selection of new product 
designs and processing technologies in a supply-chain context at a 
German dairy company. Stefansdottir et al. (2018) presented a 
multi-objective optimization model (economic, environmental) to 
determine optimal production, storage, and shipment quantities at the 
tactical planning level, considering the shelf lives of different milk 
powder products. The optimal batch scheduling of a multiproduct dairy 
process was reported by Escorbet et al. (2019), using a combined opti­
mization/constraint programming approach. A MILP model developed 
by Georgiadis et al. (2019) optimized the lot-sizing and production 
scheduling problem in a real-life yogurt industry. Regarding other types 
of food and beverage industries, there are studies on the optimization of 
energy systems for a brewery (Tokos et al., 2010) and a cheese factory 
and meat processing plant (Phillip et al., 2018). 
To the best of the authors’ knowledge and after systematic reviews, 
there are no studies on the optimization of energy supply and conversion 
systems for the dairy industry, which is the knowledge gap we aim to fill. 
To this end, the objective of the study presented herein is to synthesize 
and optimize an energy system for a dairy industry located in Northeast 
Brazil. A MILP-based optimization was utilized to determine the optimal

2. Material and methods 
2.1. Energy demands 
The study object is a milk processing plant located in Jo˜ao Pessoa 
(Paraíba state, Northeast Brazil). The industry was characterized based 
on Tomasula et al. (2013), Brasil (2020), and Lawder (2012). The in­
dustrial plant operates 16 h/day on weekdays and 8 h/day on weekends 
(355 days) and produces 47,872,734 L of milk annually. The energy 
demands were established in hourly periods considering two represen­
tative days (one weekday and one weekend day) for each month over a 
year, totaling 576 hourly periods. 
According to Tomasula et al. (2013), milk processing requires elec­
tricity, steam, chilled water, and water at ambient temperature. The 
study considered the local water concessionaire to supply water at 
ambient temperature (T0). A portion of the steam originally employed in 
cleaning activities is met by hot water due to the temperature range 
between 45 ◦C and 95 ◦C. 
Based on Tomasula et al. (2013), the annual energy demands of the 
milk processing plant were determined: electricity 4903.70 MWh, steam 
0.37 MWh, hot water 0.86 MWh, and chilled water 203.50 MWh. The 
demands are related to process requirements such as pasteurization, 
standardization, cleaning, and non-process requirements (e.g., office 
space conditioning and building lighting). 
A reliable approach for the optimization of energy systems requires 
detailed knowledge of the energy demands. A study period of one year 
was considered to establish the monthly, daily, and hourly load profiles. 
The year was represented by 24 days (one working day and one weekend 
day for each month), each day being distributed into 24 hourly periods. 
Monthly energy demands were based on milk production data from 
the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (Brazil, 2020). This 
survey reports the monthly milk production per Brazilian State, and the 
monthly production share of the State of Paraíba was multiplied by the 
annual energy demand for each utility. Daily energy demands were 
obtained by considering the number of days in each month. Table 1 
depicts the daily demands for each representative day of the year. 
Regarding the hourly energy profiles, Lawder (2012) presented the 
daily load curve records of a Brazilian dairy industry. The load per­
centage of each hour was calculated and then multiplied by the daily 
energy demand of each utility. Figs. 1 and 2 show, respectively, the 
hourly energy demands of a weekday and weekend day in March, which 
is the month with the highest rate of energy consumption. 
2.2. Superstructure 
Based on the energy demands, a superstructure was built to represent 
all the possible processes and connections of the energy supply system, 
thus incorporating all features that could be part of an optimal solution. 
The optimization model will determine the best combination of tech­
nologies and connections within the superstructure. A detailed survey of 
technologies was carried out, leading to the selection of commercially 
available equipment. Technical specifications and acquisition costs were 
obtained directly with suppliers and manufacturers. Energy balances 
were developed for each piece of equipment to establish the in­
terconnections between utilities and energy conversions. The technical 
specifications for each piece of equipment are described in Appendix 1.

The Lingo software applies the branch-and-bound algorithm to solve 
the optimization problem. As stated in Sioshansi and Conejo (2017), the 
algorithm starts by solving the linear relaxation of the original 
mixed-integer linear problem. Then, the algorithm produces a sequence 
of additional optimization problems by adding constraints to the linear 
relaxation. As the algorithm solves these additional problems, it de­
termines upper and lower bounds regarding the optimal objective 
function. This process continues until a feasible solution is found with 
sufficiently close upper and lower bounds. Fig. 4 illustrates the flow 
chart of the branch-and-bound algorithm.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Optimization 
The optimization results are presented for two systems. The first is a 
reference system, for which the optimization model does not allow the 
use of cogeneration and renewable energy. Only traditional technolo­
gies, such as gas steam boiler, heat exchangers, mechanical chiller, and 
cooling tower, were taken into account. The second system, the optimal 
economic system, considers all the feasible combinations of technologies 
within the superstructure (free optimization). Table 4 shows the eco­
nomic optimization results. 
In the optimization solver, the free optimization problem had a total 
of 74,115 restrictions and 104,653 variables, being 1750 integer vari­
ables. The model performed a total of 148,852 iterations. For the 
reference system, the problem presented 74,115 restrictions and 
104,647 variables, of which 1744 are integers. 
The reference system is based on purchasing electricity from the 
local grid to meet the total demand for electricity and chilled water 
(produced by a mechanical chiller). A natural gas boiler is installed to 
generate steam, and a portion of that steam goes through a heat 
exchanger, producing hot water. Although the capital cost of this system 
is R$ 365,344, when this value is annuitized and added to operation 
costs, the final amount is much higher than for the optimal economic 
system. Fig. 5 shows the optimal system configuration within the su­
perstructure and its energy flows. 
In the optimal economic solution shown in Fig. 5, a combination of 
natural gas and diesel generators plus the purchase of electricity from 
the local grid meet the demand for electricity and the chilled water 
demand (produced by a mechanical chiller). Following the same oper­
ational strategy of the reference system, a natural gas boiler generates 
steam. The recovery of exhaust gas heat from the generators produces 
hot water. The generators (powered by diesel and natural gas) supply 
15% of the annual electricity demand, while the purchase of electricity 
from the grid is responsible for 85% of the total. Although the amount of 
cogenerated heat was 786 MWh for the optimal economic solution, only 
71 MWh is utilized within the system. The cooling tower evacuates the 
remainder. The optimal economic system entails capital costs of R$ 
1,813,631, which is five times higher than the reference system. How­
ever, this system benefits from using cogeneration modules to substitute 
electricity from the grid at peak hours and yields a much lower annual 
overall cost. 
The higher annual cost of the reference system is due to the purchase 
of electricity from the grid during on-peak hours at the cost of 1870 R 
$/MWh. The optimal economic system imported the electricity from the 
grid on off-peak hours. During on-peak hours diesel and natural gas 
cogeneration modules operate to meet the electricity demand. Through 
this operational strategy, the optimized system replaces electricity 
during on-peak hours, representing a cost seven times higher than the 
cost of off-peak hours, with the use of natural gas and diesel at 194 and 
367 R$/MWh, respectively. 
Although the optimal system presented a high initial investment in 
equipment, it showed a reduction of 25.38% in the total annual cost 
compared to the reference system. For the optimal system, the amortized 
cost of the initial investment in equipment is R$ 235,772 annually. 
There are no similar studies for comparison in the dairy industry. 
Tokos et al. (2010) presented the results of a practical application of 
MILP-based optimization to select a trigeneration system to meet the 
electricity, heat, and cooling demands of a beverage industry. The 
annual industry demands were 13.58 GWh of electricity and 35.30 GWh 
of heat. The optimal solution consisted of a cogeneration system with a 
backpressure steam turbine powered by a natural gas boiler. The 
optimal solution employed a cogeneration system with a steam turbine, 
because of the expressive heat demand (much higher than the electricity 
demand). 
The milk processing plant presented herein has a low thermal energy 
demand because of regenerators, which reduce the amount of heating 
and cooling associated with pasteurization (Tomasula et al., 2013). 
Steam, hot water, and chilled water demands represent only 4% of the 
total annual demand. As a result, although the optimal solution employs 
cogeneration, technologies strongly related to thermal energy, such as 
gas turbines (generates a significant amount of steam) and absorption 
chillers (powered by steam or hot water), are not included. In addition, 
the optimal solution installs many heat exchangers and cooling towers to 
discard the surplus hot water, which is produced by diesel and natural 
gas generators. Interestingly, despite its low tariff, the optimal solution 
did not suggest the use of biomass to meet thermal demands. Other 
optimization studies obtained similar results. The combination of energy 
demands and economic scenario did not indicate the installation of 
renewable energy equipment: Delgado et al. (2018a) optimized the 
energy supply to a Northeast Brazilian hospital (similar location to the 
dairy industry considered herein). Solar photovoltaic panels were not 
part of the global optimal (free solution), but due to the available area 
for installation, the optimal solution installed 200 panels. However, 
when greenhouse gas emissions were considered in the objective func­
tion, Carvalho et al. (2016) verified that the same hospital benefitted 
from installing solar collectors for the production of hot water. 
Another essential aspect of analyzing energy systems is reliability. 
According to Ray et al. (2018), reliable systems assure continuity of 
energy supply without failure. The optimal system presented herein 
relies on the local grid and diesel and natural gas suppliers located close 
to the industrial plant. Thus, the continuity of the electricity and fuel 
supply is guaranteed, avoiding intermittency in energy production. 
Other studies have employed generators to assure the reliability of the 
energy supply, such as Das et al. (2020), who included a diesel generator 
set in a renewable energy system to meet the energy demands of a 
remote village in India. The generator set operates when the renewable 
technologies fail to generate enough energy.

3.2.2. Price of diesel 
The diesel prices were varied within the range −20% and +20% (in 
10% steps) of the reference case price, 367 R$/MWh. Table 6 displays 
the analysis results, and the column in bold indicates the optimal eco­
nomic solution. The only modification in the system’s configuration 
occurs at the price 20% higher. A diesel generator was replaced by one 
natural gas generator and an additional hot water heat exchanger was 
installed. 
The result indicates that the optimal economic design delivers good 
performance when confronted with unpredictable diesel price changes. 
With the same system configuration, it is possible to meet the industry 
energy demands for a range of −20% and +10% of the original price. 
3.2.3. Price of natural gas 
The natural gas prices were varied within the range −20% and +20% 
(in 10% steps) of the optimal economic solution price, which is 194 R 
$/MWh. Table 7 displays the analysis results, and the column in bold 
indicates the optimal economic solution. Modifications in the system 
configuration are not necessary. The optimal economic solution meets 
the industry demands for the entire range of prices, demonstrating a 
good response of the polygeneration system to natural gas price 
uncertainties. 
3.2.4. Capital recovery factor 
The influence of the capital recovery factor (crf) was analyzed, 
varying between 0.05 and 0.25. The original value is 0.13. Table 8 shows 
the analysis results, and the column in bold indicates the optimal eco­
nomic solution. 
The capital recovery factor represents the system investment amor­
tization and is in the function of an interest rate and a given length of 
time. The crf has a relevant influence on the total annual cost. As crf 
increases, the total annual cost also increases. When crf equals 0.05, the 
annual cost dropped by 8%, and when crf equals 0.25, the annual cost 
increased 10% (both compared to the optimal economic solution). The 
annual equipment costs vary between R$ 117,730 and R$ 453,408. 
The system’s solution for 0.05 and 0.10 applies three gas generators 
and one diesel generator. In both scenarios, the system used 62% less 
diesel and 16% more natural gas than the optimal economic solution. 
For 0.20 and 0.25, the system’s configuration consists of two gas gen­
erators and one diesel generator, the same configuration of the optimal 
economic solution. 
3.2.5. Diesel price and natural gas price 
An analysis simultaneously varying the diesel and natural gas was 
carried out to assess the system performance under unpredictable price 
combinations. The optimization was performed for each pair of prices. 
For diesel, the price was varied from 260 to 420 R$/MWh (in 20 steps), 
and for natural gas, the price was varied from 140 to 280 R$/MWh (in 
20 steps). Fig. 6 depicts the results. 
For combinations with high natural gas prices (240, 260, and 280) 
and lower diesel prices (260, 280, and 300), the solution employs System 
I as it only uses diesel generators, avoiding the use of gas generators due 
to high prices. However, for combinations with lower natural gas prices 
(140, 160, and 180) and higher diesel prices (380, 400, and 420), the 
solution identifies System II, installing three gas generators and only one 
diesel generator. The optimal system (Table 4) was used in 57 combi­
nations, while system I and II were only used in six and nine combina­
tions, respectively. It is worth noting that system II, used for the lower 
values of natural gas, still uses a diesel generator to meet demand. This 
occurs because the cost of the natural gas generator is approximately 
twice the value of the diesel generator, causing a relevant cost increase.

Finally, the study area (Northeast Brazil) is a tropical location that is 
not the subject of frequent optimization studies on energy supply and 
conversion. The presence of industry, such as the dairy plant studied 
herein, has many more impacts than just economic revenue. Due to tax 
exemption and other benefits, industries can be attracted to low indus­
trialization areas. This is generally viewed without reservations as a 
desirable addition to the community, as these investments contribute to 
local income and employment. Nevertheless, industrial installations are 
frequently associated with critical social impacts that influence the 
quality of life of nearby residents (Lima and Marques, 2017). There 
could be positive impacts of these new industrial establishments due to 
their labor requirements and service needs. Still, small communities are 
usually negatively impacted by rapid increases in population and 
industrialization. 
An energy-efficient, highly integrated energy conversion and supply 
system, such as the one presented herein, can play a significant role in 
actions directed to decrease the energy intensity and emissions of the 
industrial sector. Energy integration schemes are essential for recov­
ering from the economic crisis caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and 
necessary for addressing the climate crisis. 
4. Conclusions 
This study presented the synthesis and optimization of a poly­
generation system designed to meet the energy demands of a milk 
processing plant located in Northeast Brazil. 
The optimal economic solution did not rely on biomass (sugarcane 
bagasse) and used the combination of generators (diesel and natural gas) 
with the purchase of electricity from the local grid to meet the electricity 
demand. Electricity is also used to meet the demand for cold water 
through a mechanical chiller. A natural gas boiler generates the steam, 
and the hot water is produced by cogeneration modules of the genera­
tors (during peak hours, from 5:30 p.m. to 8:30 p.m.). During off-peak 
hours the system imports electricity from the local grid. 
The optimal economic solution showed a total annual cost that is 
25.38% lower than the reference system (which does not allow cogen­
eration or the use of biomass). This relevant cost reduction occurred due 
to the self-production of electricity with three generators during peak 
hours when electricity from the local grid is seven times higher than the 
cost of off-peak hours. The generators were responsible for 15% of the 
industry’s annual demand. Regarding the initial investment in equip­
ment, the optimal solution presented a higher cost than the reference 
system. Nevertheless, capital costs were amortized at an annual rate of 
13% over approximately eight years, still achieving a significant cost 
reduction when added to variable costs. The low heat demand reduced 
the need for cogeneration and increased the number of heat exchangers 
and cooling towers. 
In the sensitivity analysis, for simultaneous variations in diesel and 
natural gas tariffs, the optimal system was sufficiently resilient and 
satisfied most scenarios without configuration changes. These results 
indicate that in an actual operating situation, where fuel prices fluctuate 
throughout the year, the optimal system will meet the industry demand 
without significant design changes and consequently without additional 
costs. 
The results provided herein demonstrate that optimization is an 
advantageous approach to design energy supply systems for milk pro­
cessing plants. Through the methodology applied, it was possible to 
synthesize an integrated and efficient energy system to meet the in­
dustry demands. As perspectives for future work within the research 
context, the implementation of additional renewable technologies and 
resources in the superstructure would further increase the relevance of 
the research, along with the consideration of multicriteria optimization 
(economic and environmental aspects).
