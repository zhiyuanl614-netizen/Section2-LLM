Eighteen years ago, in Portugal, the expenses in a water supply system associated with energy
consumption were quite low. However, with the successive crises of energy fuel and the increase of the
energy tariff as well as the water demand, the energy consumption is becoming a larger and a more
important part of the total budget of water supply pumping systems. Also, new governmental policies,
essentially in developed countries, are trying to implement renewable energies. For these reasons, a
case-study in Portugal of a water pumping system was analysed to operate connected to solar and wind
energy sources.
A stand-alone and a grid-connected systems were tested. The stand alone was compared with the
cost of extending the national electric grid. In the grid-connected system two solutions were analysed,
one with a water turbine and another without. To be able to implement a water turbine, a larger water
pump was needed to pump the necessary water as for consumption as for energy production. For the
case analysed the system without a water turbine proved to be more cost-effective because the energy
tariff is not yet so competitive as well as the cost of water turbines.

1. Introduction
The need for saving water and energy has grown as one of the
world main concerns over the last years and it will become more
and more important in the near future. New strategies must be
developed and implemented in the major energy consumption
systems (Renewable Energy in Europe, 2004). Different policies
are being implemented in EU countries; so far all seem to have the
same denominator: they account more for the environmental
view of renewable technologies rather than their economic
aspects. Despite the different measures applied in each country,
the EU has deﬁned goals in reducing pollution emissions.
Several years ago the energy consumption associated to a
pumping system was most of the time forgotten due to low energy
prices. Water losses along the pipe system and the efﬁciency of
pumps were not the main aim in a design project, as they
represented a small portion of the total budget (Ramos and Mello,
2007). Nowadays the energy price is rising constantly and water
and energy companies need to invest in more efﬁcient solutions.
On the one hand the hydraulic losses in water supply systems
are being reduced as much as possible by using smoother pipes
(i.e.,
plastic
pipes)
and
pressure
control
policies.
However,
demand is also getting higher, forcing companies to ﬁnd different
and new sources of energy. The environmental concerns regarding
pollution emissions are also growing. Some governmental policies
have been implemented, such as higher feed-in rates, to favour
small and decentralized systems. This measure is very important
due to its impact in the private sector since the interest of private
companies is highly dependent on the economic outcome of each
project.
The fast evolution of photovoltaic (PV) and the already highly
developed wind power technologies when combined with hydro
energy are making it one of the possible sustainable energy
solutions for water pumping systems, which have intensive
energy consumptions.

(when PV has the same cost of energy—cost per kW h—as the

3. Hybrid systems
A hybrid power system is a system composed of more than one
energy source. Energy systems based on only one resource have
the disadvantage of being oversized when the resource is less
available. Hybrid systems depend on more than one resource
which means when one source is not available, the other might be
making the system more reliable.
A study of a wind/solar hybrid system was done for Turkey
which attempted to prove that wind and sun can complement
each other. Table 1 shows the monthly average available solar and
wind energy in Izmir per unit of area (Ozdamar et al., 2004) and in
Fig. 3 the energy output was determined.
The uncertainty or the loss of load inherent to these sources
can be solved with a pump-storage system. The pump-storage
system seems to be, therefore, the system with the best results in
solving these problems. There are several available water supply
systems, where a great variety of possible solutions can be
applied.

4.2. Methodology
A methodology was developed to analyse the possible use of
solar and wind energy on a water pumping system. Two computer
softwares
were used:
the
Environmental Protection Agency
Network (EPANET) and the Hybrid Optimization Model for Electric
Renewables (HOMER).
EPANET performs simulations of hydraulic and water quality
behaviour within pressurized pipe networks. It tracks the ﬂow of
water in each pipe, the pressure at each node, the water levels in
each tank and the concentration of chemical species throughout
the network during an extended simulation period comprised by
multiple time steps. EPANET is an hydraulic simulation model free
on-line available, which computes hydraulic heads at junctions
and ﬂow rates in pipe elements for a ﬁxed set of reservoir and/or
tank levels and water demands in time that are updated according
to the deﬁned patterns. EPANET employs the gradient algorithm to
hydraulically balance the network. In the analysed case-study
EPANET was used to determine the hydraulic behaviour of the
hydraulic system.
HOMER simpliﬁes the task of evaluating designs of both off-
grid and grid-connected power systems for a variety of applica-
tions. The large number of technology options, the variation in
technology costs and the availability of resources make decisions
difﬁcult. The optimization algorithms and sensitivity analysis
simplify the evaluation of the system conﬁguration. Inputting
technology options, component costs and resource availability,
the optimization algorithm tests all combinations creating a list of
feasible conﬁgurations sorted by net present cost (the symmetric
value of net present value).
4.3. Base information
The system that is going to be analysed is a based-practical
village case in Portugal, where some parameters were assumed to
simplify calculations and simulations. The system characteristics
are presented in Table 2. A scheme of the outline of the system can
be seen in Fig. 4.
For this case-study, the use of a water turbine in the system
implicates more pumping ﬂow. Water is pumped during the night
(when the energy tariff is lower) in large quantities so that there is
enough water for the population and for the water turbine during
the day. The higher the ﬂow for the turbine is, the larger the pump
has to be for a constant pumping duration since the period for low
price in the energy tariff is limited.
The village has an hourly demand shown in Fig. 5. The
Portuguese legislation deﬁnes an interval of ﬂow speeds in water
supply systems between 0.6 and 1.5 m/s. In the system without a
water turbine, the pumped ﬂow is lower (the only one to supply
the population needs), presented in Table 3. The commercial
diameters of 145, 163 and 181 mm for the pipe system were
tested. For the system with a water turbine the ﬂows in the
system are much higher making it preferential to use a larger
diameter. A diameter of 326 mm was used to attempt reducing
friction losses along the pipe.
To determine the optimized system conﬁguration and costs
associated, the investment for solar and wind equipment is
presented in Table 4 (Wenham et al., 2007) and in Table 5 for the
water pump and turbine.
The costs per kW of the water pump and turbine decrease for
higher power values. For higher ﬂows the velocity in the pipes is
higher and therefore the friction losses increase, making it
necessary to study the ﬂow variation inﬂuence.

4.5. Results
4.5.1. Stand-alone system
After the input data, the computational model runs multiple
simulations to obtain the optimized system conﬁguration and
cost.
In the stand-alone system the water turbine is not used. The
pump only needs to deliver the necessary water for the population
(a ﬂow of 16.72 l/s, assuming the pump works 18 h a day,
equivalent to a pump power of 12.69 kW for an efﬁciency of
80%). The optimized PV/wind system for the average cost of PV
modules (PV multiplier equal to 1.0) and an average pump power
(a pipe diameter of 161 mm) is presented in Table 9.
It was obtained a breakeven grid distance of 31.6 km which
means that if the water pump is at a distance of more than
31.6 km from the nearest national grid point, it is more economic-
al to have a stand-alone system than to extend the national grid.
Fig. 8 displays the optimized system conﬁguration for different
PV multipliers and pump power combinations. For example, for a
pump power of 230 kWh/day (25.56 kW) and a PV cost multiplier
of 0.4 the optimized system is composed of PV modules and a
battery bank (bottom area). However if the PV multiplier is 1.0 then
the system would also be composed of wind turbines (upper area).
4.5.2. Grid-connected system
As mentioned before, two grid-connected systems were tested,
one with a hydro turbine and another without. Firstly, the system
without a water turbine will be analysed and secondly the system
with it. Table 10 shows the optimized system for average PV cost
(PV multiplier of 1.0) and a sellback rate of 0.10 h/kW h. It is
composed of a grid connection and wind turbines. The negative
values for the net present cost mean that there are more proﬁts
than losses in the project lifetime. These proﬁts are due to the sell
of excess energy to the national electric grid. The sensitivity
analysis regarding PV modules cost and sellback rate values is
presented
in
Fig.
9.
The
different
areas
indicate
different
optimized conﬁgurations for different combinations of sellback
rates and PV multipliers. Solar modules are a part of the optimized
system for lower PV multipliers (lower PV cost). For lower sellback
rates the system is only composed of a grid connection.
For the system with the hydro source, several combinations of
water pumps and water turbines were tested. Fig. 10 shows the
optimum system conﬁguration for different turbine ﬂows and
different PV multipliers, with the net present cost superimposed.
As it is possible to see in Fig. 10 for higher turbine ﬂows the
system net present cost increases, which means that, although the
water turbine is having a higher energy output, the increase in
pump power is higher too, increasing the system cost. For this
reason, the results presented will be related to the smallest water
turbine/water pump tested. Table 11 shows the optimized system
conﬁguration and costs. The results show that the system is cost-
effective for a sellback rate of 0.10 h/kW h. The sensitivity analysis
regarding the energy sellback rate and PV cost multiplier is shown
in Fig. 11 which indicates the optimized system conﬁguration
(different areas) for different sellback rate and PV multiplier
combinations.
However, comparing these results with the results from Table 10
it is possible to conclude for this case-study that the system
without a hydro turbine is more cost-effective (lower net present
cost). This shows that the increase needed for the pump surpasses
the increase in hydro output, as well as the water turbine and the
green energy sellback rate are not yet competitive enough.

4.5.3. Systems comparison
In Table 12 the main results of the tested systems are
presented. The results for the grid-connected system are shown
for different sellback rates. It is also indicated the energy cost
(i.e., cost per consumed kW h), the energy purchased to the
national grid, the breakeven grid distance (valid for the stand-
alone system) and the renewable fraction of the consumed energy.
It is possible to verify that in this case-study the more
economical system is the water pump with wind turbines.
However, this conclusion may change if some parameters change
as well. The water turbine cost could be decreased since for small
ﬂows, a pump (operating in the reverse mode) may be used as a
turbine, with considerable lower costs. Total head losses could
also be decreased if a larger diameter was chosen. This decrease
would cause a decrease in pump head, and consequently its
required power. Hence, an increase in the water turbine head and
consequently in its energy output will occur. Also, a more
competitive energy tariff in the off-peak hours would favour the
system with a water turbine.
The factors mentioned above indicate that more detailed
research developments should be developed in order to verify the
inﬂuence of all intervenient factors on the optimized system
economic and environmental outcome.

5. Conclusions
In all three systems tested (i.e., stand alone, grid connected
with and without water turbine) most of the electric production is
provided by wind turbines.
The mean wind speed values inputted for case-studies were
3.75 m/s (annual average retrieved from Meteonorm). Higher
values were expected for Lisbon (a city near the Atlantic Ocean).
The use of low wind speed values may underestimate the wind
energy available potential.
In the stand-alone system a hybrid solution was obtained, with
a breakeven grid distance of 31.6 km which is still a high value for
a country with the size of Portugal. However, if subsidies are
applicable, this value could be reduced to 10 km (e.g., for a subsidy
of 70% of the investment cost, as it happens in some countries as
in Sweden). This can be a good solution for larger countries where
the areas without electricity coverage are higher. The cost-
effectiveness of these systems increases as its load demands
decreases. Stand-alone systems present a very high value of excess
energy which could be used in a useful way (to supply a
maintenance house or some other facility nearby).
Two grid-connected systems were tested: one with a hydro-
power and another without. Without the water turbine, the pump
works 18 h a day (it does not operate when the energy tariff is
higher—6 h per day). When a water turbine is incorporated the
pump only operates during the night (when the energy tariff is
cheaper).
Both systems are cost-effective and the increase in the turbine
and pump ﬂow leads to higher friction losses since hydraulic
circuit is he same. In both cases the optimized system is not
composed of solar modules. Solar energy is still expensive when
compared to other renewable energy sources. However, since this
work was done, new legislation has been implemented in Portugal
regarding sellback rates. For small PV systems the sellback rate
may now go up to 0.65 h/kW h making PV systems more
competitive.
On the sensitivity analysis it was possible to conclude that
there are considerable gains in net present values when reducing
the head losses and/or increasing heads, as well as major
environmental gains in considering renewable sources integrated
in a guaranteed discharge ﬂow system as water supply systems
are. It is believed that these systems (i.e., of decentralized
production of energy from renewable sources) will be one of the
best solutions to overcome the world’s dependency on fossil fuels.
With the implementation of a water turbine and wind turbines,
the emissions of CO2 can be considerably reduced.
Although the technologies presented in this work are ‘‘green’’
technologies, each one of them has also environmental impacts,
whether during the constructive process or during operation. Each
government should take mitigating measures to reduce or
compensate these impacts, also as a way to promote these
technologies among the general public.
This analysis was done under Portugal’s scope. Regulations,
costs and policies considered are relative to Portugal as well as all
the factors that were taken into consideration.
