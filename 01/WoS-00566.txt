The cost of meeting increased cooling-water demands for CO2 capture
and storage utilizing non-traditional waters from geologic
saline formations
Geoffrey T. Klise & Jesse D. Roach & Peter H. Kobos &
Jason E. Heath & Karen A. Gutierrez
Abstract Deep (> ∼800m) saline water-bearing forma-
tions in the United States have substantial pore volume
that is targeted for storage of carbon dioxide (CO2) and
the associated saline water can be extracted to increase
CO2 storage efﬁciency, manage pressure build up, and
create a new water source that, once treated, can be used
for power-plant cooling or other purposes. Extraction,
treatment and disposal costs of saline formation water to
meet added water demands from CO2 capture and storage
(CCS) are discussed. This underutilized water source may
be important in meeting new water demand associated
with CCS. For a representative natural gas combined-
cycle (NGCC) power plant, simultaneous extraction of
brine from the storage formation could provide enough
water to meet all CCS-related cooling demands for 177
out of the 185 (96%) saline formations analyzed in this
study. Calculated total cost of water extraction, treatment
and disposal is less than 4.00 US Dollars (USD) m−3 for
93% of the 185 formations considered. In 90% of 185
formations, treated water costs are less than 10.00 USD
tonne−1 of CO2 injected. On average, this represents
approximately 6% of the total CO2 capture and injection
costs for the NGCC scenario.

use . Climate change . Groundwater exploration . USA
Introduction
The United States is investing in technology research to
evaluate the costs of geologic storage of CO2 from large
point-source locations to mitigate greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions. Geologic storage of CO2 is just one of many
GHG mitigation strategies under consideration. The
research discussed here focuses on the feasibility of
long-term CO2 storage within saline water-bearing for-
mations (saline formations) and simultaneous utilization
of non-fresh water from these formations to meet added
water demands associated with CO2 capture and storage
(CCS). These demands from additional cooling loads
associated with both CO2 compression and new electric
generation capacity to power the energy intensive CO2
capture process, can increase the existing power plant’s
water consumption by 50–90 % (NETL 2009). Thermo-
electric water withdrawals accounted for approximately
41 % of the total water withdrawn in the US in 2005
(Kenny et al. 2009). Consumption of water by power
plants is also expected to increase from 42 to 63 %
between 2005 and 2030, respectively (NETL 2008a), due
to increased thermoelectric power demand and a shift
from once-through cooling to open, evaporative cooling.
Adding the water needs for CCS will place additional
stress on existing supplies, especially as power plant
cooling-demand projections may place future develop-
ments of thermoelectric power at risk, even without CCS
(Tidwell et al. 2012). As a result, saline water may emerge
as an attractive alternative to mitigate the added water
demands of CCS. There are additional costs associated
with this water for pumping it to the surface and treating it
to a desired salinity, and these costs must be analyzed
within the context of the water’s ﬁnal use. For example, if
a given power plant needs additional cooling water to
meet the parasitic energy needs of CCS, and freshwater is
scarce in that location, extracting and treating saline water
may be the only viable option. There is also high value in
a dataset that characterizes subsurface conditions for an
under-utilized water source as it may be useful for other
water and energy-intensive industries interested in saline
water as an alternative to freshwater or seawater (Court et
al. 2011, 2012).

To address these concerns, the National Energy
Technology Laboratory (NETL) funded Sandia Nation-
al Laboratories (SNL) to create a national database of
saline formation parameters that are utilized in a
national CCS and saline-water-extraction modeling
framework (Kobos et al. 2011a, b, c; Heath et al.
2012). The focus of this paper is to discuss the
application of this database to (1) project the amount
of water needed, and (2) determine the cost of
treatment to meet the parasitic energy production
demands of CCS as well as in the context of the cost
to inject CO2 on a per unit basis in US Dollars (USD)
tonne−1.
If a volume of water is extracted from the formation
equal to the amount of CO2 injected, CO2 injection
efﬁciency can be increased, resulting in increased
storage capacity for a given volume of saline forma-
tion. Other beneﬁts include the reduction in formation
over-pressurization, which can reduce potential induced
seismicity, and reduce the risk of potential CO2
breakthrough via preferential pathways through the
formation caprock (Buscheck et al. 2011; Court et al.
2011; Buscheck et al. 2012). It remains a challenge,
however, to quantify the cost to deliver useable water
to the power plant, and determine if there is enough
water available to offset additional demands associated
with CCS. Ongoing characterization efforts by the US
Geological Survey (USGS) are providing estimates of
US CO2-storage potential using a probabilistic assess-
ment methodology to account for formation heteroge-
neity (Brennan et al. 2010), but not within the context
of simultaneous extraction of saline water. Researchers
have also looked at the cost and feasibility of treating
the saline water for beneﬁcial use (Aines et al. 2011;
Bourcier et al. 2011); however, there have been no
published efforts to integrate the capital costs (e.g.,
wellﬁeld construction, desalination plant and costs,
brine disposal) and operational costs (e.g., electricity,
reverse osmosis (RO) membrane replacement) together
with a probabilistic treatment of uncertainty in the
saline formation dataset for a national suite of saline
formations.

Development of conceptual model
A conceptual model is presented in Fig. 2 illustrating the
components of this complex system where the CO2 is
captured, compressed, injected and stored, with saline
water being extracted from the same formation and treated
for makeup power-plant cooling. For the analysis pre-
sented in this paper, depth zones for injection and
extraction are determined based on the probability of
ﬁnding water of a desired salinity. Speciﬁc constraints
include an injection depth of at least 762 m (2,500 ft) for
CO2 to remain in a supercritical phase and a saline water
total dissolved solids (TDS) range of 10–35 kgm−3. The
TDS range avoids utilization of lower TDS (<10 kgm−3)
water which may be protected as a potential drinking-
water source, or brine of such high TDS (>35 kgm−3) that
costs increase due to lower membrane efﬁciencies and
higher energy usage (Miller 2003).
The power plant for this scenario is a 300 megawatt
(MW) natural gas combined-cycle (NGCC) unit with a
capacity factor of 0.80 and capture of 80 % of generated
CO2. This CO2 capture imparts a parasitic energy cost that
is made up with a separate 47-MW NGCC makeup power
plant also with 80 % CO2 capture. NGCC power plants
represent the base case given the current trend towards
lower carbon emitting power sources as well as potential
cost savings (Williams et al. 2011). The representative
power plant’s parameters are presented in Table 1 and
illustrate the CCS speciﬁc water demands for CO2
capture, compression and makeup power-plant cooling.
The water withdrawal and consumption values do not
include the existing water demands prior to implementing
CCS. The unit power plant with CCS results in a daily
water withdrawal demand of 4,163 m3day−1, which is a
22 % increase from the demand at the NGCC power plant
without CCS. A water extraction to CO2 injection ratio of
1.2 is used to look at relative water availability for a high
extraction case where demand for the non-traditional
water source is high. Formation speciﬁc extraction to
injection ratios will depend on factors such as subsurface
heterogeneity (Buscheck et al. 2012), caprock integrity,
value of pore space, and availability of freshwater. Absent
this sort of site speciﬁc information across the 325
formations which make up the database; however, this
research focuses on a single ratio that represents an
optimistic scenario for addressing power plant needs.
Additional detail on the CCS application of the modeling
framework can be found in Kobos et al. (2011a, b, c) and
Heath et al. (2012).
Development of the extended database
The extended database is intended for screening-level
analysis due to the large number of formations being
analyzed. At this level, the analysis allows for regional
comparisons of different CO2 injection and saline-water
extraction scenarios, and the resulting economic tradeoffs
necessary for understanding the locations that may be best
suited for CCS (Dooley 2011).
Subsurface investigation of saline formations is impor-
tant due to speciﬁc depth requirements. Deep formations
(> ∼800–1,000 m; Haszeldine 2009; Orr 2009) are
targeted to maintain CO2 in a supercritical liquid phase,
to minimize buoyancy due to density differences between
formation brine and the CO2, and to avoid communication
of the CO2 plume with underground sources of drinking
water or the atmosphere (Wilson et al. 2007). Geologic
data are available from shallow wells primarily used for
drinking water, or deep wells used for oil and gas
exploration and extraction. Geologic data are more limited
for the saline water-bearing sedimentary rocks that are
often found between these depths, and which are believed
to contain substantial pore space and thus a high potential

Early national-level storage assessments for CO2
storage are described by Bergman and Winter (1995),
followed by more detailed subsurface research conducted
at the Gulf Coast Carbon Center (GCCC; Hovorka 1999;
Hovorka et al. 2000). Subsurface information made
available through drilling programs and collaboration with
the oil and gas industry added more detail to the national
level understanding of potential saline formations avail-
able for large-scale CO2 storage. The National Energy
Technology Laboratory (NETL) is leading current char-
acterization efforts using a network of seven regional
partnerships to develop a database of locations and CO2-
storage estimates for various subsurface environments,
including saline formations (Goodman et al. 2011). These
data are described in the 2008 NatCarb Atlas (NETL
2008b; Carr et al. 2009). The purpose of NETL’s NatCarb
Altas is to convey to the public the best locations for
small-scale “validation phase” and ultimately large-scale
“development phase” studies (Litynski et al. 2009).
In order to develop a national analysis framework for
CO2 storage and saline-water extraction, the 2008 Nat-
Carb Atlas is used as a starting point to determine both the
number and spatial extent of potential saline formations
and CO2-storage estimates. The supporting data used to
calculate the CO2-storage estimates in the 2008 Atlas were
made available to SNL from the seven regional partner-
ships and is one of many data sources used in this
analysis. For the purposes of this paper, the term ‘2008
NatCarb database’ means the publically available data
from the seven regional partnerships for both saline
formations and basins. It is important to note that those
data are incomplete with respect to formation depths,
thicknesses, depositional environments, permeability, po-
rosity and water salinity at speciﬁc depths. In short, the
national-scale quantitative systems-level analysis that is
done within the WECSsim modeling framework relies on
multiple data sources outside of data available from the
2008 NatCarb Atlas and database.
As stated by Goodman et al. (2011), referring to the
NatCarb Atlas and supporting data (2008 NatCarb
database), any data collected using the US-Department
of Energy (DOE) CO2-storage methodology at a national
level has a ‘high degree of uncertainty.’ Many of the
parameter values in the 2008 NatCarb database represent
single average values without standard deviation or
probability distribution functions (pdfs), and do not
account for the complex geologic structure and ﬂow
properties that are found in the saline formations. Despite
this uncertainty, there is much insight to be gained from
data collected by the regional partnerships, which is
incorporated into the WECSsim modeling framework
with the goal of advancing an understanding of the
geologic and economic challenges surrounding CCS
(Dooley 2011). Methods utilized to address the incomplete
datasets include formally accounting for heterogeneity by
using probabilistic distributions of formation properties as
a function of geologic setting, and determining the
probability of drilling a well of a given water quality
based on existing well records.
To conduct an analysis on the national potential of
saline formations as both CO2-storage targets and sources
of non-traditional water supplies, existing data were
reviewed and statistical methods were developed to create
an extended saline formation database for integration
within the WECSsim modeling framework. Table 2
presents the parameters of interest within the extended

description of how each one is used within the modeling
framework. Many of these parameters are used to estimate
the rates of CO2 injection and saline-water extraction. For
the purposes of this modeling framework, the word
formation is used to describe both formations and basins.
A review of the existing saline formation and basin
data from the 2008 NatCarb database and the GCCC
revealed substantial data gaps in the available geologic

formation depth, thickness, porosity, permeability, and
water quality were compiled from many different sources

data compiled from oil and gas exploration and CO2-
storage studies. In order to address data uncertainty, a
statistical approach was used to determine probabilistic

Determining formation area, depth, thickness
and salinity
The ﬁrst step in developing the saline formation database
consisted of delineating the projected map-view surface
area of the desired saline formations within the contiguous
US. Using the spatial representations of saline formations
from the 2008 NatCarb Atlas (supplemental geospatial
data) (NETL 2008b) as a starting point, over 10,000
individual features were analyzed to create a database of
325 saline formation ‘polygons.’ In many cases, the
polygon representation of a saline formation was merged
due to previous divisions based on state lines. Other
polygons were merged from hundreds of small features
into one feature. As can be seen in Fig. 1, there are many
areally overlapping formations due to different depths of
the formations, with some formations located on the
continental shelf. Political boundaries mirrored for some
saline formations shown in Fig. 1 are a result of the
division of deﬁned study areas into the seven regional
partnerships and/or the lack of data to determine an exact
formation boundary. In some cases, information is
presented at the basin scale, because more detailed
formation-speciﬁc information within that basin is not
available.
The value for the depth to the formation is the average
depth below ground surface (bgs) to the top of the named
formation (or basin in cases where the partnerships only
deﬁned basins). The value for thickness is the average
formation thickness extending from the top of the
formation to the bottom of the formation (or basin in
cases where the partnerships only deﬁned basins). Using
the average value for the formation depth and thickness
will introduce model uncertainty as spatially varying
information is not available for all formations and the
national modeling framework is set up computationally
for an average value or user override for a speciﬁc
formation.
Where depth or thickness data are not available from
the 2008 NatCarb database or the GCCC, the Kansas
Geological Survey (KGS) brine well database (M. Moore,
Kansas Geological Survey, personal communication and
unpublished data, 2010) is used with potentially intersect-
ing well analysis. Potentially intersecting wells are deﬁned
as wells whose wellhead overlies a given formation. All
KGS wells with a latitude and longitude within the
footprint of one of the 325 formations were utilized. A
down-selection of wells was performed for 42 of the 325
formations that had potentially intersecting wells, but did
not have depth or thickness values reported in the 2008
NatCarb database. A literature search of general USGS
reports and petroleum hydrocarbon ﬁeld studies (dis-
cussed in the following) and analysis of reported
formation age in the well record’s screened interval was
compared with the age of the formation reported in the
database. This detailed analysis was used to narrow down
the potentially intersecting wells to a set of wells that were
likely to be associated with that formation. Next, the well
records were further limited to cases in which the screened
interval was deeper than 762 m. The depth and thickness
of the screened interval of all remaining wells was used to
create a distribution of depths and thicknesses utilized in
cases where no other depth or thickness information is
available. The down selecting of potentially intersecting
wells to wells likely to be associated with a given
formation was time consuming and found to be of
marginal value; thus, for formations with potentially
intersecting wells, a depth and thickness distribution was
calculated using data from all potentially intersecting
wells. From the potentially intersecting well analysis,

Porosity and permeability
The key parameters used to determine or describe overall
pore space and potential CO2 injection and/or brine
extraction rates for a formation are porosity (the void
space per total rock volume) and permeability (the ease at
which ﬂuids can be transmitted through porous rock).
Within the analysis framework, porosity and perme-
ability are used to calculate CO2-storage capacity and
injectivity, respectively, with the volume of water
removed from the formation related to the percentage
of CO2 stored (extraction ratio).
Permeability is not available in the NatCarb 2008
database, though there is porosity data for about half of
the formations in the dataset and porosity data from the
GCCC for 13 out of 325 formations. All of these values
represent an average porosity for the entire formation.
Having permeability information is important in estimat-
ing the potential rates of CO2 injection or saline-water
extraction per well, and thus the total number of wells
required. Porosity and permeability were estimated using a
statistical technique for all saline formations.
Porosity and permeability from probability distributions
Formations are characterized according to depositional
environment as a means of grouping formations of similar
geohydrologic properties. A single formation, however,
may be comprised of multiple lithologies (i.e., sandstone,
mudstone, carbonate, etc.). Therefore, each formation
does not always represent a single lithologic rock type,
and porosity and permeability values can vary substan-
tially within the formation. To deal with multiple
lithologies, the formations were assigned to a limited set
of rock types having well-deﬁned porosity and permeabil-
ity pdfs and spatial structure properties (i.e., nugget, sill,
and ranges of a semivariogram). Four general rock types
were chosen to represent the lithologic information:
– Clean sandstone
– Dirty sandstone
– Carbonate
– Gulf Coast
The rock type percentages were obtained for forma-
tions based on expert knowledge and literature revealing
general lithologic descriptions, and for further clariﬁca-
tion, the Gulf Coast rock type is representative of areas of
cyclic deposition where there has been both marine
transgressive and nonmarine transgressive deposition for
the formation. (Domenico and Schwartz 1998; Freeze and
Cherry 1979; GCCC 2003; Hjellming 1993; Mancini et al.
1987; Thomson 1982; USGS 2011).
Many of the formations cover a large region and
represent a number of depositional environments over an
extensive period of time. Accordingly, the formations may
be considered a combination of multiple rock types, which
is captured by estimating the relative percentage of the
rock types (i.e., 75 % clean sandstone; 25 % carbonate).
The lithologic description is based on general estimation
from available qualitative or quantitative descriptions of
the formations, and not from examining well logs or other
quantitative data.
Information on lithology, porosity and permeability
ranges of any formations for which it was available, and
supplemental rock type data are used to construct the
quantitative rock type deﬁnitions in terms of permeability
and porosity presented in Table 3. Permeability and
porosity ranges are converted to pdfs to estimate ﬂow
rates using the following assumptions:
– Arithmetic porosity values are assumed to be normally
distributed.
– The log10 transformation of permeability values is
assumed to be normally distributed.
– The data ranges for porosity and log10 permeability are
assumed to fall near the upper and lower tails of the
normal distributions.
As indicated by previous studies of geologic formations,
permeability data are typically log10 normal (Hoeksema and
Kitanidis 1985) and the minimum and maximum values of
the ranges lie near the tails of the normal distributions
(Fig. 5). The mean of each pdf is assumed to be the average
value of the minimum and maximum values of the ranges of
porosity and log10 permeability, respectively, where the
range refers to the minimum and maximum values, not the
range of the semivariogram. It is also assumed that the
minimum and maximum values of the ranges of porosity and
log10 permeability fall two to three standard deviations away
from the mean of the range. The permeability and porosity
described here as implemented in the modeling framework is
described in more detail by Heath et al. (2012).

Salinity combined with well-drilling probabilities
For this analysis, the percentage of well records in a given
depth interval with salinity values within a desired range
(calculated from the formation depth, thickness and
salinity) is expressed as the probability of drilling a
useable extraction well in that interval. Costs to produce
and treat this deep saline water are calculated as a function
of both the odds of drilling a useable well (as represented
by the average salinity of well records within the desired
salinity range) and the cost to treat the water. More detail
on the methodology used to determine these probabilities
from the data presented in the preceding can be found in
Kobos et al. (2010), (2011b, c, d), Heath et al. (2012).
For example, a certain formation may have a low
average salinity, but a wide distribution of salinities. The
low average will reduce treatment costs, but the wide
distribution will decrease the chances of drilling a useable
well, and thus increase drilling costs. Conversely, a
formation with high salinity and a tight distribution may
have higher treatment costs, but lower drilling costs. In
these cases, the relative magnitude of up front drilling
costs compared to annual treatment costs will determine
economic preference. The Woodbine-Paluxy formation in
east Texas as shown in the outlined area in Fig. 6, and
Fig. 8 gives an example of comparing average acceptable
salinity and probability of drilling an acceptable well
across the three depth ranges. As seen in Table 4, drilling
wells in the ﬁrst depth range will result in the lowest cost
due to low average salinity for well records within the
target range, and high probability of drilling a well that
produced water in this range. Well record data suggests
that drilling a well deeper into this formation would result
in higher water treatment and drilling costs resulting from
drilling more and deeper wells. The shallowest interval
will result in the lowest drilling and treatment costs due to
the high probability of accessing lower salinity waters.
To visualize this across formations with sufﬁcient data,
Fig. 8 presents for each formation, the average salinity of

quality range on the X-axis. For example, in the ﬁrst depth

Water treatment and disposal costs
Extracted water is treated using RO, using methods and
assumptions described in more detail in Kobos et al.
(2011a) and Altman et al. (2011). The salinity of the water
determines membrane efﬁciency and electricity costs
(USBOR 2003). For this analysis, electricity costs for
the makeup power are held constant in 2010 US dollars
around 0.10 USD kW−1h−1. The membrane efﬁciency
used for this analysis includes a range of around 75 %
recovery for water up to 20 kgm−3, 65–70 % for water
near 20 kgm−3 and 40–50 % for water between 25 and
30 kgm−3. With the current water extraction to CO2-
injection ratio of 1.2, and injection of the waste brine,
there are only 8 out of 185 formations (4 %) where treated
water volumes are insufﬁcient (shown in red; Fig. 10a) to
meet the additional CCS related water demands, while 177
(96 %) formations would meet that demand, with some
formations exceeding that demand (shown in blue;
Fig. 10a). For this analysis, the treatment costs include
treatment of all water, even in cases where extracted water
exceeds demand at the NGCC power plant.
Where demand cannot be met, it is a result of high
formation pressure (deep or offshore formations) resulting
in dense stored CO2 and thus reduced water extraction, or
high salinity water that cannot be treated very efﬁciently
or some combination of the two. Increasing the extraction
ratio in some locations would make more water available;
however, the practicality of high extraction ratios is
beyond the scope of this paper. The cost to treat that
water, for 174 out of 185 formations (94 %), is below 2.00
USD m−3, with the remainder between 2.00 and 3.00 USD
m−3. These treatment costs for approximately 50 % of the

these estimates. Considering CO2 injection, these costs are
in the range of 1.90–9.00 USD tonne−1 CO2 injected, with
84 % of the 185 formations below 4.00 USD tonne−1 CO2
injected. More recent work by Sullivan et al. (2012),

Summary and conclusions
This paper presents the application of an extended
database of parameters associated with deep saline
formations in the United States used to analyze the
extraction and treatment of formation saline water to meet
added water demands associated with CCS. The analysis
suggests that water could be extracted from 96 % of the
formations to meet the cooling demands of an 80 %
carbon capture and storage scenario for a 300-MW NGCC
power plant and the additional 47-MW NGCC parasitic
power plant. For the NGCC power plant CCS scenario
considered here, of 185 potential storage and water
producing formations, only 8 would not produce sufﬁcient
treated water to meet the added CCS related water
demands, even at an aggressive extraction ratio of 1.2.
Thus, CCS even on a relatively water efﬁcient NGCC
plant with an evaporatively cooled NGCC make-up

options in addition to the brine extraction and treatment
with reverse osmosis that was the focus here. Such an
analysis would extend the insights of this paper in order to
begin to understand the mix of technologies that might be
brought to bear to most intelligently offset water-demand
related impacts of large scale CCS at a national scale.
Efforts were made to determine all parameters neces-
sary for a full analysis of all 325 saline formations derived
from the NatCarb Atlas (2008b) supplemental data,
NatCarb 2008 database, GCCC data and site-speciﬁc
literature searches. However, not all parameters are
available for all locations primarily due to the limited
geographic distribution of wells available in the KGS
saline well database. Further research is necessary to ﬁll in
this data gap, through site-speciﬁc analysis, or an
application of results due to be released by the two USGS
efforts that may improve our understanding of subsurface
conditions (SECURE Water Act 2009; Brennan et al.
2010). The cost analysis to use saline water within a CCS
system builds on a subset of the extended database
compiled by SNL and is presented to showcase the
analysis framework in terms of taking an underutilized
water resource and treating it for a beneﬁcial use.
