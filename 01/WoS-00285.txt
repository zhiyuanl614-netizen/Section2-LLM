Myth and reality of the hybrid desalination process*
Ismat Kamal
Fluor Enterprises, Inc., One Fluor Daniel Drive, Aliso Viejo, CA 92698, USA
Tel. +1 (949) 547-0297; email: ismatk@gmail.com
Received 8 November 2007; Accepted 14 November 2007
Abstract
The paper discusses some misconceptions that have contributed to the continued use of thermal desalination
processes and promotion of the hybrid desalination process for new plants being built or considered at Middle East
locations. The misconceptions are examined both on the basis of fundamental thermodynamic principles and in terms
of practical engineering parameters. The analysis shows that there is no economic or performance advantage in the
installation of greenfield hybrid power/thermal desalination/ seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO) plants in preference
to power/SWRO plants, because the latter would produce water more cheaply under all conditions and at all fuel
costs, and would provide more operational flexibility than the former. The paper identifies situations where the hybrid
desalination process can be fully justified: in existing power/desalination plants, where aging boilers and multistage
flash (MSF) units need to be repaired or replaced, through retrofitting and repowering. In such situations,
abandonment of the MSF process would result in a reduction in the power output of the plant. The paper refers to
previous work which showed that the repowering of a typical existing power/desalination station with refurbishment/
replacement of the MSF units, together with the addition of SWRO units, would result in a several-fold increase in
the water and power output and a dramatic improvement in the fuel efficiency, without any additions to the existing
seawater intake system. The paper emphasizes the importance of test stations/demonstration plants at existing
power/desalination stations in the Middle East in order to obtain data and make improvements in the technology of
higher temperature SWRO, with the feed obtained from the cooling water returning from the power plant condenser
and the thermal desalination plant. The paper shows that the potential benefits would easily justify the investment
in research and development required to validate this concept.
Keywords:
Hybrid desalination process; Power/desalination plants; Condensor cooling water return as feed for
SWRO; Energy usage in desalination; Plant simulation; Energy-use comparison; High temperature
SWRO

1. Introduction
The hybrid thermal/SWRO desalination pro-
cess was proposed by a number of different
sources in the late 1980s (see, for example, [1]),
at a time when SWRO technology was still in its
infancy, and at best produced water at approxi-
mately the same cost as thermal processes.
However, owing to dramatic improvements in
technology and reductions in membrane costs (in
terms of inflation-adjusted dollars), SWRO can
currently produce water at about half the cost
obtainable from its thermal counterparts. It has
become the process of choice for seawater
desalination plants being built in Europe, the
Americas and the Far East. On the other hand,
thermal desalination processes continue to be
popular in the Middle East, except for the state of
Israel. Since the Middle East remains overwhelm-
ingly the core area of activity in the desalination
field, preference for thermal processes has
indirectly impeded the growth and adaptation of
SWRO technology. We seem to be in a vicious
circle, where not many SWRO plants are being
built because there is not enough operating
experience, and there is not enough operating
experience because not many SWRO plants are
being built!
2. The myth
Apart from genuine concerns on reliability
with SWRO plants in the Middle East, certain
misconceptions regarding energy costs of desali-
nation have led to the continued use of thermal
desalination processes and the concept of hybrid
desalination for new projects. Some of the
misconceptions are as follows:
C
Steam withdrawn from the low pressure
sections of a steam turbine is cheap.
C
When energy costs are low, thermal processes
are more economical than SWRO.
C
Hybrid processes provide greater flexibility in
power/desalination plant operation.
Each of these misconceptions is discussed in
the following sections.
2.1. Cost of low pressure steam
Steam withdrawn from the low pressure sec-
tions of a steam turbine is not cheap. A large
portion of the power output in steam turbines is
produced in the low pressure sections. The
withdrawal of low-pressure steam for process use
imposes a substantial penalty on the power output
of the steam turbine. To illustrate this point,
STEAMPRO® software [2] was used to evaluate
the difference in the power output for non-reheat,
non-extraction steam turbines with condensation
at 1.5 psia and with back-pressure steam with-
drawal at 35 psia (a typical value for low-pressure
steam supply to an MSF plant). STEAMPRO®
runs were made for two different turbines, with
nominal capacities of 100 MWe and 50 MWe and
throttle conditions of 1465 psia/1000EF and
650 psia/750EF, respectively. The results are
plotted on a Mollier chart in Fig. 1, and show that
expansion through the last 33.5 psia produces
35% of the useful work or power output for the
100 MWe turbine, and 43% of the useful work
for the 50 MWe turbine. Sometimes there is a
misconception that steam extracted from power-
producing turbines is cheap because it has been
spent and is “low-grade”. It may be low grade in
terms of is pressure, but is by no means low-
grade when we consider the power it could have
produced!
2.2. Energy consumption of desalination processes
The comparative energy consumption of sea-
water desalination processes has been discussed
in a number of previous papers [3–7]. Most of
these comparisons are based on the “power-loss”
method, a concept first introduced in a paper
presented at the 3rd International Symposium on
Fresh Water from the Sea at Dubrovnik in 1970
[8].

The theoretical energy requirement of an
ideal, reversible seawater desalination process
with the brine concentration maintained at about
twice that of the seawater itself is 5.6 kJ/kg.
Taking the irreversibilities of a real desalination
process into account, Professor Silver had shown
in a series of class room lectures during the late
1960s that the energy requirement was 240–
280 kJ/kg for single-purpose thermal desalination
processes and 45 kJ/kg for reverse osmosis. He
had also shown that, for a dual-purpose power/
thermal desalination processes, the energy

requirement is reduced to 96 kJ/kg, which is
equivalent to about 100 kWh/1000 (US) gallons
in terms of the “power-loss” method [9].
For reverse osmosis, Professor Silver had used
a high-pressure pump efficiency of 75% and a
fuel-to-power conversion efficiency of 33%. With
improvements in technology, the efficiency of
high pressure pumps in large-sized SWRO plants
units can be as high as 87%, and the fuel-to-
power conversion efficiency in a combined-cycle
power plant based on advanced gas turbines can
be about 54%, even at high ambient temperatures
typical for the Middle East (as we shall see later
in this section). With these considerations, the
minimum energy requirement for SWRO is
reduced to 23.3 kJ/kg. Professor Silver’s analysis
also did not include energy recovery which, with
a typical Pelton wheel type recovery device, can
reduce the power requirement by about one-third,
resulting in an energy consumption of 15.5 kJ/kg.
This is equivalent to 16 kWh/1000 (US) gallons,
in comparison to 100 kWh/1000 (US) gallons for
a thermal desalination process. The energy
requirement for SWRO can be further reduced
with the pressure-exchanger and work-exchanger
type of recovery devices, now increasing in
popularity.
For the current study, the energy requirement
was evaluated for MSF, thermal vapor com-
pression multiple-effect distillation (TVCMED)
and SWRO utilizing GTPRO® [2], a software
obtained under license from Thermoflow, Inc. to
simulate the design of power/seawater desali-
nation plants. The power plant simulation is
based on 12 natural-gas fuelled GE 7241FA com-
bustion turbine generators (CTGs) associated
with four steam turbine generators arranged in a
four-train, three-on-one configuration. The choice
of CTGs is for purposes of illustration only, and
does not imply any preference on the part of
Fluor. Similar results would be obtained with
other advanced CTGs. The same fuel input
(1,446 MMBtu/h for each CTG) was used for all
four cases. The gross power output of the base
plant (Fig. 2) is approximately 2,800 MWe.
Figs. 2–6 show the graphic outputs for the power
cycle as well as for the thermal desalination
plants (MSF and TVCMED). The results are
tabulated in Table 1, along with results for
reverse osmosis. 
It is remarkable that the results extrapolated
from Professor Silver’s predictions in the late
1960s are so close to the ones obtained in the
current study using sophisticated simulation tools.
The energy cost for thermal desalination pro-
cesses is several times higher than the cost for
SWRO, irrespective of the price of energy. It is
true that the cost differential decreases as the
price of energy goes down, but current trends on
fuel prices are definitely going in the opposite
direction.
2.3. What flexibility?
Proponents of the hybrid desalination process
for greenfield plants sometimes claim that it
provides greater flexibility. A problem does exist
in the master-slave relationship of a dual-purpose
power/desalination plant based on thermal desali-
nation. Some of us would recall how, during the
late 1970s and early 1980s, street lamps in
Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, were on throughout the day
because the power plant had to be kept going in
order to provide the steam required for desali-
nation. The flexibility advantage is true only if
there is an existing thermal desalination plant to
which SWRO can be added, to help with the low
flexibility of dual-purpose power/thermal desali-
nation plants. For new dual-purpose plants,
SWRO alone would provide all the flexibility one
would ever need. The reason is that, typically,
most combustion turbines can be turned down to
50% (and lower if environmental emission rules
are relaxed), and most steam-turbine based power
plants can be turned down to 25%. The turn-
down is greatly enhanced in a plant with multiple
power trains, where all but one train could be shut
down. In the example for SWRO shown in

3. The Reality
The considerations discussed in Section 2.3
clearly point to the need for adding SWRO to
existing power/thermal desalination plants. This
is true not only in order to enhance plant
flexibility, but also to improve overall plant
performance.
There was a spate of construction activities in
the area of dual-purpose power/desalination
plants in the Middle East during the late 1970s
and early 1980s [10]. Most of these plants were
based on conventional boilers with steam turbines
for power generation and MSF distillation for
seawater desalination. While the steam turbines
and some of the desalination units are still
performing satisfactorily, many of the boilers
associated with these plants are close to the end
of their useful life. At the same time, increasing
demand calls for substantial additions to the
power generation and water desalination capacity
of the region.
High-efficiency combined cycle plants based
on combustion turbines with heat recovery steam
generators and steam turbines could provide an
excellent means of obtaining a dramatic increase
in the gross power plant capacity and the fuel
efficiency through repowering. At the same time,

the thermal desalination plants could be refur-
bished or replaced with new units and reverse
osmosis, with feed drawn from the cooling water
returning from the power plant condenser and the
heat rejection section of the thermal desalination
plant, could be used to supplement the MSF units
of these plants, greatly increasing the plant
capacity and efficiency.
It could be argued that it would be more
profitable to replace the existing MSF units with
reverse osmosis units. However, it has been
shown in previous papers [11,13] that, because of
the design of the existing steam turbines based on
back-pressure or extraction schemes, there would
be a drastic reduction in the power output if the
thermal desalination plant were completely
removed.
In case studies based on a capacity of
240 MWe/88,000 m3/d (approximately 20 MIGD)
for an existing plant [11–13], it has been shown
that:
C
the net power output can be increased nearly
three times to approximately 640 MWe,
C
the water output can be increased more than
six times to 588,000 m3/d (approximately
130 MIGD) without any addition to the exist-
ing seawater intake system,
C
the net fuel efficiency of power production
can be increased from 27.7% for the existing
plant to 47.2%, and
C
attractive rates of return can be obtained if the
refurbishment is carried out by an independent
water and power producer (IWPP) on a build,
own and operate basis.
It should be noted that Kamal and Tusel [11]
was based on heavy-duty 7EA combustion tur-
bines and resulted in lower efficiencies, 42% with
treated crude oil fuel (to reflect fuel availability
on the West Coast of Saudi Arabia at that time)
and 43% with natural gas fuel.
4. The Need
Unlike the cool seawater temperatures off the
Pacific coast, for which the re-use of power plant
condenser cooling water return as feed for SWRO
was first suggested in a paper presented at a
National Water Supply Improvement Association
(NWSIA) conference in Newport Beach,
California [14], seawater temperatures prevailing
in the Middle East would present specific chal-
lenges when used in the manner proposed in this
paper. Although membranes stable at tempera-
tures of up to 50EC are available, there is a need
for pilot plant testing in order to evaluate long-
term performance at elevated temperatures, and to
incorporate improvements in technology based on
feed back from the test results. There is a need to
establish test facilities and demonstration plants
at some of the major power/desalination stations
in the Middle East. The following locations come
to mind:
C
Saudi Arabia, East Coast
C
Saudi Arabia, West Coast
C
Gulf Region — Dubai/Abu Dhabi
C
Oman, possibly under the auspices of the
Middle East Desalination Research Center
(MEDRC)
The research and development effort could
include demonstrations of recent innovations in
energy recovery devices and pretreatment
methods. The potential benefits would easily
justify the investment in the research and
development work. Since the Middle East is the
hub of seawater desalination activities, the desali-
nation research and development authorities of
the countries in the Middle East need to be at the
forefront of this effort. The improvements in
technology would bring us nearer to the goal of
making seawater desalination affordable for
countries of the third world, which are facing
severe water crises.
5. Conclusions
C
There is no technical, economic or operational
justification for new hybrid power/thermal
desalination/SWRO plants.

C
There are significant advantages in repower-
ing and refurbishing existing power/thermal
desalination plants with the addition of SWRO
in a hybrid arrangement. 
C
One of the greatest potentials for SWRO is in
the re-use of power plant condenser cooling
water and thermal desalination seawater return
as feed for SWRO plants in the Middle East.
C
The only reason thermal desalination plants
are still being used is the dearth of confidence
in the reliability of SWRO process.
C
There is a need for the establishment of
SWRO test stations and small demonstration
plants based on the concept described in this
paper at some of the major power/desalination
facilities in the Middle East.
C
The research and development organizations
of countries in the Middle East need to be at
the forefront of this effort.
