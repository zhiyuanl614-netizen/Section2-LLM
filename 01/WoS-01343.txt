Resilience of Water Distribution Systems during
Real-Time Operations under Limited Water
and/or Energy Availability Conditions
Puneet Khatavkar, Ph.D., S.M.ASCE1; and Larry W. Mays, Ph.D., F.ASCE2
Abstract: A methodology for determining system operation resilience is presented for the real-time operation of water distribution systems
(WDS) under critical conditions of limited water or electrical energy resulting from extreme drought or electric grid failure. Resilience for
water distribution systems is defined as how quickly the WDS recovers or bounces back from emergency to normal operations. The algorithm
for operational resilience was interfaced with an optimization–simulation model for the real-time optimal operation of water distribution
systems. The resilience methodology considered both demand and water quality requirements of both the municipal WDS and the power
plant cooling systems. The optimization–simulation modeling approach interfaced a genetic algorithm optimization procedure with the WDS
hydraulic and water quality simulator (EPANET) in the framework of an optimal control problem. The interfacing of the genetic algorithm in
MATLAB and the EPANET model was implemented using a MATLAB–EPANET toolkit. An example WDS including two cities, five power
plants, and reclaimed water from a wastewater treatment plant was used to demonstrate the application of system operation resilience
concepts to assess the performance. The resilience computation methodology presented in this study is applicable to both short-term
and long-term failures of WDS. For the purposes of this study, the methodology was applied to three scenarios of short-term (2–6 h) power
outages for the example WDS. A sensitivity analysis was performed for resilience of example WDSs under varying degrees of long-term
system-level power and water shortages. Applications of the methodology are used to illustrate improved operation resilience of the system.

Introduction
Power distribution systems (PDSs) and the water distribution
systems (WDSs) are two critical infrastructure systems with opera-
tional interdependencies that have been recognized and studied in
broad generalities for some time. The main dependency of the PDS
on the WDS is the requirement of water for the cooling cycle of
thermoelectric power generation. The main dependency of the
WDS on the PDS is the power for pumping water from the source
to the treatment plant through the WDS. The interdependencies be-
come more acute under conditions of limited water or power
availability. The optimization–simulation framework used for this
study considers these interdependencies. If power plant cooling
water demands are not met, the PDS may fail to supply adequate
power for WDS pumping stations, leading to a cascading failure of
both systems. This study presents a methodology for computation
of operational resilience of WDS considering the interdependencies
between the water and power distribution systems. The methodol-
ogy can consider both the impacts of reduced water availability and
reduced power supply simultaneously or individually. This study is
limited to assessment of the effects of power or water shortage on
WDS operations. An optimization–simulation model for PDS oper-
ations is being developed as a part of the ongoing research for NSF
Project 029013-0010, CRISP Type 2—Resilient cyber-enabled
electric energy and water infrastructure modeling and control under
extreme drought. The ongoing research includes development of a
combined PDS-WDS resilience computation methodology to study
the effects of power or water shortage on operations of both PDSs
and WDSs.
Definition of Operational Resilience for Water
Distribution Systems
Several definitions of resilience of engineered systems are present
in the literature; a few notable ones are presented in this section.
Resilience measures the ability of a system to recover following
some disturbance or failure. Following are general definitions of
resilience not necessarily related to water distribution systems.
Folke (2006) stated that resilience measures the capacity of an
engineered system to absorb disturbance while the system is under-
going changes to retain essentially the same function, structure, and
feedbacks. Bruneau et al. (2003) defined resilience through the four
Rs of resilience: robustness, redundancy, resourcefulness, and rap-
idity. Walker et al. (2004) acknowledged that different interpreta-
tions of resilience used in the literature cause confusion and stated
that resilience of a system needs to be considered in terms of the
attributes that govern a system’s dynamics. Pandit and Crittenden
(2012) stated that resilience is the capacity of a system to absorb
shocks and perform under perturbations and that resilience can
serve as an appropriate indicator of system performance. Hosseini
et al. (2016) provided a detailed review of definitions and mea-
sures of resilience for engineered systems. Shin et al. (2018) inves-
tigated the definitions of quantitative resilience measures for water

A few definitions of resilience for WDS network structures and

design of WDSs, except a couple of studies by Aydin et al.
(2014a, b), which are based on operational resilience of WDSs.
Operational resilience is defined as the capacity of an engineered
system to absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing
change so as to retain essentially the same function, structure, iden-
tity, and feedbacks (Walker et al. 2004; Blackmore and Plants
2008). Operational resilience of water distribution systems was de-
fined by Aydin et al. (2014b) as how quickly a WDS recovers from
not meeting acceptable pressure levels. Aydin et al. (2014a) defined
operational resilience as the ratio of the number of times a satis-
factory performance follows an unsatisfactory performance and
the number of times an unsatisfactory performance occurs within
the system. This study uses the definition of operational resilience
given by Aydin et al. (2014a) for developing a real-time resilience
assessment methodology for WDS operations under emergency
conditions. An unsatisfactory performance would result when one
of the performance conditions, such as pressure requirements, is not
met. Meeting the end-user water quality requirements is another
important performance criterion for WDS operations.
The operational resilience computation methodology presented
is a comprehensive tool for performance assessment of WDS under
normal and emergency conditions of limited power and water avail-
ability. Operational resilience values show in real time the level to
which the system recovers from a performance failure. Therefore,
operational resilience is the best tool for WDS operations perfor-
mance assessment.

The constraints include two types: (a) those solved implicitly by
the EPANET model and (b) those incorporated into the objective
function as bound constraint violations.
The equations solved implicitly by EPANET for hydraulic and
water quality analysis are:
1. The conservation of mass at each junction node;
2. The conservation of energy for each pipe m connecting nodes i
and j;
3. The bounds on the water level of storage tanks; and
4. Equations to define water quality analysis.
Bound constraints are incorporated as bound violations into the
objective function.
1. The nodal pressure head minimum and maximum bounds;
2. Bounds on pump operation times;
3. Switch constraint for turning pumps on or off;
4. Power requirement constraint of the pumps;
5. Water quality constraint defining minimum and maximum
bounds on concentrations; and
6. Bounds on “satisfied” demand multipliers.
Genetic Algorithm
Genetic algorithms solve an unconstrained problem that is a re-
duced optimization problem consisting of the objective function
with the bound constraints incorporated as penalty functions. In
summary, the unconstrained optimization problem uses EPANET
as stated previously to solve constraints defined as the hydraulic
and water quality constraints, and the bound constraints are incor-
porated into the objective function as penalty functions to define the
reduced objective, which is solved using the genetic algorithm. The
interfacing of the genetic algorithm in MATLAB and the EPANET
model is implemented using a MATLAB–EPANET toolkit devel-
oped by Elíades and Kyriakou (2009).
Fig. 1 illustrates the optimization–simulation procedure inter-
faced with resilience computations. The optimization–simulation
model is provided with inputs including the EPANET network file
and the power availability [PowðavailableÞpump;t] as a function of
time t. Optimization–simulation iterations are performed to provide
the optimal pump controls and demands satisfied, which are used in
the resilience computations (Fig. 1).
The optimization–simulation model presented by Khatavkar
and Mays (2018) considers supplied water demands at a service-
area or pressure-zone level for the example presented herein but can
also be considered at a higher resolution with an appropriate water
demand prediction model. The demand patterns are less reliable at
an individual consumer level as compared to the service-area or
pressure-zone level. A statistical model that predicts the water
demands at an individual consumer level and considers historical
demands would be necessary to predict future demands. Provision
of flow control valves would be required at an individual consumer
level to control the supplied demands and would also be needed
for application of the optimization–simulation model at a higher
resolution.
The priority of WDS operations during emergency conditions of
limited power or water availability is to meet the requested
demands to the maximum possible extent. Therefore, the model
presented by Khatavkar and Mays (2018) considered the objective
of minimizing the difference between the demands requested
and supplied during real-time operations. The main aim of the

Real-Time Operation Framework during Limited
Electrical Power Generation
A few studies have been reported in the literature for optimization
of real-time controls of WDSs considering water-energy nexus in-
teractions. Fang and Chen (2017) investigated the links between
WDSs and energy systems for a city. Vakifard et al. (2018) pro-
vided a detailed literature review for techniques and approaches
for optimization of water supply systems considering the water-
energy nexus. Most of the studies explained include design ap-
proaches for WDSs considering water-energy nexus interactions.
Li et al. (2018) presented an optimization model for real-time
operations of water-energy nexus systems. The model considered
optimization of pump schedules during power outages but did
not use a WDS simulation model. Oikonomou and Parvania (2019)
presented a methodology for optimal coordinated controls of
WDSs and PDSs considering minimization of operational costs
for both systems. The study did not include simulation of real-time
WDS operations under emergency conditions. The methodology
presented herein considers optimization–simulation of real-time
operations of WDSs under emergency conditions of limited power
or water availability interfaced with an operational resilience com-
putation methodology for performance assessment.
The aim of optimizing the operations of a water distribution sys-
tem under limited availability of electrical power is an attempt to
satisfy the requested levels of demand at various locations while
also meeting the pressure requirements of the system. The objective
statement formulated here is to minimize the differences between
the requested and satisfied demands in all service areas in the
system. The following describes the overall concept of real-time
operation for each municipal water system:
1. At real-time t, receive electrical power input from PDS.
2. Receive latest data [status of pumps, tank levels, status of valves,
and flows in and out of the system from the supervisory control
and data acquisition (SCADA) system] for each water distribu-
tion system.
3. Update the EPANET for each water distribution system input
using data from the SCADA system for each WDS.
4. Run the WDS optimization model to determine the actual de-
mand pattern and pump operation that can be met with the
limited electrical energy input. During the optimization and si-
mulation process, the EPANET model is called repeatedly from
the genetic algorithm optimization to determine WDS opera-
tions over the next 24 h. The optimization model searches over
the decision variables (pump operation and a satisfied demand
pattern) to minimize the difference between demand requested
and demand satisfied. The simulator determines the values of
the state variables (nodal pressure heads, pipe flows, and tank
levels) for each set of control variables (pump operations and
satisfied demand multipliers) determined in the optimizer.
5. Implement the optimal pump schedule over the next hour, which
is accomplished through the SCADA system.
6. Repeat Steps 1–5 each time increment during the emergency
event, each time incrementing the time t ¼ t þ τ, with
τ ¼ 1 h.
In this approach, feedback from the EPANET model is provided
at each iteration of the overall real-time operation model. The
EPANET output from this process includes satisfied demand multi-
pliers (Dsat;z;tim), power consumption (Powpump;tim), pressure heads
at nodes (Pn;tim), and water quality (Cq;n;tim) at simulation time tim.
The system operation resilience (RESt) is computed at real time (t)
for simulation using the approach given in Fig. 1.
Operational Resilience Computations
The overall objective is to define a resilience as a weighting of the
pressure, power, demand, and water quality satisfaction. A function
in this study is defined as a measure of performance efficiency of
the WDS with respect to a performance parameter (pressure, power,
demand, and water quality satisfaction).
The respective functions for pressure, power availability, de-
mand satisfaction, and water quality are defined as follows.
Pressure Function
Pressure bounds are an important performance assessment criterion
for water distribution system operations. During emergency condi-
tions, water demands may be satisfied at lower pressures than gen-
erally acceptable, although lower pressure bound violations are a
partial failure of the water distribution system because prolonged
lower pressure bound violations could be a public health safety
hazard. Similarly, water demands could be satisfied at pressures
higher than the upper pressure bounds, which may lead to failure
of WDS components or household faucets. Therefore, from an
operational resilience perspective, pressure bound satisfaction is
important for the demands that can be satisfied.

Application Examples
Two example WDSs were used to illustrate the application of the
model. The first WDS system in Fig. 3 does not include water stor-
age tanks at the power plants, whereas the second system considers
a water storage tank at each of the power plants in the system. Each
example system includes two cities, a reclaimed wastewater treat-
ment plant (WWTP), and a power distribution system. City 1 con-
sists of four service areas (designated as nodes 1.1–1.4) with a total
base demand of 30,000 gpm, and City 2 (designated as nodes
2.1–2.5) consists of five service areas with a total base demand
of 25,000 gpm. A total of 17 freshwater pumps and 11 reclaimed
water pumps serve the WDS.
The power distribution system is based on the IEEE 14 bus sys-
tem (Kodsi and Canizares 2003), which consists of five power
plants. The cooling water for these power plants is supplied from
both a freshwater source and the reclaimed water from a wastewater
treatment plant. For the first WDS system (Fig. 3), instantaneous
mixing takes place for the fresh and reclaimed water supplied at the
power plant node, whereas for the second WDS system, the mixing
takes place within the tank connected to the power plant.
Each service area has a separate demand pattern. The WDS has
six demand patterns. One pattern applies to all the residential zones
within the cities and for each of the five power plants in the system.
Pipes in the WDS are categorized into three types. Main
lines (ML1–ML7) connect the freshwater pumps to the various
power plants and cities, intermediate lines (IL 1.1–IL 1.4 and IL
2.1–IL 2.5) are interconnecting nodes within the cities, and re-
claimed water pipelines (RW1–RW5) connect the waste water
treatment plants to the five power plants in the system. The pumps

methodology presented in this paper to the two WDSs discussed
previously for the three scenarios of short-term power outages

demands.

Observations
The following observations are made based upon application of the
optimization–simulation model and the resilience computation
method (Fig. 1). Figs. 4(a–c) show the system operation resilience
for Scenarios 1, 2, and 3 applications to first example, WDS 1.
A decline in resilience was observed during the hours of
limited power availability [Fig. (4a)] for Scenario 1 application
for the first example WDS. Similar observations were made for
Scenarios 2 and 3. Because no quality constraints were considered
in Scenario 1, no changes in the resilience were observed during
normal power availability because demands were satisfied during
such times. The value of resilience (RESt) is directly proportional
to the demand satisfaction and inversely proportional to the num-
ber of violations of the power availability constraints and pressure
bounds.
The system operation resilience of Scenario 2 application
for WDS 1 [Fig. (4b)] shows a decrease in the resilience at
hour 18. This decrease is attributed to a quality upper bound
(2,000 mg=L) imposed within the optimization model. It is evident
in this scenario that the system resumed normal operations after the
power shortage period.
The resilience in Scenario 3 [Fig. (4c)] shows different varia-
tions of resilience as compared to Scenario 2 [Fig. (4b)] because
of the more stringent water quality requirements (1,500 mg=L)
used for this application. The lower values of resilience observed
in this case are due to the lower level of demand satisfaction at the
power plants due to quality constraints.
For the second WDS, the storage tanks provided the system with
a redundancy in terms of stored water, which can be supplied in case
of an emergency. The storage tank also provided better control of the
water quality within the power plants because mixing within the tank
is not instantaneous and is modeled in EPANET using an extended
period simulation. With this increased robustness for the system, the
tanks also provided for increased complexity in terms of increased
connections, components, and costs. Fig. (5a) illustrates the system
operation resilience for Scenario 1 application for WDS 2.
The results of resilience computation show that the system could
fulfill its functions and did not suffer from any failure, even with a
limited power availability of 2 h at some pumps in the system
(given in Table 1). Fig. (5b) shows system operation resilience for
Scenario 2 application for the second example WDS. It is also evi-
dent that because system operation resilience values are based on
recovery of the WDS from a failure, lower values are only observed
after a failure occurs and not because of any other characteristics of
system performance. The lowest system operation resilience values
are observed during the hours of limited power availability only
for a short period, rather than failing to fulfill the functions for
the entire emergency period.

quality upper bounds (1,500 mg=L) do not affect the resilience of

observed in Fig. 4c for the first system.
Application of the model for a long-term drought scenario was
modeled with different levels of water and electrical energy supply
shortages. Fig. 6 shows the system operation resilience for 24-h
simulation–optimization under different conditions of water avail-
ability. The demands were satisfied to the requested levels to about
55,000 gpm of water availability and thereafter reduced with the
reducing water availability. The system operation resilience showed
a decline with a decrease in the water available.
System operation resilience for 24-h optimization–simulation
under different conditions of electrical energy availability is illus-
trated in Fig. 7. For energy availability of up to 150,000 kW-h per
day, it was observed that all the required system functions were
achieved to the fullest and hence the resilience values showed
100% for all values beyond that level of energy availability. With
a long-term reduction in power availability of 100,000 kW-h,
system operation resilience was between 0.75 and 0.8. This was
followed by small decreases in resilience values until an energy avail-
ability of 40,000 kW-h was reached, where a system operation resil-
ience of 0.7 was observed. The values of resilience showed a steep
fall for energy availabilities lower than 30,000 kW-h because the
pumps cannot be operated under lower energy availabilities.
For long-term drought scenarios, system-level limited water
availability was considered. Similarly, for long-term limited energy
availability scenarios, a user-supplied WDS-level limited electrical
energy availability was considered. This study is limited to assess-
ment of the effects of power or water shortage on WDS operations.
Ongoing research aims at assessment of feedback of the PDS
and WDS.
Conclusions
Performance of water distribution systems under the conditions of
limited power and water availability is an important consideration
for design and operation of a water-smart city. The aim of this study
is to present a mathematical formulation for system operation resil-
ience under considerations of limited water and power availability
conditions.
The resilience computation method presented in this paper
provides for a real-time WDS performance assessment tool. The
interactive nature of the optimization–simulation method used in
conjunction with the resilience computation methodology would
provide the WDS utility a real-time performance analysis and
decision-making tool. In addition to the real-time operations appli-
cations, the methodology presented in this study could also be ap-
plied to anticipatory drought scenario modeling and policy-making.
Decision-making using the resilience computation methodology
would involve several scenario and risk analyses considering the
various forecasted climate conditions. This paper shows the foun-
dation for mathematical formulation of an overall anticipatory
drought management tool based on WDS performance analysis
and control optimization for water-energy nexus considerations.
The operational resilience computation methodology presented
in this paper does not consider WDS component failure (pipe bursts
or pump failures) in the computation metrics. The objective of the
research is for extreme drought conditions.
The operational resilience computation methodology (shown in
Fig.1)wasapplied totworegional-levelexampleWDSscoupledwith
a supporting PDS. The system operation resilience was computed
based on four criteria: demand satisfaction, pressure bound compli-
ance, power availability, and water quality considerations. Results of
the application of resilience computation methodology for the exam-
ple systems showed the applicability of the model for short-term and
long-term limited power availability and for drought scenarios. Water
storage tanks at the power plants in the second WDS improved the
overall operational resilience as compared to the first WDS.
Future work will involve development of an interactive water-
energy system combined resilience computation methodology,
which would consider the performance of both the water and power
distribution systems under a combined control optimization–
simulation framework.
