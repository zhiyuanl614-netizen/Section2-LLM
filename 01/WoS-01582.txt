A B S T R A C T  
This paper studies a co-design of solar generation-based nano-grid and water treatment in remote areas. The goal 
is to provide the minimum essential drinking water and electricity needs to communities in remote, underde­
veloped regions such as Colonias in South Texas at the United States-Mexico border. It is shown that a portable 
nanofiltration treatment unit, powered by only a few photovoltaic (PV) panels augmented with batteries and 
supplemented with water storage tanks, offers crucial flexibility in accommodating solar-powered electricity 
service which is intermittent in its nature. Given the lack of infrastructure and associated primitive conditions in 
Colonias, the proposed planning and operation of the nano-grid leverages the flexibility of power consumption 
for the water filtration process to compensate for the fluctuating solar PV generation. A risk-tunable, scenario- 
based optimization is formulated to quantify the risks associated with the design, given different weather sce­
narios. The case study based on bench-scale experiments, optimization, and economic considerations coupled 
with historical weather data for targeted Colonias in Texas suggest the unique benefits of joint optimization of 
both electricity and water needs.   
1. Introduction 
One of the grand challenges of the 21st century is to meet the dual 
objectives of achieving sustainable economic growth while addressing 
significant disparities in basic infrastructure available to under-served 
minority communities [1]. In particular, most under-served commu­
nities in remote locations do not have access to clean water. However, 
the treatment of any available water supplies, in turn, requires a reliable 
supply of electric power, which is also not readily available in these 
communities. In this context, this paper examines a jointly optimized 
design of energy and water services for under-developed regions, such as 
Texas Colonias [2–4]. 
Colonias are unplanned housing developments largely on the Texas- 
Mexico border lacking essential services including drinking water,

solutions are needed to ensure a basic level of survivability in terms of

There have been several efforts to develop solutions for such under- 
developed communities around the world, such as in Sub-Saharan Africa 
[5], within islands [6], and elsewhere after significant disasters like 
hurricanes [7,8]. These examples have either focused on a static com­
munities in a particular geographical location with a limited budget, or 
temporary communities with a sufficient budget. However, there is a 
gap in developing sustainable solutions for communities that are 
simultaneously temporary and operate under severe financial 
constraints. 
In this setting, the main objective of this paper is to develop a low- 
budget localized solution to jointly fulfill survivable water and elec­
tricity needs using the Texas Colonias as a representative under- 
developed community. Although laws and policies supporting these 
Colonias are in place, the lack of a sustainable business plan, as well as 
their frequent impermanence, make significant infrastructure in­
vestments in these locations infeasible. Therefore, these societies 
continue to be vulnerable to health threats arising from a lack of clean 
water and continue to remain disadvantaged from an economic stand­
point, requiring locally tailored solutions to meet their infrastructure 
needs [9]. While the Texas Colonias are adopted as a representative 
community for our case study, we note that the proposed design and 
methodology are generalizable towards other under-developed regions. 
State-of-the-art: In general, under-developed, rural, and remote 
communities often lack centralized power infrastructure to fulfill sur­
vivable energy needs. In this context, recent advances in renewable 
energy-based distributed generation have enabled small-scale power 
systems known as micro-grids or nano-grids that can generate and store 
energy to serve a small collection of homes or buildings in an area [10], 
with numerous studies demonstrating the techno-economic feasibility of 
such grids, typically based on solar or wind power, in developing regions 
[11–20]. Traditionally, such micro- and nano-grids have solely focused 
on generating electricity for residential needs, with a separate energy 
system assumed to be in place for requirements like water treatment, 
heating, and cooling. Recently, hybrid energy systems commonly known 
as multi-generation, polygeneration, or cogeneration systems that 
jointly supply energy for some combination of electricity, water treat­
ment, heating, cooling, or transportation needs have emerged as an 
attractive alternative to traditional micro- or nano-grids [21–25]. In this 
paper, we will consider such a nano-grid that jointly optimizes energy 
requirements for both water filtration and residential needs. 
The lack of water and electricity infrastructure in many under- 
developed communities like the Colonias [26] also necessitates the 
search for suitable, cost-effective, and efficient water treatment systems 
with low energy requirements. Available water supplies in this region 
typically contain high total dissolved solids (TDS) and arsenic concen­
trations, requiring desalination to meet federal drinking water standards 
of 500 mg/L TDS (secondary) and 10 μg/L arsenic (primary). Currently, 
reverse osmosis (RO) is the technology-of-choice for desalination 
[27–30]. However, RO incurs significant energy costs that account for 
approximately half the total treatment costs [28,31]. 
The specific energy consumption (SEC), i.e. energy consumed 
normalized to filtered water (i.e. permeate) volume, is tightly linked 
with feedwater salinity since higher transmembrane pressures are 
required to overcome the increased osmotic pressure [32,33]. Im­
provements in membrane material technology and introduction of en­
ergy recovery devices has reduced the average SEC substantially 
[28,34]. SEC can be substantially reduced by employing nanofiltration 
(NF) as an alternative to RO, especially for brackish water [35–39]. 
However, NF membranes are more permeable to both water and salts 
compared with RO, necessitating their separate evaluation for suitability 
towards individual water supplies [35,40,39]. 
An additional consideration for NF/RO implementation for water 
filtration in impoverished locations is energy availability, with 
numerous studies investigating the feasibility and reliability of culti­
vating renewable energy with photovoltaics (PVs) as the dominant 
technology [34–36,41–44]. In this paper, we exploit and optimize the 
potential of PV installations augmented with battery storage capacity to 
jointly supply energy for both water filtration and residential needs. 
Contributions: In this paper, we design a nano-grid system that jointly 
addresses both water filtration and residential electricity needs using 
Texas Colonias as a model system. The nano-grid system comprises of a 
collection of photovoltaic (PV) panels and battery storage components 
that are used to supply energy to a portable water filtration unit that is 
transported between Colonias. Here, it is worth distinguishing nano- 
grids from the more typical micro-grids encountered in power systems 
literature [45]. Micro-grids cover a wide variety of applications and are 
usually not specifically designed to operate independent of the grid. 
Although some micro-grids can be developed for single buildings, they 
mostly interface with the utility. Some micro-grids aren’t even fully 
islandable. In contrast, a nano-grid is defined as a standalone power 
system for a small community in this paper. The nano-grid defined in 
this paper is an autonomous DC-based system that digitally connects 
individual devices to one other, as well as to power generation and 
storage within a building or small community [46]. The excess energy 
available from the PV panels after satisfying water treatment needs is 
stored and used for residential needs. We optimize the cost of this system 
as follows:  
• Optimizing water treatment costs: Employing NF membranes instead of 
RO will have significant reductions in filtration energy requirements, 
which, in turn, will reduce the sizing of the solar panels. In addition, 
the water required for two weeks is treated by a portable filtration 
unit over a duration of one week and stored in stationary tanks to 
ensure a continuous supply. The portable filtration unit can then be 
transported to another Colonia in the following week. Thus, a single 
water filtration unit is shared between Colonias to minimize system 
cost associated with water treatment.  
• Optimizing the sizing of the solar panels: The number of solar panels 
required to guarantee sufficient energy availability towards water 
filtration to meet a specified basic water requirement as well as fulfill 
essential residential needs varies due to the uncertainty in solar in­
tensity associated with seasonality and weather. The risk of not being 
able to generate enough energy to meet these needs can be decreased 
by installing a larger number of solar panels, albeit at an increased 
system cost. We provide a mathematically rigorous framework based 
on the scenario approach to quantify this trade-off between risk and 
system costs. The scenario approach is a paradigm that quantifies the 
probability (risk) of violating the constraints of an optimization 
problem whose parameters are subject to uncertainty [47]. We then 
use this insight to choose the optimal number of solar panels that 
minimizes system costs of the nano-grid while guaranteeing an 
acceptable risk threshold based on the uncertainty in solar genera­
tion at different times of the year.  
• Optimizing the battery storage: Since the water required for two weeks 
is obtained by treatment over a week’s duration, the pressure 
requirement for water treatment can be varied to flexibly harvest 
solar power during times of peak availability. Thus, the flexibility of 
scheduling energy for water treatment can be leveraged to minimize 
the battery storage capacity (and associated system cost) required to 
supply energy for water filtration by mostly utilizing it to serve the 
residential electricity need. Given the optimal number of solar panels

The key contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:  
• A joint design framework is proposed to consider water treatment 
flexibility and solar energy availability to simultaneously optimize 
the needs of water and electricity in nano-grids.  
• Based on the co-design formulation, a computationally efficient and

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first work that (i) 
explicitly leverages the flexibility offered by the water treatment process 
and water storage capabilities to minimize costs associated with energy 
generation and storage in such nano-grids, and (ii) provides a rigorous 
mathematical approach to quantify the trade-off between the risk 
associated with the uncertainty in renewable energy generation and 
system costs in the joint optimization of water treatment and residential 
electricity needs. While this manuscript focuses on the case study of 
Colonias, the technique and application can be generalized to any region 
without access or with unreliable access to minimum living standards of 
safe drinking water and electricity.

2. Overview of the nano-grid with jointly optimized energy and 
water filtration systems 
In this section, we outline the architecture and modes of operation of 
the proposed nano-grid with joint optimization of the energy and water 
filtration systems. 
2.1. Main components of the system 
We propose a system that consists of a stationary energy generation 
component (photovoltaic panels and battery station), a mobile water 
filtration unit, and storage tanks, as shown in Fig. 1. The main objective 
of this design is to jointly optimize the water and energy needs to 
minimize overall system costs by sharing the filtration unit between two 
Colonias. The mobile filtration unit consists of nanofiltration mem­
branes in a pilot-scale setting. The filtration unit can be hauled on a 
truck to be shared between two Colonias weekly, i.e., units will generate 
water during one week that is enough for a two-week supply. The 
filtered water is stored in stationary tanks to allow for continuous water 
supply during the week that the filtration unit is operating in the other 
Colonia. The battery banks serve two primary purposes. The first pur­
pose is to maintain a steady energy supply for the filtration unit for 
continuous operation, while the second purpose is to fulfill the surviv­
able electricity demands for the Colonias residents. 2 
2.2. Constant and variable pressure filtration modes 
We consider two water filtration modes, both aimed at fulfilling a 
water supply of 30 Liters of water per day for each of 200 Colonia res­
idents for a 2-week period but only over 6-days resulting in a capacity of 
14,000 L per day. A 6-day period was chosen to move the treatment unit 
between alternating Colonias every other week and leaving a day for 
moving, connecting, and disconnecting the filtration system to the feed 
water source and perform any necessary maintenance. In the first mode, 
constant pressure is applied to represent battery usage while maintain­
ing a steady power supply. The second mode relies on variable pressure 
and pumping that is designed to minimize battery costs by harvesting 
solar power at its peak while using batteries for filtration at a low- 
pressure corresponding to the duration of low solar radiation intensity. 
2.3. Solar energy design scenarios and their associated risks 
The design of the solar component of the system accounts for mul­
tiple scenarios in terms of the selected number of solar panels to fulfill 
water filtration and other electricity demands. Due to the fluctuating 
nature of the solar power, the energy component design of the system, i. 
e., the number of solar panels, will vary depending on these seasonal and 
weather variations. Therefore, the required PV panels for each week of 
the year may change dramatically between the weeks of the most and 
least available solar intensity to harvest. The design principle is to 
determine a good trade-off between the system costs (minimal number 
of solar panels and battery size) and the associated risk of not being able 
to satisfy the energy requirements for water filtration and residential 
needs due to the inherent uncertainty in the solar power generation. 
Hence, we employ a scenario approach to optimize the number of solar 
panels to minimize system cost, while guaranteeing water filtration and 
household electrical needs throughout the entire year of operation with 
a desired risk threshold. Additionally, we minimize the battery size 
required (and hence costs) for this system by leveraging the flexibility of 
the water filtration system to operate at variable pressure to directly 
harvest solar energy during peak periods. 
3. Methods for co-design of energy and water filtration systems 
In this section, we detail all the experimental procedures and 
mathematical formulations to co-design and optimize the energy and 
water filtration systems in the nano-grid. The schematic representation 
in Fig. 2 visually illustrates and links the methodologies employed to 
achieve the optimized design. 
3.1. Filtration component design and configuration 
Implementing nanofilters as an alternative to RO requires investi­
gating these membranes to determine the quality of the treated water 
(the permeate). Hence, we conducted the following procedures for 
samples collection and analysis: 
3.1.1. Water sources 
Water samples were collected from wells in Colonia Campacuas 
(Latitude +26.18068 and Longitude −97.92886) and Colonia San Isidro 
(+26.71883, −98.44413), and an unnamed canal that runs by Colonia 
Wes-Mer (+26.20246, −97.95648), as shown in Fig. 3. These are located 
in Starr and Hidalgo counties near the United States-Mexico border, 
which have a high density of Colonias making these water sources 
suitable candidates for this research. Samples were received in plastic 
containers and stored at 4 ◦C until experimentation.

3.2.2. Residential electricity demands (dn[w]) 
The residential electricity consumption was estimated based on the 
essential living needs of a household, such as lighting, cooking, charging 
for cellphones, and other purposes. It is treated as a soft constraint 
throughout the paper. We assume the use of 40 and 60 W incandescent 
bulbs for 2–5 h per night (depending on the month of the year using a 
time series of dusk to dawn), amounting to a maximum of 100 W × 5 h 
= 0.5 kWh. Cooking was assumed necessary for at least 1–2 h each day, 
using a 1 kW stove. Cellphone charging and other needs accounted for 1 
kWh. These estimates bring the total household daily consumption to 3.5 
kWh. Without the loss of generality, the average number of people 
within each household is assumed to be six in the design. 
3.3. Energy-water joint optimization 
The electricity for the above-mentioned water treatment and resi­
dential demands needs to be available and delivered reliably. The joint 
optimization involves minimizing the number of solar panels needed to 
generate the electricity for water treatment as well as residential use. 
The electricity generated by solar panels is used either directly, or 
temporarily stored in a battery for later use. The objective of our opti­
mization framework is to minimize system cost while taking into ac­
count seasonal and weather related uncertainties in solar intensity, and 
guaranteeing that the energy needs for water treatment and residential 
purposes are met with an acceptable risk threshold. The key target for 
this optimization is to provide a minimal nano-grid design supplying the 
community with potable water and residential electricity, while pre­
venting overly expensive strategies.

quantify the risk associated with the decisions. In the context of the 
nano-grid considered in this paper, the cost of the energy generation 
system can be minimized by solving optimization problem (5) to decide

based on the weather and season-dependent solar intensity for a given

[47] to quantify the risk of not being able to fulfill the required water 
and energy needs by using the number of solar panels as determined by 
the solution to the optimization problem (5). 
One of the primary results of scenario theory is that, given an 
adequate number of samples from any unknown uncertainty, a convex 
optimization problem could be solved such that the risk associated with 
the solution (i.e. probability of the solution violating the constraints of 
the optimization problem) is guaranteed to not to exceed a risk

scenario theory is that no explicit information on the underlying dis­
tribution of uncertainty is needed to obtain a guarantee on the risk. Such 
a scenario approach, providing constraint satisfaction guarantees with a 
quantifiable risk, has been successfully adopted in several power and 
energy applications like risk-tunable economic dispatch and demand 
response [51–54].

The purpose of this section is to estimate costs of system components,

4. Results and discussion 
The experimental and mathematical outcomes are presented and 
discussed in the same order as detailed in Section 3 and Fig. 2. Therefore, 
the filtration component configuration will be detailed first, followed by 
the estimated required energy for filtration, and the optimized nano-grid 
design. Finally, the cost comparisons of multiple design decisions are 
discussed at the end of this section. 
4.1. Filtration component design 
Results of WAVE simulations and lab-scale experiments showed that 
the loose, less energy intense NF270 membrane fitted the various design 
needs as detailed below. 
4.1.1. Simulation results based on WAVE runs 
WAVE simulations based on feed water quality (Table 1) revealed 
that three 4′′ × 40′′ NF270-4040 membranes (total area of 22.9 m2) in a 
single-stage setup were sufficient enough to maintain a permeate water 
quality with TDS below 500 mg/L without a need for pretreatment or 
antiscalant addition. Multiple cases were simulated to have permeate 
water flow rate ranging between 0.3–1.2 m3/hr, with a fixed system 
recovery of 40% to minimize fouling effects [65]. Permeate flow rates of 
0.3–0.5 m3/hr generated permeate water with TDS > 500 mg/L. How­
ever, when these flow rates are considered only to maintain system 
performance during zero solar radiation times, the cumulative filtered 
water will yield TDS < 500 mg/L. 
4.1.2. Filtration 
Experiments run according to solar energy data were modeled using 
the power profile for the median day (Fig. 4) and also at constant 
pressure (Fig. 6a). Resulting changes in the specific flux (defined as the 
ratio of permeate flux to applied pressure, Fig. 6b) and power con­
sumption (Fig. 6c), demonstrate that both follow the imposed pressure 
change pattern. The power mostly followed the trend of the pressure 
profile but did not increase to the same extent as permeate flux (for 
example, power consumption increased only 3-fold when water pro­
duction increased by 5.5-fold). This results in a lower SEC with higher 
water production, which can, in turn, allow for a lower specific cost of 
production, similar to other reports [36,65]. 
While the variable pressure experiments have significant importance 
in simulating conditions responding to fluctuations in available energy, 
the constant pressure experiments can highlight other benefits in un­
derstanding the system performance and membrane susceptibility to 
fouling, which are critical operational considerations. Constant pressure 
(70 psig) filtration reduced specific flux by 2.2% and 6.6% after 24 and 
120 h of continuous operation of well Campacuas water, respectively, 
(Fig. 6d). Although membrane fouling was not our focus, it is worth 
highlighting the possible foulants and suggestions for chemical cleaning 
since they are essential for successful system operation and maintenance 
[40]. Both groundwaters were more saline than the surface water, with 
the reverse being true for organic carbon (Table 1). Therefore, it is ex­
pected that the Canal Wes-Mer water can cause more natural organic 
matter and/or biofouling compared with well water from Campacuas 
and San Isidro. Further, surface water is well known to be more fouling 
than groundwater, due to a higher concentrations of colloids (turbidity) 
and organic matter [66].

4.2. Required energy for filtration 
NF relationships were fitted to correlate flux, power, and pressure 
cross relationships and provide these data for a better understanding of 
scale-up. Power and energy requirements scale-ups were estimated 
based on filtration system available SEC data of NF270 in literature 
[35,37], the procedure outlined in Section 3.2.1, and WAVE outputs. 
These calculations revealed that three 4′′ × 40′′ NF270 membranes (each 
with 7.6 m2 active area) would satisfy both water quality and quantity 
needs for 200 Colonia residents (30 L/person/day). Hence, lab experi­
ments agree with WAVE simulations lending higher credibility to our 
approach and results. 
Three issues restrict the direct scale-up of pumping and energy re­
quirements from simple bench-scale studies similar to the ones we 
conducted herein to larger scales. First, real-world brackish water NF/ 
RO plants typically operate at high feed water recoveries (ratio of the 
filtered water to the feed water flowrates), such as the 40% recovery of 
this study. In contrast, bench-scale experiments are typically performed 
at low recoveries to maintain high cross-flow velocity and minimize 
concentration polarization [40], which artificially increases energy re­
quirements [65]. This can be seen from (2), whereby lowering recovery, 
the feed flow is increased for the same design capacity, in turn, 
increasing the required power for filtration. 
Second, commercial NF/RO membranes are all spirally-wound to 
increase the packing density compared with flat sheets employed in lab- 
scale systems used for research purposes. The pressure drop and mass 
transfer in spiral-wound pressure vessels are substantially different from 
flat sheet cross-flow systems used herein [67], thereby changing energy 
requirements as well. Finally, centrifuge or turbine-based pumps pref­
erentially used in large-scale NF/RO plants cannot be scaled down for 
the low flow (order of mL/min) requirements for small units. Therefore, 
power consumption during lab-scale experiments was utilized only to 
understand the general trends of power variations with system changes. 
WAVE simulations revealed a lower SEC for NF270-4040 membranes 
(0.13–0.51 kWh/m3) compared with NF90-4040 membranes (0.23–0.67 
kWh/m3) for the targeted flow rate range 0.3–1.2 m3/h. Estimated en­
ergy was also calculated based on (2) and yielded similar SEC values to 
WAVE simulations. Hence, NF270 selection resulted in less energy from 
batteries during periods of minimal available solar energy. Our findings 
are corroborated by other reports that SEC of NF270 membranes for 
small systems was ̃ 0.8 kWh/m3 [37,35], which is slightly higher than 
our analysis, probably due to smaller system design (system capacity of

4.3. Co-Design of electricity system with consideration of water filtration 
flexibility 
The co-design of the nano-grid PV panels and battery storage ca­
pacity is carried out by initially calculating the minimum PV panels 
needed to provide sufficient energy for each week of the whole annual 
data set, followed by assessing the risks of multiple PV scenarios, and the 
computation of the minimum necessary battery storage capacity. Both 
filtration modes (constant pressure and variable pressure) are consid­
ered for all these steps. 
4.3.1. Design for the minimum number of PV panels 
To provide a suitable baseline for comparison, the results of using 
planning strategies based on (5) and (6) were evaluated for both modes 
of filtration as described in Section 2.2. Based on the annual solar data 
[49], the minimum number of solar panels needed for each week of the 
year is shown in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 for the constant and variable pressure 
modes of operation, respectively. 
The PV requirement for residential electricity needs is fixed in both 
modes of filtration. Hence, the difference between these figures is 
limited to the filtration modes. In Fig. 9, the output from PV generation 
is primarily used directly for filtration, with any excess being stored in 
batteries. This direct harvesting of solar energy for filtration reduces the 
energy loss corresponding to the battery charging/discharging cycles. As 
seen in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9, the majority of the consumption is dedicated to 
residential electricity requirements. 
From the blue-colored columns in Figs. 8 and 9, less than twenty, for 
fixed pressure mode, or ten, for variable pressure mode, solar panels 
could be enough to supply the required electricity for the water treat­
ment unit over the six days of water treatment. Thus, by fixing the en­
ergy requirement for water filtration as a hard constraint and treating 
the residential electricity as a flexible need, one may guess from the re­
sults in these figures that sufficient supply of electricity for water 
treatment in the fixed and variable pressure filtration modes can be 
guaranteed using 20 or 10 solar panels, respectively. 
It is important to emphasize here that the current design itself in­
cludes the weeks with worst-case solar profile conditions, as shown in 
Figs. 8 and 9. Based on these figures, there are several weeks of the year, 
e.g. between week 40 and week 50, where the number of required solar 
panels increases by more than 200% when compared to the remaining 
weeks of the year. Hence, for these weeks, there will likely be an 
insufficient supply of energy for residential needs; however, the water 
filtration needs, being a hard constraint in the design, can still be ful­
filled. It is also worth mentioning that since the design was aggregated 
on a weekly basis, days with “zero” solar energy will be compensated for 
by other days of the week with available solar energy. 
An important aspect of the design analysis is to assess whether the 
above-mentioned numbers of solar panels will be sufficient for the same 
Colonia when considering multiple years with variable weather and 
solar conditions. This is the concept of operational risk associated with a 
particular choice of solar panels. In the next subsection, we will adopt 
the scenario approach described in Section 3.3.1 to rigorously compute 
an upper bound on this risk. 
4.3.2. Scenario approach for risks associated with PV panels design 
Figs. 8 and 9 reveal a broad range of ̃ 30–100 and ̃ 20–80 PV panels 
with the constant and variable pressure modes of operation respectively, 
required to satisfy the filtration and household energy needs for the 
weeks with most and least available solar energy to harvest. With the aid 
of the scenario approach, we can obtain a more rigorous understanding 
of risks associated with each PV design scenario. 
For instance, if 100 independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) 
samples are collected based on the historical data for the formulation in 
(6) with the number of decision variables 4 being γ = 1 (that is, the 
number of solar panels, denoted by x), then the risk is guaranteed to be 
less than 0.1544 with 1 −10−6 confidence. These results are a significant 
improvement compared to calculations using (5) since a numerical 
bound on the dependability of the results can also be defined. 
To explore the relationship between the risk parameter and the 
sampling size, synthetic data (solar generation per panel for 6 days of the 
week over all 52 weeks of the year using data from [49], converted to 
kWh per panel per hour) was generated by fitting a normal distribution 
over the weekly radiation data [68]. The resulting data satisfies the i.i. 
d requirement of the scenario approach theory [47]. A truncated normal 
distribution [69] at μ ± 2σ is used to generate i.i.d samples according to 
this data. Without loss of generality, the normal distribution was 
employed; however, different distributions could be utilized. The pro­
cedure of generating samples without knowing the distribution of un­
derlying uncertainty is also the subject of several pioneering studies on 
scenario theory [70,71]. The optimization problem (6) is then solved 
using these sample numbers to find the optimal number of solar panels 
to be installed. Also, the risk parameter associated with each of different 
sample sizes is calculated.

significantly. For instance, APV pertaining to the lowest risk presented in

filtration mode in comparison to the constant filtration mode. Table 3 
summarizes the battery sizes and their average costs. The cost per kWh 
of batteries are extracted from [64]. It is important to mention that the 
values and comparisons in Table 3 pertain to the battery storage ca­
pacities required for filtration purposes, i.e. BC. The total battery ca­
pacity, i.e. TBC, will be part of the cost estimations in the following 
section. These results further elucidate the benefits in leveraging the 
flexibility offered by the variable pressure mode of filtration to reduce 
energy system costs. 
4.4. Overall cost estimation 
From the above-mentioned results, we selected multiple designs for 
an overarching evaluation and comparison of the annual costs associ­
ated with multiple decisions. In these designs, the filtration unit cost was 
fixed, since water filtration is a hard constraint and costs are not affected 
with multiple filtration modes. A summary of these costs , provided with 
three significant figures,is presented in Table 4. 
The water filtration costs estimations are summarized in Table 4 
based on methods detailed in Section 3.4.1. It is worth mentioning that 
the calculated unit costs were comparable to price quotations obtained 
from manufacturers of membrane systems and appurtenances, vali­
dating and confirming the factors and calculations in our cost estima­
tion. For instance, based on these quotations and communications, the 
annual cost for the NF system itself is around 1060 dollars/year, which is 
comparable to the calculated annual cost based on (11) of 1390 dollars/ 
year. 
To maintain a reasonable baseline for comparisons, the number of PV 
panels for each filtration scenario was selected to obtain the same risk 
parameter ∊ for both filtration modes. Therefore, 47 and 29 panels were 
selected for systems running under constant and variable filtration 
modes, respectively. 
As shown in the Table 4, the final annual cost of stationary solar 
panels and mobile filtration unit will be around 112,000 and 88,800 
dollars/year for the systems running at fixed and variable pressure 
filtration modes, respectively. Therefore, there is approximately a 20% 
reduction in costs if the system is designed for variable pressure oper­
ation. This results in substantial savings when these costs are normalized 
to the unit volume of filtered water or to cost per capita, as shown in 
Table 4. Note that these estimations are higher than some reported solar 
water estimated costs in literature [41], due to inclusion of minimum 
residential electricity supply needs in this study. 
Two more comparisons are also worth mentioning in this context. 
The first comparison is associated with our decision to mobilize the NF 
unit and share it between two Colonias. If the NF system were stationary, 
the annual costs for the two Colonias would increase to 124,000 and 
101,000 dollars/year for the systems running at fixed and variable 
pressure filtration modes, respectively. These costs translate to 
approximately a 10 and 12% increase in the costs for both modes, 
respectively. Therefore, the decision of sharing NF system provides its 
own economic benefits. The other comparison is the impact of excluding 
the battery storage capacity for residential electricity demands due to 
financial restrictions, since residential electricity needs are considered a 
soft constraint. By utilizing costs based on BC only, the total annual costs 
for the two Colonias in this case will drop significantly to 62,000 and 
38,400 dollars/year for fixed and variable pressure modes, respectively. 
5. Concluding remarks 
This paper presents a co-design of water and electricity provision in 
remote areas through the means of solar-powered nano-grids. By 
leveraging the flexibility of power needs for water treatment over time, 
we show that the planned sizing of solar panels and battery storage for 
the nano-grids could be designed with the dual objectives of providing 
survivable levels of water and energy needs. This design takes into ac­
count uncertainties in solar power through a scenario approach,
