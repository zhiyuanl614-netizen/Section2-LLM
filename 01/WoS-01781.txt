A B S T R A C T  
Critical infrastructure plays an important role in providing essential products and services to people; however, it 
is vulnerable to disasters and its disruption can have a severe societal impact. This study establishes a theoretical 
framework and proposes an integrated methodology to evaluate the societal impact of disrupted infrastructure in 
disaster events. Here, the societal impact is based on a water analysis and is defined as the percentage of the 
population in different levels of need satisfaction, which depends on the amount of water that people can obtain. 
Subsequently, based on water supply resilience analysis, an engineering model is proposed to estimate the water 
quantity during a disaster event considering the availability of tap water, emergency water, and bottled water. 
Meanwhile, physical interdependencies among the water supply system, emergency service, commercial facil­
ities, electricity system, and transportation system are incorporated into an availability analysis of the three 
water sources. Finally, the proposed model is applied to the city of Osaka to validate its applicability and 
effectiveness. The results demonstrate that the proposed model can provide valuable information to decision- 
makers about the severity and spatial distribution of the societal impact of infrastructure disruption.   
1. Introduction 
Modern societies comprise several levels of interdependent critical 
infrastructure (CI), which provide people with essential resources and 
services, e.g. water, food, housing, and medical services [1]. Infra­
structure can be disrupted due to damage caused when natural and 
man-made hazards strike a modern city. In such situations, residents 
would have difficulties in accessing life-supporting resources and some 
people’s basic needs may not be satisfied, which could lead to severe 
societal impacts. As cities continue to develop, residents are becoming 
increasingly dependent on CI, and the societal impact due to unexpected 
disturbances become larger. Therefore, evaluating the negative impact 
of infrastructure disruption on society is critical for providing essential 
information to decision makers and to ensure minimal impact upon the 
daily lives of residents in the aftermath of disasters. 
There are several studies regarding the vulnerability [2,3], reliability 
[4,5], and resilience [6,7] analysis of infrastructure. Furthermore, the 
interdependency among different infrastructure systems, which can lead 
to cascading failure propagations, has received increasing attention 
during the last decade [8–11]. Results of these studies have highlighted 
the vital role of CI in social and economic development. However, little 
effort has been made to connect various infrastructure disruptions with 
their societal impact, owing to the difficulties in quantitatively 
measuring the societal impact and integrating it with disruptions. 
The societal impact of a disaster can be multifaceted due to the 
multidimensionality of society and can include socioeconomic, psy­
chosocial, sociodemographic, and political impacts [12–14]. When 
focusing on the consequences caused by damaged CI, the impact can be 
measured using the well-being change dimension [15,16]. Tabandeh 
et al. [17,18] assessed the decrease in well-being due to a disaster via a 
reliability-based capability approach that considers the disruption of 
infrastructure. Deshmukh et al. [19] developed a framework to 
semi-quantitatively evaluate the changes in socio-economic activity due 
to disrupted infrastructure. However, many indicators used to measure

impacts in these studies rely heavily on the experts’ subjective judge­
ment and may lead to estimation bias. Conversely, Dargin et al. [20] 
proposed a theoretical framework to assess the effect of lifeline service 
disruptions on the well-being of households. They used hardship expe­
rience as a proxy variable to measure the emotional aspects of 
well-being. Similarly, Esmalian et al. [21] and Dong et al. [22] utilised 
the hardship experience of individuals to measure the well-being impact 
and quantified it by comparing the service disruption severity and 
household tolerance levels. Overall, using hardship experience to mea­
sure changes to well-being is based on social investigation, so the results 
are region- and disaster-specific. Additionally, they did not examine the 
underlying reasons for negative emotional well-being, which is induced 
and escalated by the unsatisfactory fulfilment of residents’ needs. 
Mahdavian et al. [23] illustrated that people’s anticipated needs for 
water and food increase immediately after a blackout, and the unsat­
isfaction of essential service needs, especially information, could esca­
late the negative emotions of individuals. However, this mechanism has 
not been incorporated into the definition and evaluation of societal 
impacts in previous studies. 
Incorporating needs satisfaction into the definition of societal impact 
can help connect infrastructure disruptions with societal impact evalu­
ation. This involves evaluating the gap between the demand and supply 
of goods/services during a disaster event. Penchansky and Thomas [24] 
proposed five dimensions (accessibility, availability, affordability, ac­
commodation, and acceptability) to evaluate the supply-demand 
disparity in the health care system. Nozhati et al. [9] proposed a prob­
abilistic framework to predict failure of food supply, considering dis­
rupted CI from three dimensions (accessibility, availability, and 
affordability of food) after a disaster. During a disaster event, the 
availability of necessary resources/services for individuals is mostly 
impaired due to functionality failure of multiple CI systems, and its 
reduction directly leads to the unsatisfaction of individuals’ needs. 
Hence, estimating the availability of essential services after disasters is 
critical to predict their societal impact. This is one stream of infra­
structure resilience analysis, in which the resilience is usually defined as 
the potential to remain operational under any disruption [25]. 
In the field of infrastructure resilience analysis, extensive research 
has been conducted with respect to service availability analysis during 
disruptions, such as medical, food, water, and housing services. Chang 
et al. and Yavari et al. [26,27] established a model to predict the func­
tionality of medical services (hospitals) by integrating the performance 
of lifelines (electricity and water), building damage, and personnel. The 
availability of water supply systems is usually evaluated by network 
analysis and hydraulic simulation considering the failure of water sys­
tem components and interdependent infrastructure [28–30]. For 
post-disaster studies, Nazarnia [25] incorporated the social network 
method to analyse water service disruption patterns using household 
service disruption data. The housing service of buildings, which is 
measured by population dislocation and outmigration, were estimated 
as a function of socio-economic factors, physical damage to buildings, 
electricity network condition, and water networks [26,31]. In general, 
available resources/services for individuals usually depends on the 
resilience of multiple infrastructure systems. Additionally, the adaptive 
capacity of CI is an important component of resilience, which influences 
the availability of life-supporting goods/services. For example, the ca­
pacity to supply substitute or emergency resources from institutional 
agencies in a disaster event [32–34]. However, few studies consider 
adaptive capacity when estimating the total amount of resources/ser­
vices available to individuals, and this may overestimate the societal 
impact. 
In view of the abovementioned challenges, this study proposes a 
theoretical framework and an integrated methodology to quantify the 
societal impact of infrastructure disruptions in the context of disasters. 
The main contributions of this study include the incorporation of indi­
vidual needs satisfaction into the definition and evaluation of societal 
impact; and the estimation of availability of resources/services for 
individuals considering the interdependency and adaptive capacity of 
infrastructure systems. The remainder of the study is organised as fol­
lows. In Section 2, a theoretical framework is proposed to illustrate how 
disrupted infrastructure can have a societal impact; we expand this 
framework from the perspective of a water analysis. In Section 3, an 
integrated methodology is presented to estimate societal impact 
considering the disruption of water-related CI systems, including water 
supply system, electricity system, transportation system, emergency 
service, and commercial facility. Then, Section 4 presents a case study of 
Osaka using the proposed methodology, and explains the corresponding 
results. Finally, a discussion of these results and the conclusions of the 
study are provided in Section 5. 
2. Theoretical framework 
The theoretical framework shown in Fig. 1 presents how infrastruc­
ture disruptions and societal impact are connected. In general, from the 
perspective of the individual, people consume essential goods/service, 
which are provided by CI, to perform necessary activities in order to 
satisfy their needs. According to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the most 
fundamental needs are physiological needs, followed by safety and se­
curity, belonging, and growth and achievement needs [1,35]. In the 
context of disaster events, physiological needs, like food, water, housing, 
and clothing, are more urgent than others [1]. Thus, individual physi­
ological needs are the focus of this study. If infrastructure systems are 
damaged due to a disaster, their service provision for individuals will be 
reduced. Consequently, people’s daily activities may be affected, which 
can lead to people’s needs not being satisfied. The different levels of 
satisfaction of everyone’s needs can reflect the severity of the whole 
societal impact. Hence, this study defines societal impact as the per­
centage of a population whose needs cannot be satisfied due to the 
infrastructure disruptions caused by disasters. 
To better illustrate the mechanism by which infrastructure disrup­
tions can have a negative societal impact, this study focuses on water, as 
water plays a vital role in daily life. The definitions and categories of CI 
used in this study are adopted from the National Infrastructure Protec­
tion Plan (NIPP) [13,36]. As previously mentioned, the availability of 
resources/services depends on infrastructure resilience, which can be 
quantified as the functionality of systems providing services to cus­
tomers following a disaster [1,37]. Meanwhile, infrastructure systems 
possess inherent resilience and adaptive capacity, that is, the ability or 
potential to respond successfully to a disruption (e.g. emergency 
response) [32–34]. Thus, it is necessary to incorporate adaptive capacity 
into hazard impact evaluation [38]. Considering the water supply 
resilience in the aftermath of disaster, there are three main water 
sources provided by CI: tap water (TW), bottled water (BW), and 
emergency water (EW), as shown in Fig. 1. Many studies have proved 
that the increased social capital of communities could also enhance the 
water supply resilience; however, this was not considered in our study 
[39,40]. TW is the main water source provided by water supply system, 
while the supply of BW and EW reflects the adaptive capacity of the 
whole infrastructure systems. Specifically, BW is a TW substitute that 
residents can obtain from commercial facilities such as stores, malls, and 
supermarkets. The functionality of these commercial facilities influences 
the availability of BW. EW supply occurs in response to TW suspension, 
and mainly depends on the functionality of emergency services. More­
over, the electricity system and transportation system are considered in 
this study because the water supply of CI is physically dependent on 
them and, therefore, they influence the availability of water [33,41]. 
Simultaneously, water resource supports social functions of sustaining 
human activities, e.g. individual daily activities, production, and other 
activities [42,43]. Individual activities are emphasised in this study as 
they are achieved to satisfy human needs; for instance, drinking and 
cooking could satisfy the need for survival; washing, bathing, and 
laundry could meet people’s basic hygiene needs. Whether people’s 
needs are satisfied or not under disruptions determines the level of

To quantitatively estimate the societal impact of infrastructure dis­

social dimension analysis involves evaluating the needs satisfaction 
level from the perspective of individuals, i.e., focusing on the societal 
adverse effect estimation. The engineering dimension analysis is related 
to the resilience analysis of infrastructure; the availability of essential 
services is estimated considering disruptions to CI systems.

Based on the theoretical framework presented in Section 2, an inte­
grated methodology was developed to quantify the societal impact of 
disrupted infrastructure. From the social dimension analysis, a quanti­
tative relationship between the societal impact estimation and quantity 
of available water was established. From the engineering dimension 
analysis, an integrated water quantity estimation model was developed 
to estimate the availability of water in a disaster event considering the 
disruption to and interdependency of CI systems (i.e. the electricity 
system, the water supply system, the transportation system, emergency 
services, and commercial facilities). By connecting the engineering and 
social dimension analyses using water quantity, the societal impact can 
be estimated given direct damages to infrastructure systems during a 
disaster event. 
3.1. Societal impact estimation of water disruption 
Societal impact, here, is defined as the percentage of a population 
whose physiological needs cannot be satisfied due to disrupted infra­
structure. In other words, the level of needs satisfaction for every indi­
vidual contributes to the societal impact as a whole. Whether needs are 
satisfied or not depends on the achievement of the corresponding ac­
tivities for each individual. Furthermore, the achievement of activities 
relies on the provision of essential goods/service by CI. 
Water is fundamental to maintain essential daily activities, such as 
drinking, cooking, washing, bathing, and laundry. The water supply in 
the society, which is illustrated in Fig. 1, is susceptible to disasters, for 
example, earthquakes, typhoons, and storm surges [39]. This could 
reduce the provision of TW, BW, and EW, restricting people’s abilities to 
conduct daily activities. As listed in Table 1, five levels of needs satis­
faction are defined according to the achievement of different activities 
from the perspective of individuals [44]. According to the characteristics 
of people’s daily activities, the achievement of the activities is a function 
of the quantity and quality of water [43]. However, considering 
poor-quality water, which is not meeting water quality standards, will 
not be supplied to citizens in a disaster event [45], we assume that the

The water required for individuals’ activities varies with climatic

to the achievement of daily activities, can be linked to the quantity of 
water (Table 1). Thus, based on this quantitative relationship, the needs 
satisfaction level of each individual can be evaluated given the different 
spatial distribution of water quantity due to damaged CI. Then, the 
percentage of the population at different levels can be derived to reflect 
the quantitative societal impact.

3.2.1. Availability analysis of tap water 
The water supply system, which provides TW for residents, is the 
main source of water. It purifies water from a river, lake, or well in a 
purification plant, and transports the treated water to every household 
through water pipelines to ensure that people have access to clean water 
from household taps. As indicated in Fig. 2, the essential components of 
the water supply system are the purification plant, pump station, storage 
node, and water pipeline. Pump stations play an important role in 
ensuring the delivery of clean water by providing enough pressure. 
Storage facilities, like water tanks, water towers, and water reservoirs, 
are used for storing treated water and ensuring a steady water supply 
when the water demand is high. Pump stations and purification plants 
cannot operate without electricity, which is mainly provided by the 
electricity system. Standby power systems are used in case of a power 
outage in the aftermath of a disaster. 
After a disaster, some components of the water network can be 
directly or indirectly damaged, which could lead to the unavailability of 
water at demand nodes (the aggregated water demand of households in 
a service area). For example, more than one million people lost TW 
following Hurricane Katrina in the southern United States, and almost 
100% of people lost water services after the 2010 Chile earthquake [39]. 
A connectivity analysis of water supply systems was performed to 
evaluate whether treated water can be delivered from the source to 
demand nodes or not after the failed components were removed [28]. 
However, the connectivity analysis could not adequately model the 
availability of water from taps because water cannot be transported to 
taps without sufficient pressure and flow in the network; thus, a hy­
draulic analysis is also needed [47]. There are many hydraulic simula­
tion tools for water network systems, amongst which EPANET is the 
most popular [7,29,48,49]. However, EPANET, which uses a 
demand-driven hydraulic engine, is not suitable for pressure-deficient 
situations that often occur in disaster events [27]. In order to solve 
this problem, Pathirana developed the EPANET-EMITTER package using 
a pressure-driven approach on the basis of the EPANET solver [50]. This 
package is suitable for low pressure analyses and has a modest input data 
requirement. Therefore, the EPANET-EMITTER package was adopted in 
this study to build a water supply system and performed a hydraulic 
simulation. 
The availability of TW is related to the network topology, pressure, 
and flow. Pressure is a more important factor to consider than topology 
or flow when measuring the availability of TW, because the pressure 
value can reflect the connectivity and flows of system. A pressure of zero 
indicates no connectivity between the purification plant and the demand 
node. In addition, the flow rate at the demand node is a function of 
pressure and is reduced when the pressure is below a certain threshold, 
which is set to 10 m based on the literature [50]. Moreover, low pressure 
increases the possibility of the potable water becoming contaminated in 
the water network. Some regions allow non-potable water (low pressure 
nodes) to be transported to customers while ensuring that customers are 
informed of methods to treat the water, whereas others choose to 
terminate the supply of water with poor quality [45]. This study adopts 
the latter strategy, in which poor quality water is not delivered to 
households, and assumes that water has a poor quality at demand node i 
if the pressure falls below a minimum value (10.6–14.1 m) [47,48]. 
Therefore, the availability of TW at every demand node i is a function of

the distribution centre to stores, malls, markets, etc. by trucks, and these 
logistics heavily depend on the condition of the transportation system.

3.2.3. Availability analysis of emergency water 
Governments or water companies would take measures to ensure EW 
supply in the aftermath of a disaster if TW is unavailable. For instance, in 
2015 Nepalese earthquake and other disasters, EW supply is proved to 
be an essential water source for residents [33,45]. The primary approach 
is using water trucks to deliver the treated water from EW sources 
(Fig. 2) to temporary water tanks in shelters, such as wide-area shelters, 
accommodation shelters, and nearby city parks. Thus, the amount of 
water that can be provided for people in emergency shelters is a function 
of the emergency strategies, transportation system, water purification 
capacity of EW sources, and the number of water trucks. In order to 
estimate the maximum amount of EW that an individual can access in a 
shelter, we adopt an equal water distribution strategy. This is because 
the emergency response strategy that governments usually choose is to 
provide equal amounts of water to every individual considering the 
limitation of resources [53,54].

4. Osaka case study 
In this section, the proposed model is used to analyse the societal 
impact of infrastructure disruption based on real-world and inferred 
data of the city of Osaka. Osaka is one of the most populous cities in 
Japan, with a total estimated population of 2.69 million. As mapped in 
Fig. 3, there are 24 districts in Osaka and the size of the population grid 
is 500 × 500 m (analysis units). In addition, Osaka is threatened by 
various natural hazards, such as the earthquakes, floods, typhoons, and 
storm surges, which can lead to the failure of CI and affect residents’ 
daily lives. This study conducts a scenario analysis to address the 
following two questions: (1) How should the proposed model be applied 
to evaluate the societal impact due to post-disaster infrastructure 
disruption in Osaka? (2) How does societal impact change due to 
different disruption severities?

4.1. Data description 
The Osaka water supply system is operated by the Osaka Municipal 
Waterworks Bureau (OMWB), and its main components are presented in 
Fig. 4. Three water purification plants, with a total purification capacity 
of 2,430,000 m3/d, provide the TW for the whole city. The TW is 
distributed by 12 distribution plants (including the storage reservoir and 
pump station) to ensure the stability of the water supply (Fig. 4 (a)). The 
total length of the distribution pipelines is approximately 5200 km. 
There are 611 demand nodes, in which the base demands are estimated 
by multiplying the number of people in the service area by hourly 
average water consumption per person (Fig. 4 (a)). The transportation 
network data and commercial facility data (e.g. convenience stores, 
supermarkets, and malls) of Osaka indicated in Fig. 4 (b) were derived 
from OpenStreetMap [57]. The population grids, emergency shelters, 
and distribution centres were obtained from Japanese National Land 
Numerical Information [58]. 
4.2. Illustrative case analysis 
To quantitatively estimate the societal consequence caused by 
infrastructure failures, this study assumes the following disaster sce­
nario: a strong typhoon and its induced storm surge struck Osaka in 
Japan. Kunijima purification plant was disrupted due to contamination 
by the storm surge, and the electricity in Higashiyodogawa district was 
interrupted, which lead to the malfunction of the pumps in the Kunijima 
distribution plant. Additionally, this study assumes that 5% of roads 
were disrupted randomly, which means that 5% of road segments (7588 
road segments) were selected randomly from all roads (151,769 seg­
ments in total) and closed. Similarly, 1% of commercial facilities were 
selected randomly to suffer from direct damage. These disruptions were 
applied to the proposed methodology to evaluate the societal impact 
within Osaka. 
4.2.1. Tap water estimation 
The water supply network for the city of Osaka was established using 
the EPANET-EMITTER software, and the pressure at each of the demand 
nodes was simulated under normal conditions. The simulation results 
are presented in Fig. 4 (a) and, to some extent, they can be validated by 
Javanbarg’s research [59]. Then, considering the disruption scenario, 
which is the failure of the Kunijima purification plant and disruption of 
the pump station, the structure of the water network was updated ac­
cording to the mapping algorithm shown in Table 2. Then, we recon­
ducted the hydraulic simulation and updated the corresponding water 
pressure in each demand node. Finally, the availability of TW in each 
demand node and in each service area was evaluated according to Eq. 
(2). As indicated in Fig. 5, the red areas are the regions without TW 
under the assumed disaster scenario settings; however, these results 
cannot be verified because Osaka has not experienced the interruption of 
TW supply due to disaster. 
4.2.2. Bottled water estimation 
In the analysis of BW, only commercial facilities in areas without TW 
were evaluated (Fig. 6). We used ArcGIS to build the transportation 
network of Osaka and then perform an Origin-Destination (OD) cost 
analysis to calculate the connectivity from distribution centres to stores, 
considering the failure of transportation (5%) [55]. It is worth noting 
that the disruption of transportation was modelled by generating barrier 
points in randomly selected road or bridge segments. Combined with the 
availability of electricity and direct damage (1%) of commercial facil­
ities, the availability of BW in every facility was calculated using Eq. (3). 
Commercial facilities do not have certain serviceable areas that cover 
the whole region; hence, we assumed that if there was an available store 
in the population grid, the residents in that grid could access BW. 
Finally, the population grids with available BW were identified, and 
these are shown as green grids in Fig. 6. 
4.2.3. Emergency water estimation 
As for areas without TW, the OMWB will deliver EW from the 
Niwakubo purification plant to the corresponding shelters, such as parks 
and accommodation shelters, via 21 water trucks (shown in Fig. 7). 
Similar to the connectivity analysis in the BW model, the travel costs 
from purification plants and shelters were calculated using an OD cost 
analysis based on the Osaka transportation network with 5% disrup­
tions. Other important details were as follows: the volume of each truck 
was 4 m3; the speed of the trucks was assumed to be 40 km/h; the pu­
rification capacity of Niwakubo for EW was 128,000 m3, which is the 
total capacity minus the water supplied in the water network; the pop­
ulation served by each shelter was estimated by summing the number of 
people in its service area that were assigned by the proximity principle; 
the water inventory for the park was 0; and the water inventory in ac­
commodation shelter was estimated by multiplying 0.2, the capacity of 
the shelter, and 3 L/d. Then, these parameters were applied to the 
emergency equal distribution model to estimate the maximum water 
quantity that every individual could access in shelters (availEW
k = 5 L/d). 
Finally, the EW quantity (5 L/d) was mapped to each grid according to 
the service area of the shelter. The EW available areas are shown in 
Fig. 7. 
4.2.4. Estimated societal impact of infrastructure disruptions 
By integrating the availability of TW, BW, and EW, the proposed 
methodology can estimate the maximum water quantity that each in­
dividual can access in each grid (Eq. (1)). There were three possible 
water quantities under this disaster scenario (shown in Fig. 8), including 
5 L (only EW was available), 8 L (EW and BW were available), and 100 L 
(TW is available). From the societal impact analysis, the people’s needs- 
satisfaction levels in each grid can be derived according to the quanti­
tative relationship in Table 1. The spatial distribution of the societal 
impact in Osaka due to infrastructure disruptions was estimated and 
mapped in Fig. 8. Overall, 84.6% of residents were in Level 1; all the 
needs of these people could be satisfied and daily water-related activities 
could be achieved. However, 15.4% of residents were in Level 3; only 
the survival needs of these people could be satisfied and some of their 
hygiene-related activities could not be achieved. 
In general, as presented in Figs. 5 and 8, the societal impact pattern 
in Osaka showed the same characteristics with the spatial distribution of 
TW availability. This means that the impact was dominated by the 
availability of TW under this disaster scenario. Moreover, the avail­
ability of BW and EW, which are related to the adaptive capacity of 
infrastructure, were crucial in mitigating the negative societal impact in 
TW shortage areas because 15.4% of residents would have been exposed 
to Level 5 if BW and EW were not incorporated. Furthermore, by 
comparing the effects of EW and BW on societal impact (Figs. 6–8), it is 
found that the availability of EW was more important than BW in the 
regions without TW. This is because people’s needs satisfaction could 
achieve Level 3 with the available EW (5 L/d) regardless of whether BW 
was integrated or not. 
4.3. Sensitivity analysis

scenarios on societal impacts. We focused on the electricity and trans­

water. In mechanism, disruptions to the electricity system can lead to

cause the unavailability of TW. They can also disrupt commercial fa­
cilities and lead to unavailability of BW. Thus, analysing the disruption 
of electricity helps examine the effects of pumping station malfunctions 
and the unavailability of commercial facilities on societal impact. In 
addition, different damage ratios for transportation were simulated 
because the transportation system is essential for logistics, and trans­
portation disruptions lead to inaccessibility and increased travel costs,

4.3.2. Effects of transportation disruption on societal impact 
For the transportation disruption sensitivity analysis, the benchmark 
was also the illustrative scenario (in Section 4.2), and this section 
assigned different disruption ratios (1%, 5%, 10%) for road segments in 
order to check the effect of transportation disruption. For each disrup­
tion ratio, transportation network components were disrupted randomly 
with equal failure probability. Additionally, to avoid the uncertainty of 
random disruption, 100 cases were simulated for each disruption ratio to 
explore the average and worst societal impact (shown in Table 4). 
The results indicate that the average impact increased slightly with 
increasing disruption ratio. This was mainly reflected in the increasing 
percentage of residents in Level 4 (0.01% to 1.2%) and Level 5 (0.01% to 
0.36%). The percentage of residents in Level 1 remained unchanged 
(84.59%) because transportation disruption did not affect the TW sup­
ply. With increasing disruption ratio, the percentage of residents in Level 
3 gradually declined, and more people’s survival needs could not be 
satisfied. The reason for this phenomenon was the reduction in EW and 
BW caused by increasing travel costs and the inaccessibility of emer­
gency shelters or commercial facilities. 
Comparing the average results with the worst-case results, we found 
that the uncertainty of societal impact increased with the higher 
disruption ratios. As shown in Table 4, when the disruption ratio was 
10%, there were significant differences between the average societal 
impact (13.84% in Level 3 and 1.20% in Level 4) and the worst case 
societal impact (6.36% in Level 3 and 8.43% in Level 4). In this typical 
case study, the societal impact of transportation disruption was not 
significant; however, it is still possible that a high societal impact could 
occur under a higher disruption ratio. This result, coupled with the study 
by Mostafavi et al. [33], emphasises the importance of the trans­
portation system in water supply resilience. 
5. Discussion and conclusion 
In a disaster event, disruptions to CI directly reduce the supply of life- 
supporting resources/services, which leads to the unsatisfaction of res­
idents’ physiological needs and further causes societal impact. However, 
societal impact cannot be easily quantified due to the complexity of 
connecting it with infrastructure disruptions. The dependency and 
adaptive capacity of CI systems in a disaster event increase the diffi­
culties in accurately estimating negative effects on society. To fill this 
gap, this study focused on the water analysis, and proposes a theoretical 
framework and integrated methodology to estimate the corresponding 
societal impact due to related infrastructure disruptions. The integrated 
methodology combines social and engineering aspects. From the anal­
ysis of social dimensions, a quantitative relationship between needs 
satisfaction level and available water quantity is established. As for 
engineering analysis, the available water quantity for individuals under 
disruptions of infrastructure is estimated using the integrated water 
quantity estimation model. This model includes availability analyses of 
TW, BW, and EW based on the hydraulic characteristics of the water 
network, failure causes of the commercial facilities, and the equal dis­
tribution principle of emergency services, respectively. As such, the 
severity and spatial distribution of the societal impact caused by a 
disaster can be calculated. 
The illustrative case study using data from the city of Osaka 
demonstrated that the spatial distribution of societal impact can be 
derived from the proposed methodology, which can provide essential 
information for emergency management. The results indicate that the 
impact was dominated by the availability of TW, followed by EW and 
BW under this specific scenario. Additionally, the results highlight the 
importance of the adaptive capacity of water supply infrastructure and 
are consistent with previous work [33]. Specifically, 15.41% of the 
residents of Osaka were in Level 3 (survival needs can be met) for our 
illustrative case study, but these people would have been in Level 5 (no 
needs can be met) if TW and BW were not included. However, in prac­
tice, several countries have developed emergency response strategies to 
deliver EW to water-shortage areas. For example, the distribution of EW 
in Osaka city of Japan is based on the equality principle, and other 
policies can be examined according to the background of different cities. 
Sensitivity analyses were conducted to investigate the effects of 
disrupted CI on society and to validate the proposed model. The societal 
impact became larger with increasing severity of power and trans­
portation disruptions. Specifically, when power outage areas increase 
from one to eight districts, the percentage of residents in Level 4 or Level 
5 increases from 0% to 12.74%. As for transportation disruption, in the 
worst case, the percentage of residents in Level 4 or Level 5 increase 
from 0.26% to 9.05% when the disruption ratio increased from 1% to 
10%. Furthermore, compared with road segment failure (15.41% of 
residents were not in Level 1), power outages affected more people 
(29.18% of residents were not in Level 1); because the availability of TW 
could be influenced by the disrupted electricity instead of transportation 
disruption. However, it is still possible to have a large impact (8.43% of

The proposed framework and methodology can provide valuable 
empirical information for city managers regarding disaster prevention/ 
mitigation and emergency rescue, allowing them to maximise the pos­
sibility of maintaining residents’ normal activities and reduce the 
negative societal impact. Although this study validates the proposed 
methodology using a water analysis in a disaster scenario, the frame­
work can also be applied to other essential goods/services analyses. 
Future work could extend our proposed model by considering the 
evacuation of residents after disasters, because regional population 
redistribution would affect the availability of TW and EW. The post- 
disaster recovery of different infrastructure systems could also be 
included in this model, such that the dynamic societal impact and re­
covery strategies in a city can be explored. Analysis on other basic in­
dividual survival needs (e.g. food, housing, and medical services) under 
disruptions should be integrated into the societal impact evaluation. To 
predict the societal impact more accurately, failure probability of 
infrastructure components and different emergency policies should also 
be incorporated and simulated in future studies.
