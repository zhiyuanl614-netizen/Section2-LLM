a b s t r a c t
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) are the backbone of critical infrastructure found in
power, water, manufacturing and other industries. An ICS controls a physical plant
through the use of sensors and actuators. A Historian sits on a plant network and
receives, parses, and saves data and commands transmitted over the network, across the
Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), sensors and actuators. This data has at least two
uses. One use is to check for any process anomalies that may occur due to component
failures and cyber attacks. The other use of this data, and the focus of this work, is to
serve as critical input to off-line activities such as forensic analysis. A cyber attack on
the Historian could jeopardize any forensic analysis be it for maintenance, or discovering
an attack trail. In this work, a novel architecture, named ICS-BlockOpS, is proposed to
secure plant operational data recorded in the Historian. ICS-BlockOpS is designed to
enhance data security along two dimensions: immutability and redundancy. An integrity
checking mechanism, in combination with blockchain, is used to ensure data integrity.
Data redundancy is achieved by applying an efficient replication mechanism and en-
ables data recovery after an attack. A prototype implementation of ICS-BlockOpS uses
Ethereum blockchain in the local network as part of the tamper proofing mechanism.
The implementation is in an operational six-stage water treatment plant. The underlying
design ideas are generic and could be applied to other ICS as well.

1. Introduction
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) [1] are interconnected cyber–physical systems (CPS) for mission critical tasks. Examples
of such systems include nuclear power plants, power grid, water treatment plants, and autonomous vehicles. Many such
systems are expanding their communication capabilities for flexibility and ease of management. The use of networked
computers in an ICS leads to an inherent vulnerability that continues to be exploited by individual, group and state actors.
Recent incidents, such as the Ukraine power blackout [2] and Stuxnet [3], have raised the importance of cyber security in
ICS.
An anomaly detector is a mechanism that monitors the state of an operational plant and detects unusual behavior
referred to as anomalies. A variety of machine learning algorithms have been used to discover anomalies [4–6]. The data
logs include sensor measurements of the plant state. This data can also be used for root cause analysis of failures in an
ICS. Forensic analysis could also be used to determine if a hostile action occurred against the plant controllers [7]. Thus,
it is of utmost importance that data in the Historian be secured.

Data in a Historian could be damaged intentionally, such as via a cyber attack, or mistakenly. Cyber attacks could be
launched in various ways even when the Historian is secured using traditional means such as through password protection.
For example, a malicious attacker, or an insider, could enter a plant network, alter the data log and thus prevent the
determining of what an attacker did during the attack [8].
To secure plant data in the Historian, the focus is primarily along two dimensions: immutability and redundancy. In
addition, the solution ought to be usable, reliable, and consistent. These properties imply that (a) isolation of a Historian
from the network is impractical; the solution (b) must not affect any control logic of the plant, and (c) must be deployed
and integrated with the existing ICS.
A novel approach is proposed that ensures data integrity without the need for protection using firewalls. Blockchain
technology is used together with data registration [9]. The proposed system, referred to as ICS-BlockOpS, uses efficient data
replication logic to provide data redundancy. ICS-BlockOpS is designed to protect a Historian against attacks and failures.
The replication logic used is inspired by the HDFS (Hadoop Distributed File System) architecture and is combined with
blockchain. ICS-BlockOpS addresses two major data requirements: immutability and redundancy. Separate Historians are
created to collect data from different parts of the plant that uses a distributed ICS. The data from each Historian is time
stamped and registered in the blockchain ledger. The time stamped data can be validated to ensure data integrity. The time
stamped data is replicated in the Historian network for recovery. An on-chain permissioning system is included through
the use of cryptographic key pairs. Details of ICS-BlockOpS including the method used, architecture, implementation, and
results from an experiment, are described in the remainder of this paper.
Novelty: The novelty of ICS-BlockOpS lies in the selection of the application domain, the replication architecture used
for retrieving tampered data, and the design and implementation of a real-time checker that monitors the data in the
historian.
Contributions: (a) A secure decentralized Historian database for ICS. (b) A method based on blockchain, and its application
to a water treatment plant, to secure real-time process data generated during plant operation. (c) Generalization of
ICS-BlockOpS to ICS found in other critical infrastructures.
Organization: The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents information about ICS and security
requirements of the Historian. Potential solutions to data integrity are in Section 3. Section 4 describes potential solutions
to data redundancy available in the literature. ICS-BlockOpS is briefly discussed in Section 5. Implementation details and
experimental results are in Sections 6 and 7, respectively. Section 8 presents related work. Conclusion and future work
are in Section 9.
2. Industrial control systems
An Industrial Control System (ICS) is a combination of control systems and instrumentation used for process control. In
general, ICS includes a Supervisory Control and data Acquisition (SCADA) and one or more programmable logic controllers
(PLCs). ICS can be found in chemical plants, manufacturing units, power systems, and oil and gas systems. An ICS [1] also
includes physical, control, and network devices. The PLCs in an ICS can be viewed collectively as a distributed control
system that transforms the state of the process through the use of sensors and actuators. Complex CPS, especially those
found in critical infrastructure, consist of multiple stages where each stage is controlled by a dedicated PLC. In such
distributed control systems, PLCs communicate to determine the state of the other parts of the system not directly under
their control. SCADA, HMI, engineering workstation and the Historian are connected to level 1 network for monitoring.
The logic programmed in the PLCs determines the steps that the physical process should take at any point in time based
on the current state. The Historian in an ICS can be viewed as a time series database. It stores the sampled plant state in
a time series where data is indexed by time.
Requirements: High level requirements were set for ICS-BlockOpS prior to initiating its design. These requirements are:
(1) Tamper-proof or data integrity of Historian database for ICS systems. (2) High availability of the operational data for
the analysis of cyber attacks as well as maintenance work. (3) Redundancy to recover the operational data after any cyber
attack or system/components failure. Blockchain was considered as an effective structure for meeting the requirements
above. The strength of blockchain in enhancing ICS resilience should be evident from the experimental assessment of its
effectiveness (Section 7) in meeting the above requirements.
3. Potential solutions for data integrity
There are different potential solutions that can provide data integrity. Cryptographic hashes are mostly used for
integrity checking. However, each solution has its own advantages and disadvantages.
Solution 1: One hash for a vector: A hash for each vector is generated by using a cryptographic hash function, e.g., sha256
[10]. Each hash is stored in another database either on the same or a different computer. An attacker can bypass, who
knows this mechanism overwrite the hash while damaging the vector.
Solution 2: A hash chain with each hash for a vector: A cryptographic hash for each vector is generated as in solution-1
discussed above. However, in this case, the input of cryptographic hash function is not only the vector, but it takes the
hash of the previous vector together with the values of the current vector. However, if the attacker overwrites the entire
chain of hashes to remain undetected, that this solution cannot detect the attack. When the attacker does not change

anything inside the vector database but deletes some or all the hashes. Then, both solution-1 and solution-2 cannot ensure
the data integrity due to a lack of validity.
Solution 3: Decentralized hash chain: The hash generation mechanism of solution-2 is retained but the storage for the hash
chain is architecturally decentralized and logically centralized. This implies that identical hash chains are stored in many
computers instead of a single computer. However, several challenges arise in the design of such a decentralized storage:
How can the hash chain be securely synchronized across the network? How can the correct hash chain be selected when
there are different hash chains on different computers? These questions have already been answered by making use of
blockchain [11].
Solution 4: Blockchain as decentralized hash storage: Blockchain is the underlying technology of bitcoin [11], a distributed
ledger. Each block may contain one or more transactions. Since each block contains the hash of the previous block, it is
almost impossible for an attacker to change the data (transactions) inside each block.
Blockchain is architecturally decentralized and logically centralized. A single identical chain of blocks is stored on
many computers across the network. The synchronization of the blocks among these computers is achieved by utilizing a
consensus rule. The choice of this consensus rule can change the behavior of the blockchain decentralization, i.e., public or
private blockchain. The cryptographic chain of blocks together with a consensus protocol protects the blockchain against
cyber attacks.
Blockchain provides tamper proofing of transactions using the underlying architecture and can be used to track digital
assets, i.e. transactions that contain any information. This is achieved by applying a transaction-based state machine
running on top of the blockchain. Transactions, in this case are various inputs to trigger the state machine inside
blockchain. The state variables of this state machine are changed by processing the inputs of the transactions. The state
machine stores its state variables inside the blocks of the blockchain. Blockchain provides tamper proofing of these state
variables. This transaction-based state machine was first introduced by Ethereum [12] and termed as a smart contract.
From the above discussion, it is claimed that by using blockchain, and various consensus rules, one can solve the
above mentioned decentralization problem. Moreover, any type of data can be stored inside the blockchain using smart
contracts. The decentralized hash chain architecture can be implemented more easily by using blockchain. The hashes of
vectors are stored in the blocks of the blockchain as in Fig. 1. Hash chaining and decentralization, are accomplished in the
underlying blockchain layer. Thus, only the individual hashes of vectors need to be stored inside the blockchain that is
already tamper proof. This concept of using blockchain as an underlying layer to provide data integrity is shown in Fig. 2.
Other than those mentioned above, there exist other mechanisms to provide data integrity of the historian. Data
diodes [13] are solutions to provide read only databases. Data diodes provide unidirectional data flow from the underlying
network layer. However, using data diodes in a Historian network may affect the current network architecture of ICS in
legacy plants; BlockOpS does not require any change to the underlying network architecture while providing the security
of data logs.
Another method configures a Historian to add values to its database, and does not allow any other operation on the
data. However, an attacker may not use the Historian engine to overwrite data. Instead, the attacker may change the values
at the lower layer of the OS. Such manipulation would lead to a significant challenge in detecting data manipulation. The
method used in BlockOpS will also detect any changes made through the lower layer of the OS; the hashes are difficult
to compromise as they are stored in blockchain.
4. Potential solutions for data redundancy
It is important to provide redundancy in storing operational data from an ICS to enable data recovery. In ICS, the data
is recorded inside one or more Historians according to the size of the plant. Methods to provide data redundancy, and
their pros and cons, are discussed in the following.
Solution 1: Traditional backup systems: A backup system can periodically copy data from a database into an archival file
and onto another storage device. The purpose of such a backup is to recover the original data after consequent to a data
loss event [14].
Solution 2: Static replication In static replication [15] data is replicated in real-time from a Historian into another similar
device. However, such as a system makes it challenging to use a large number of Historians as each Historian needs to store

attacker can change the data in all replicated Historians or the attacker may selectively wipe-out data inside all Historians.
Solution 3: Dynamic replication: In dynamic replication data to be replicated is sliced into smaller data blocks. Several
computers, known as nodes, in the network replicate the data blocks. Each data block is replicated to a set of dynamically
selected nodes among all nodes in the network. The number of nodes to be replicated can be configured, e.g., each data
block is replicated to three different nodes such as in the Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS) [16] (Fig. 3).
In the HDFS architecture, there are Namenode, Datanodes, and client. Datanodes are responsible for storing data blocks
of files whereas Namenode is responsible for replication of data blocks across different data nodes. A client provides an
interface for the end user to manipulate files in the system. Each file in an HDFS is stored as a sequence of blocks. All
blocks in a file, except the last block, are of the same size. The blocks in a file are replicated dynamically across different
data nodes in a large cluster. The data nodes, that store a copy of a data block of a file, are listed in metadata of that file
inside the Namenode. The block size and replication factor are configurable per file.
In ICS, the above mentioned dynamic replication is used across different Historians in the plant network. Data inside
the Historians is in the form of vectors of sensor and actuator values collected as multiple time series. Each vector from
one Historian can be replicated to other Historians, labeled as replica, across the plant network. As an example, suppose
consider 100 Historians in the plant. Vector 1 of Historian 1 is replicated to Historian 3 and 4, while vector 2 is replicated to
Historian 6 and 10. When a vector is tampered, it can be recovered from any of the replica nodes. An attacker must know
the location of all replicas of a vector that is to be damaged. When such information is not available, all Historians must
be attacked making the task extremely challenging for an attacker. Among the three redundancy mechanisms, dynamic
redundancy is considered superior to others described above and is used in ICS-BlockOpS.
5. Architecture and design of ICS-BlockOpS
Blockchain is used in a decentralized hash storage architecture to provide data integrity. Resilience to data integrity
attacks is achieved through dynamic replication. Data from each stage of a distributed control plant is saved to the
corresponding Historians. Measurements from sensors are collected as vectors and saved as time series. One could

generate the hash of each vector and store it inside the blockchain. However, doing so for large plants, and thus large
vectors, would lead to significant storage overhead. The overhead is a combination of the size of the hash and that of the
block in the blockchain. To reduce the storage overhead, the hash is generated for groups of vectors within a time range,
e.g., using vectors collected at one minute intervals.
Grouping of vectors makes it easy to perform dynamic replication across multiple storage nodes. Each group of vectors
is replicated to different storage nodes in the plant network. As in HDFS, the storage nodes, with a copy of each vector
group, must be recorded on some media to enable recovery. As the hash of each vector group is stored inside the
blockchain, the corresponding storage nodes for each group can be stored inside the blockchain together with the hash.
The hash, time range, and a list of storage nodes together form the index for a vector group. The indices are stored inside
the blocks of the blockchain. Data from each PLC in a distributed control plant is stored inside the corresponding storage
node and dynamically replicated to other storage nodes. Fig. 4 shows the architecture of ICS-BlockOpS. This architecture
can also work with Industrial Personal Computer (IPC). Data from IPC can be feed into the storage nodes of the ICS-
BlockOpS like PLCs. The usage of ICS-BlockOpS is not limited to specific plant with unique data. The data can be from any
domain such as water and power.
Storing indexes inside the blocks of the blockchain is carried out by using smart contracts. Smart contracts can hold
state variables of any type such as integer, string, array, mapping, and structure. A structure data type was used for an
index that contains the hash of the vector group, the time range, and the list of storage nodes. The smart contract can
be initialized with an empty array of indexes with dynamic length. When the storage nodes store the indexes for vector
groups, the indexes are appended to the array.
A smart contract in ICS-BlockOpS contains a function to store new indexes. This function accepts parameters including
the hash of vector group and the time range. To store an index for a vector group, a storage node sends a transaction that
contains a function call to the smart contract with the corresponding parameters. The replication of that vector group
occurs after the index is successfully stored inside the blockchain by means of the smart contract’s state variable.
In ICS-BlockOpS, each storage node has its own cryptographic key pair. The public keys of storage nodes are marked
as authorized to store indexes in the smart contract at the time of deployment. The storage nodes then send transactions
to store indexes of vector groups. This method prevents the storage of indexes received from unknown resources.
In summary, ICS-BlockOpS is designed to make use of blockchain in an efficient way to provide immutable hashes for
integrity checking of operational data. The redundancy of the vectors is also achieved in an efficient way by using dynamic
replication.
6. ICS-BlockOpS implementation
The implementation and deployment of ICS-BlockOpS on an operational ICS is explained next. ICS-BlockOpS uses
blockchain to store indexes of vector groups from storage nodes as mentioned earlier. Therefore, the implementation
includes the infrastructure setup of the blockchain network, the smart contract development, and its deployment. The
design and implementation of the storage nodes, that constitute the key parts of the decentralized storage system, is also
discussed. Details of how ICS-BlockOpS is deployed in the plant follow the description of its implementation.

6.1. Blockchain infrastructure setup
Blockchain platform selection: There exist several implementations of blockchain. Most of the implementations support
smart contracts, i.e., transaction based state machines, with different programming languages to develop contracts.
Ethereum [12,17] is an implementation of blockchain with a state machine. There exist several tools to setup ethereum
blockchain network and deploy smart contracts on it. Therefore ethereum was selected for the implementation of ICS-
BlockOpS. Ethereum itself is implemented in different programming languages. Specifically, Go-ethereum1 is used in this
work. The detail design decisions of blockchain: aes-256 algorithm, the design of block, etc., are done by Ethereum, and
not the focus of this manuscript.
Blockchain deployment: Private ethereum blockchain is used in ICS-BlockOpS as the blockchain is maintained under one
ICS. To setup a private ethereum blockchain, ethereum accounts and cryptographic key pairs need to be created. The
accounts are generated by using the Go-ethereum application. This application generates an account with an encrypted
private key inside a JSON file. The ethereum address for the corresponding private key is also included in the file. This
address is used when configuring the genesis block.
After generating a genesis configuration file, a new blockchain is initiated by using it on different machines. A couple
of bootnodes are also setup in the network. All the blockchain nodes are configured to connect to the bootnodes and start
running. The bootnodes improve the peer discovery process of the blockchain nodes by sharing the address among nodes.
When all nodes are connected to each other, they start creating new blocks by voting consensus. The blockchain can now
accept all the transactions including the smart contract deployment transaction.
6.2. Smart contract design
In ICS-BlockOpS, indexes of the vector groups are stored inside the blockchain by using the smart contracts. Three
smart contracts are developed for the purpose of modularity and ease of maintenance. The relationship of these smart
contracts with the external system is shown in Fig. 5. These smart contracts are explained below.
Storage node contract: This contract is responsible for storing the list of storage nodes inside the network. Only the
ethereum account address of the storage nodes are stored inside the list. The list can be predefined at the time of contract
deployment. Also, the new storage nodes can be added to the list by using the storage node registration function of this
contract. This function can only be triggered by the owner of the contract, i.e., the one who has the account which is
hard-coded as the owner in this contract. The security of the registration can also be increased by making the smart
contract to request the permissions from more than one owner. There is also a read only function in this contract to
verify whether an account is listed as storage node or not. This function is referred to as the verification function.
Indexer contract: The indexes of the vector groups are stored inside this smart contract. Index registration function in
this contract accept three parameters; the hash of the vector group, the start and end timestamps of the time range.
Before creating a new index, the permissions of the sender are verified by using the verification function of the storage
node contract. If the sender’s address cannot be verified, i.e., the address is not in the list of the storage node contract, the
request is rejected and the index is not created. If it is verified, the storage nodes to store the replicate of the corresponding
vector group is selected by using a selection mechanism — currently round robin selection is used. The number of storage
nodes to select can be defined as the replication factor at the time of contract deployment. After selecting the storage
nodes, a new index is created using the index structure as mentioned in Section 5 with the sender’s address as the first
storage node followed by other previously selected as replicated nodes. Finally, a log event for the successful registration
of a new index is emitted from inside the smart contract. That log event notifies the replicated nodes to proceed with the
replication process.

6.3. Storage nodes
Multiple storage nodes are used in ICS-BlockOpS, together with the blockchain, to provide an immutable, decentralized
storage system. Each storage node stores the data in the form of time series vectors that are indexed inside the blockchain
together with the hash. The replication of groups of vectors is performed between different storage units in the plant
network. Used Javascript library called web3.js was to communicate with the blockchain. Currently, InfluxDB is used as
the time series database to store the operational data. A block diagram of the storage nodes, together with the procedural
steps, is in Fig. 6
Input handler: This program collects operational data from the corresponding PLC and stores it inside the database. This
program may not be needed when deploying ICS-BlockOpS technology on the existing Historian since the data may be
stored in some other ways into the Historian.
Register module: This module runs using a timer with a configurable time interval. After every time interval, the program
queries the group of vectors for a time range from the database. The time range is formed by using the previously recorded
last timestamp and current timestamp, i.e., the configured time interval for the timer in an ideal case. The vector at the
last timestamp is ignored in the database because at the time of querying the vector, the vector might not be written
fully inside the database. After that, the time stamp of the ignored vector is recorded to use as the starting time for the
next vector group.
After querying the vector group, the register module generates the hash of that vector group using the sha-256
cryptographic hash function. The generated hash, together with the time range, is used to call the index registration
function of the indexer contract. The function call is done by creating a transaction and sending it to the blockchain. This

Replica handler: This module is responsible for the replication of vector groups across different storage nodes. After the

Data gateway: It is a WEB-API service working as an access control layer for the database query. Every database query
needs to be signed with the sender’s private key and the signature is verified as in blockchain. When the verification
succeeds, the permissions for the sender are checked by using the storage node contract and operator contract inside the
blockchain. If the sender is not allowed for that query, the query is rejected.
Validator: It is a polling process running in each storage node. It reads the indexes that are registered inside the indexer
contract in the blockchain, from the first index to the currently last one. For each index, it queries the vectors from the
database with the corresponding time range. It regenerates the hash of that group of vectors and compares it with the hash
of the index coming from blockchain. If the hashes do not match, alert is raised and logs the violation as a record inside
the system. Thus, the system operator can check the validity of the data inside the storage nodes. The data replication
process is carried out when there is some data integrity violation.
6.4. Deployment in a Secure Water Treament (SWaT) plant
The approach sketched above was experimentally validated in an operational water treatment (SWaT) plant [18]). SWaT
(Fig. 7) is described next.
Stages in SWaT: Stage P1 controls the inflow of water to be treated by opening or closing a valve that connects the inlet
pipe to the raw water tank. Water from the raw water tank is pumped via a chemical dosing (stage P2) station to another
Ultrafiltration (UF) feed water tank in stage P3. In stage P3 a UF feed pump sends water, via UF unit, to a Reverse Osmosis
(RO) feed water tank in stage P4. In P4 an RO feed pump sends water through an ultraviolet dechlorination unit controlled
by a PLC in stage P4. Stage P5 contains a PLC to control the Reverse Osmosis (RO) unit that further filters the water using
a 2-stage RO process.
An independent workstation serves as a Historian. Data transmitted across the network is routed to the Historian
through a switch. What data is saved, and what is not, can be pre-programmed. For example, commands sent to a pump
by a PLC, or data sent to a PLC from a level sensor, can be saved in the Historian. This data is collected in real time
and can be used in real-time, or later, for analysis. One use of this data is to detect process anomaly or network traffic
anomaly. Another use is to conduct forensics in the event of a plant malfunction due to a component fault or a cyber

7. Experiments and results
Storage nodes contain operational data from six stages of SWaT in the form of vectors. These nodes ensure the integrity
of the stored vectors and provide data recovery using dynamic replication technique. It is important to note that ICS-
BlockOpS provides data integrity after the data is successfully stored inside the storage nodes. ICS-BlockOpS cannot detect
the integrity of data that enters from the plant and the attacker could remain undetected if the data is tampered prior to
entering the storage nodes. The design, launch, and response of various attacks on the Historian are described next.
7.1. Threat model
The attack points in an ICS include sensors, e.g., level sensor, and actuators, e.g., water pump. The Historian is the
attack point considered in this work. An attacker could access the Historian via the PLC communications network as well
as directly via the SCADA workstation in cases where access rights have been obtained, say, through social engineering.
Once attacked, an attacker could modify the Historian database. The following threat model is considered in this case
study. An attacker has the capability to change plant data, that includes both process data and network traffic, at the Historian.
Examples of such attacks are described next.
Selective modification: Upon gaining access, an attacker could selectively modify data in the Historian such that the true
state values are replaced by ‘‘seemingly correct state values’’. Doing so could make it difficult, or impossible, to correctly
analyze the causes and perpetrators of an attack. If such modification is done in real-time then a process anomaly detector,
such as the one proposed in [19], may not create an alarm when it should.

A sample of attacks designed and launched on the Historian in SWaT are listed in Table 2. Three attacks were designed

Attack launch on traditional Historian: In SWaT, the Historian uses OSI soft server.2 and PI WEB API client3 Data integrity
attacks were launched on operational data in the Historian using the PIWEBAPI client.
Attack on traditional Historian: Attacks on Historian were launched with the aim of performing modification (M), injection

Attack on ICS-BlockOpS: Attacks on ICS-BlockOpS were launched with the aim of performing modification, injection and

is the way in which attacks were launched, Influx HTTP API was used instead of PIWEBAPI.
7.3. Results
Traditional Historian: In the traditional Historian it was possible to modify (M,W,I) data. This is real time operational sensor
and actuator data. In this case, it is not possible to recover the data by any means.
ICS-BlockOpS: The outcome of experiments performed with ICS-BlockOpS implemented in the Historian is presented next.
Selective modification: As mentioned earlier, the validation process runs periodically on each storage node. After some time,
the process detects the modification of that group of vectors because of the difference in the hashes. Other than detection,
the damaged group of vectors can be recovered from the storage nodes that store a copy of that particular vector group
according to dynamic replication. The system, therefore, can successfully defend from such selective modification attack.
Data wipe out: The groups of vectors are indexed inside the blockchain. As the indices contain the time range of the vector
groups, the system can query the vectors from the database with these time range. If some or all of the data is wiped out
in a certain time range, the resulting hash of that data (even when empty) cannot be the same as the previous one stored
inside the blockchain. Therefore, the system can detect selective as well as all data wipe out attack.
Data injection: The injection of additional data within a particular time range can also be detected by comparing the
resulting hash with the stored hash. On the other hand, the system cannot assure the integrity of the data outside the
time range of all indexed vector groups. However, no use such un-indexed vectors can be ensured in the implementation.
Thus, the ICS-BlockOpS can detect data injection for all indexed vector groups.

for a vector stored inside the Historian. TS denotes the time instant when the vector is stored inside the Historian. TR is
the time instant before registering that vector inside a vector group. The duration between TS and TR can be adjusted in
the implementation. In the current implementation it is set to one minute. Any change made during TS and TR cannot be
detected in the current implementation of ICS-BlockOpS. Thus, this is the most vulnerable time interval for the Historian.
TB is the time when the block containing the registration of that vector is successfully added into the blockchain. Between
TR and TS, the vector can be changed, but that change can be detected when the registration of that is confirmed inside
the blockchain. However, there is also a chance that the registration can be canceled. TE denotes when the vector is
successfully replicated to other storage nodes. If the vector was changed before TE, the correct vector cannot be recovered
even though the modification can be detected. At TE, the vector is both immutable and stored redundantly among the
replica nodes.
Any damaged data in a Historian is recovered using BlockOps. In the work described here, the focus has been on using,
and not testing, blockchains for tamper proofing and recovery. There certainly is a need to ascertain the resilience of
BlockOps under attacks against blockchain. The method proposed in this work and implemented, makes it challenging for
an attacker to damage data in a Historian beyond recovery.
7.5. Discussion
It is important to note that the blockchain technology proposed here is complementary to existing technologies such
as firewalls and methods for anomaly detection [20], and several commercially available intrusion detection systems.
Blockchain inherently provides a platform for tamper-proofing data. Using blockchain as a data storage mechanism
improves confidence in the authenticity of data in the Historian. It is not fair to compare proposed ICS-BlockOpS with the
ones presented in Section 3 because they cannot ensure the integrity of data in historian.
The following properties of blockchain enabled the enhancement of the security of SWaT by securing its Historian:
(a) Decentralization4 with no infrastructural central point of failure. (b) Secure and auditable: it is possible to audit the

The remainder of this section is devoted to a discussion on requirements for a solution to secure a Historian and threats
that are not considered in the work described here.
Read/write efficiency: The read and write efficiency of the Historian is important because the data is used real time control
of the underlying physical process. However, in ICS-BlockOpS, data entering the database is not processed directly; only
data that is permanently stored inside the database is queried in the background and the hash registered inside the
blockchain. Therefore, the efficiency of the database is not affected.
Storage requirements: The storage space required for each blockchain node is the total size of the indices of vector groups
of the entire plant. This amount is manageable because the size of the hash and other information contained in an index
is small when compared to the size of a vector group. The number of indices can also be reduced by increasing the time
range or size of each vector group for large plants. However, it is difficult to detect the actual attack point when the time
range is too large. The most suitable time range and the size of each vector group can be adjusted according to the ICS
requirements. Thus, in ICS-BlockOpS, the size of data for which a hash is created can be configured to achieve a balance
between computational requirements, storage cost, and data security. If the size is too long, the hashes are calculated at
a slower rate and the storage space required in blockchain is reduced. However, the specific data damaged is difficult to
identify due to larger data size and vice versa. Size of data for hashing needs to be aligned with the net data rate of the
ICS. Indeed, balancing the various attributes of security and cost of implementation would be a challenge in environments
such as a smart grid where a large amount of data enters, possibly into multiple historians, at a rapid rate.
Computational cost and overhead: In blockchain, the Proof of Work consensus consumes significant computing power as
it is aimed at a public cluster. In ICS-BlockOpS, the private consensus, Proof of Authority, is used to avoid unnecessary
computational overhead.
Scalability in the number of nodes: ICS-BlockOpS is designed to work well with the decentralized Historians in large
distributed ICS. One could have hundreds of data nodes in such ICS that sample many sensors and manage many actuators.
The security of the decentralized Historians and blockchain is improved with the increasing number of nodes in the
network. The storage space requirement for each Historian is much smaller than for a single centralized Historian. The
storage space required for each blockchain node does not changing with the number of nodes because it depends only
on the total amount of data generated by the ICS.
Testing of blockchains: This work does not focus on security testing of blockchain, whether public or private. Hence,
any alterations to the potential attack surface due to the deployment of the proposed solution, though important, is
not discussed. Indeed, authorities who govern the private blockchain, could compromise and defeat the entire system.
In fact, authorities could alter the hash function so that it is easily invertible. Several such attacks are possible by the
authorities who may have access to the entire code base of ICS-BlockOpS. Hence, additional measures ought to be deployed
to prevent manipulation of the ICS-BlockOpS itself. Nevertheless, it would be a significant challenge for anyone, including
the authorities, to beat the system without data tampering being detected assuming that the implementation is as
proposed.
Limitation: The data modification attacks which occur before the data is saved in the historian cannot be covered
by the ICS-BlockOpS. If the attacker can take over all the private blockchain nodes, the data integrity cannot be
verified.
8. Related work
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to apply blockchain towards data security in industrial control
systems; closely related articles from ICS blockchain domains are mentioned in this section.
Security of CPS is gaining attention with the increase in attacks [3]. Framework for information flow security properties
is presented in [21]. Information flows between different physical processes that are not intended to exchange information,
should be prevented by multilevel security systems. Researchers [22] have shown how safety critical systems are
interconnected and complex. CUSUM based approach is proposed in [23] to limit the impact of attacks. [24] offers a
review of anomaly detection in smart grids. Data driven anomaly based approaches are discussed in [4]. Lee [25] presented
problems in computing and network technologies for full fledged design of emerging CPS. Attack models designed
specifically for CPS include a variety of deception attacks such as surge, bias, and geometric [26,27]. Several existing attack
models were borrowed from network security [28] and do not consider the physical aspects of a CPS.
In recent years blockchain is creating a revolution [11,29] in the economy sector. Christidis et al. used blockchain to
address the security and privacy issues in IoT devices [30]. Also, in [31], researchers improved the security of the energy
sector using blockchain. Bahga et al. proposed a blockchain platform for industrial IoT [32]. In ICS, a framework [33]
was proposed to protect modern power systems based on blockchain. In [33], the focus is on the communication
among metering infrastructure in advanced power grid infrastructure. On the contrary, the focus of ICS-BlockOpS is
on safeguarding data in the Historian. The result is a different implementation architecture of ICS-BlockOpS than that
proposed in [33]. In [33], the transferred data is broadcast to all other meter-nodes using public, private key pairs,
consensus and SHA algorithms which is not the case in ICS-BlockOpS. A blockchain-based solution is presented in [34]
to enhance security and privacy in smart factory with a private blockchain and a case study on IIoT.

9. Conclusions and future work
The proposed blockchain platform ICS-BlockOpS improves the security of ICS and, when needed, enables forensic
analysis with correct plant data. While ICS-BlockOpS has been presented in the context of SWaT, we believe it is broadly
applicable to other ICS as well. The work presented here can be extended across the following dimensions. (1) Use of
smart contracts while downloading and uploading the control logic/software updates in ICS. (2) Behavior analysis of the
blockchain platform when some nodes in a database have failed or compromised.
