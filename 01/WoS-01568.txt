Optimal Coordinated Operation of Interdependent
Power and Water Distribution Systems
Konstantinos Oikonomou
, Member, IEEE, and Masood Parvania
, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—With the rising electricity demand for freshwater
production, power and water sectors are becoming increasingly
interdependent. This vital link could create new opportunities
for power and water distribution system operators to coordi-
nate the operation of their systems and achieve operational cost
and energy efﬁciency beneﬁts. This paper proposes the ﬂexible
power-water ﬂow (FlexPWF) model for co-optimizing the oper-
ation of ﬂexible energy-intensive components in water systems
with the operation of both power and water distribution systems.
The FlexPWF model leverages the energy ﬂexibility provided
by water treatment and desalination plants in conjunction with
variable speed pumps and water storage tanks to minimize the
operating costs of interdependent power and water distribution
systems. The proposed model takes into account the operational
constraints of both power and water distribution systems, thus
coordinating their operation without endangering the reliable
supply of power and water to customers. The paper highlights
the merits of integrating water treatment and desalination plants,
where a detailed model is proposed to capture their dynamic
operation for producing variable amounts of freshwater and pro-
viding energy ﬂexibility. Simulations, conducted on the 33-bus test
distribution system and a 15-node test water distribution system,
show that the FlexPWF model effectively coordinates the oper-
ation, reduces the operating costs and improves the operational
metrics of both power and water systems.
Index Terms—Interdependent critical infrastructure, ﬂexibil-
ity, power-water nexus, distribution systems, optimization.

I. INTRODUCTION
A. Background and Problem Description
T
HE INTERDEPENDENCE between power and water
systems has recently attracted the attention of both power
and water system communities who seek to better under-
stand how a synergistic operation between their systems could
improve energy efﬁciency and reduce their operating costs [1].
This synergy is commonly referred to as the power-water
nexus in the sense that the efﬁcient use of one system depends
on the availability and efﬁciency of the other [2]. Water is
needed in all power generation stages, from thermal power
plant cooling to fossil fuel extraction, while power is required
to treat, desalinate and distribute large amounts of high-quality
water over long distances. For instance, in water-stressed states
like California, water facilities consume 20% of the state’s
total electricity for their operations [3]. As increasing pressures
of drought and population growth arise, the nexus between the
power and water sector is intensiﬁed.
Water treatment and desalination are energy-intensive
processes in which salt, contaminants, and other minerals
are extracted to produce potable water for urban consump-
tion, industrial use, and agricultural irrigation [4]. In the U.S.,
public drinking water systems supply potable water, which
is ﬁrst puriﬁed in water treatment facilities to more than
80% of the total population [5]. As of 2018, more than
300 municipal-level seawater and brackish water desalination
plants are identiﬁed with the ability to produce at least 25,000
gallons of potable water per day in the U.S., with countless
projects in the pipeline [6]. California, Florida, and Texas are
the leading states in the desalination sector in U.S., both in
terms of the installed capacity and the total number of operat-
ing plants [7]. In California, in drought-prone counties like
San Diego, the desalinated water serves up to 8% of the
freshwater demand [8]. In Florida, Tampa Bay seawater desali-
nation plant supplies about 10% of the region’s freshwater
needs [9]. In Texas, there are 46 municipal brackish water
desalination plants with capacities greater than 0.025 million
gallons per day [10]. Desalination technologies have also been
employed to process wastewater supplies. Currently, 7% of
wastewater in the U.S. is reused, but only a small portion for
potable uses [11].
A coordinated operation between power and water systems
could uncover signiﬁcant operational beneﬁts for both infras-
tructures [12]. For example, electric water pumps with control-
lable power consumption features could be utilized by local
grid operators and power utilities in the form of ﬂexible loads
to optimize the operation of both power and water distribu-
tion systems. In this regard, controllable assets from the water
sector (i.e., water desalination and treatment plants, variable
speed pumps), if properly managed, could reduce operating
costs of both power and water systems, improve the energy
efﬁciency of electric water pumps, reduce power and water
losses, as well as offer a range of services (i.e., ﬂexible energy,
frequency regulation, congestion management) that can help
grid operators counterbalance the variability and uncertainty
of renewable generation resources [4], [13].
Despite the operational beneﬁts that emerge from an inte-
grated operation of power and water systems, yet these systems
are managed, regulated, and operated independently from one
another. One barrier is the concern that the optimal opera-
tion decisions of one system may not always be supported by
the operational constraints of the other, which may adversely
impact the primary operation objective of the critical infras-
tructures for reliable delivery of power and water services.
For instance, although reducing the electricity consumption
of pumps during periods of high energy prices would lower
the operation cost of the power distribution system, it may
pose a threat to reliably supply the water demand of the water

water systems by leveraging the ﬂexibility of their controllable
assets and coordinate the power and water ﬂow interactions
while maintaining each system’s performance objectives.
B. Literature Review
The technical literature includes multiple recent frame-
works aimed at co-optimizing the operation of power and
water systems. A model is proposed in [14] that integrates
and optimizes the energy ﬂexibility of WDSs’ variable speed
pumps (VSPs) and water storage tanks in bulk power systems
operation. The work in [14] is further expanded in [15] by
proposing a comprehensive model to deﬁne and optimize
the feasible energy ﬂexibility ranges of WDSs. In [16], a
model is developed that co-optimizes the operation of power
and water distribution systems by employing the ﬂexibil-
ity of water storage tanks and ﬁxed-speed pumps, and a
quasi-convex hull relaxation technique is proposed to yield
computationally efﬁcient solutions. However, the use of ﬁxed-
speed pumps restricts WDS energy ﬂexibility and limits the
beneﬁts emerging from coordinating the operation of power
and water distribution systems. A model that co-optimizes the
operation of power and water distribution systems by tapping
on the ﬂexible operation of water storage tanks and variable
speed pumps is proposed in [17], where a feasible point pur-
suit successive convex approximation algorithm is adopted to
obtain near-optimal solutions. A model that co-optimizes the
operation of hydroelectric and desalination plants is proposed
in [18], however, without considering intertermporal operation
constraints and network topology of power and water systems.
A model is developed in [19] for deploying the energy ﬂex-
ibility of water treatment plants to improve the operation of
power distribution systems.
More recently, there has been signiﬁcant progress in solv-
ing WDS optimization problems of different objectives by
developing models based on geometric programming (GP)
approaches [20]–[22]. These models convert the non-convex
water ﬂow constraints into a series of convex GP prob-
lems that can be efﬁciently solved by commercial solvers
without prior knowledge of water ﬂow directions or max-
imum ﬂow rates. These models display several advantages
in providing tractable and globally optimal solutions for
large water networks, however, they are not yet implemented
in power-water nexus applications. Technical literature also
includes models that optimize the schedule of valves in WDSs,
which could provide another degree of freedom to improve
the coordinated operation of water and power distribution
systems [22], [23].
In a different approach, models are proposed to integrate the
energy ﬂexibility of WDSs in power demand response (DR)
programs and frequency regulation markets. These models aim
at helping water distribution system operators (W-DSOs) off-
set a portion of their electricity cost by providing services in
power markets. A model is proposed in [24] that optimizes
the schedules of VSPs to maximize W-DSO proﬁts in the
day-ahead energy market, while considering water demand
uncertainties. The works in [25]–[27] develop models that
leverage the ﬂexible operation of water storage tanks, pumps,
and desalination plants for maximizing W-DSO proﬁts in both
DR programs and regulation markets.
Although the technical literature includes models to co-
optimize the operation of power and water systems, as well
as to integrate the energy ﬂexibility of WDSs in electric-
ity markets, less attention has been given to investigating
and modeling the operational beneﬁts that water desalination
and treatment plants could provide to the joint operation of
power and water distribution systems. The models presented
in [14]–[27] do not consider the ﬂexibility provided by the
dynamic operation of the water desalination and treatment
plants in their co-optimization processes. Water treatment and
desalination plants are energy-intensive infrastructures that
can ﬂexibly operate via controlling the amount of freshwa-
ter produced by their ﬁltration systems. However, including
the ﬂexible operation of water plants increases the level
of complexity of the power-water ﬂow scheduling problem.
Therefore, computationally-efﬁcient mathematical models are
needed that capture and schedule the energy ﬂexibility of the
water plants as part of co-optimizing the operation of power
and water distribution systems.

II. FLEXIBLE POWER-WATER FLOW SCHEDULING MODEL
This section presents the proposed ﬂexible power-water ﬂow
(FlexPWF) model. In the proposed FlexPWF model, the power
and water distribution system operators (P-DSO and W-DSO)
coordinate the operation of their systems to minimize day-
ahead operation costs by tapping on the ﬂexible operation
of water treatment, desalination, and conveyance subsystems.
These subsystems, shown in Fig. 1, consist of VSPs, water
storage tanks, and ﬁltration membranes. VSPs can operate
ﬂexibly via rapidly adjusting their speed (therefore their power
consumption) to balance water load ﬂuctuations. In water con-
veyance subsystems, VSPs consume electricity to generate

long distances, in addition to storing freshwater in the stor-
age tanks. As the pumping energy required to deliver water
to storage tanks is ﬂexible via the utilization of VSPs, the
water pumping schedule into tanks could be optimized to pro-
vide ﬂexibility in power distribution systems. Water ﬁltration
membranes are part of both water treatment and desalina-
tion subsystems, which purify water for consumption [28].
In order to ensure freshwater productivity, VSPs are utilized
on the feed side of membranes to pressurize waste or saline
water. Considering the ﬂexible operation of VSPs, water treat-
ment and desalination plants are also candidates to provide
ﬂexibility to power distribution systems.
The proposed FlexPWF model coordinates the day-ahead
ﬂexible operation of water treatment, desalination, and con-
veyance subsystems in WDS with the operation of power
distribution systems, assuming that both power and water
ﬂows are in steady-state while all transient states (especially
those associated with the slower water ﬂow dynamics) have
damped out. In addition, we assume that appropriate intel-
ligent metering, telecommunication, control, and automation
systems at each facility and associated controllable equipment
(e.g., VSPs) are in place that enable a two-way communication
channel across the two systems.

III. SOLUTION APPROACH
The proposed FlexPWF model is a non-convex and non-
linear programming (NLP) problem with nonlinearities repre-
sented by the pressure head loss constraint (14), the VSPs
operation constraints (30) and (31), the water treatment or
desalination plants operation constraints (21) and (22), as well
as the complex power ﬂow quadratic constraint (8). NLP mod-
els, however, can be computationally intensive for large WDSs,
and a feasible global optimal solution may not always be
guaranteed. In our previous works [15], [26], we developed
models to approximate and replace the nonlinear functions
of constraints (14), (30) and (31) with convex piecewise lin-
ear alternatives, and showed that linear approximations lead
to fairly accurate solutions in much lower computation times.
The readers are referred to [15], [26] for linear models for
constraints (14), (30) and (31). In this paper, we present a
model that utilizes continuous and binary variables to linearize
water treatment and desalination constraint (21), in which the
freshwater recovery ratio Yt is substituted by (22).

applicability to larger systems.
V. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed the FlexPWF model that co-optimizes
the operation of power and water distribution systems by
leveraging the energy ﬂexibility provided by water treatment
and desalination plants as well as the variable speed pumps
and water tanks in order to minimize the operation costs of
both systems. The paper emphasizes on the additional opera-
tional beneﬁts and energy ﬂexibility that water treatment and
desalination plants could provide to interdependent power and
water distribution systems and a mixed-integer linear model is
proposed to capture its ﬂexible operation for providing variable
amounts of freshwater.
Simulation results of implementing the model on the 33-bus
test power distribution system and a 15-node water distribution
system show that the coordinated operation between power and
water distribution systems reduces their operation costs, espe-
cially in the case where water desalination plants operate at
varying freshwater recovery ratios. Further, the ﬂexible oper-
ation of water desalination plants allows W-DSOs to better
utilize the water storage capacity of tanks as the additional
energy ﬂexibility improves the coordination of electric water
pumps with water storage tanks’ charge and discharge sched-
ules. Multiple sensitivity analyses are conducted showing that
dynamic energy tariff schemes lead to larger cost savings for
the W-DSO as compared to traditional night tariff incentives.
