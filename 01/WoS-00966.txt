A B S T R A C T
This paper proposes the use of Combined Heat and Power systems for providing water and energy, and thermal
and electric utilities, in geographically isolated communities, where the interconnection with the grid is not
available. A common design practice is to implement diﬀerent energy storage technologies, usually batteries, for
reducing the gap between energy generation and electric consumption. However, in communities with low
incomes, it is not possible to use batteries due to the high capital costs. This work presents a new approach for
providing simultaneously electricity and water using the fresh water extraction and storage for reducing the gaps
between generation and consumption. The proposed approach is based on a mixed integer nonlinear pro-
gramming formulation, which accounts for the optimal selection of multiple combined heat and power tech-
nologies and the sizing of the water storage and pumping technologies. A multi-objective analysis is presented
considering as objective functions the economic costs and environmental impact associated with the space used
by the system and water consumption. A community in the mountains of Mexico is presented as case study. The
results show that it is possible to provide energy and water at low costs and reducing the environmental impact,
reaching a trade-oﬀbetween the considered objective functions.
1. Introduction
The economic and technological developments have generated
multiple satisﬁers to society. One of the most important is the electric
power supply. According with data of The World Bank, by 2012 around
85% of the world population had access to electricity [1]. It means that
there are communities that do not have electric utilities. The main
reasons why these populations lack elementary energy utilities are
varied. First, the geographical conditions that make diﬃcult to inter-
connect to the grid isolated communities [2]. Furthermore, sometimes
the needed energy is not signiﬁcant from an economic point view, and
to expand the grid to some far locations results in a non-proﬁtable in-
vestment [3]. In developing countries, these conditions are remarkable,
especially in rural areas where there is concentrated most of the po-
pulation without access to energy utilities [4]. The situation of these
communities is complicated considering the low-income of the local
population and the lack of other basic utilities (i.e., water) [5]. A water
pumping system is the typical used approach to extract, transport and
supply water; but, its operation is limited by the availability of energy
sources. In addition, low-income communities frequently have not en-
ough economic resources for covering the costs associated to pumping,
and frequently it is needed to transport the water from remote places
[6]. Considering this background, integral energy systems capable to
provide water and energy at low costs are needed for solving the si-
tuation of these isolated communities.
The oﬀ-grid and island operation of energy systems have been ad-
dressed using technologies based on renewable sources or conventional
systems as wind [7], solar [8], wave [9], solar-wind [10], Combined
Heat and Power (CHP) [11], biofuels [12], Rankine cycles [13] or hy-
brid systems using multiple technologies [14]. Despite the development
of these systems, it is possible to detect at least two signiﬁcant problems
for implementing the oﬀ-grid systems in low income and isolated
communities. The ﬁrst one is the availability of renewable resources,
which depends on the geographic conditions. In regions with not sig-
niﬁcant bio-resources available, or wind or solar low potentials, it is not
proﬁtable the use of renewable technologies [15]. This fact leads to the
second factor, which corresponds to the low income of the community.
Some distributed energy technologies are signiﬁcantly expensive, even
though their eﬃciency, for applying in low income communities, it is a
remarkable problem in developing countries [16]. Batteries have be-
come in the core of the energy storage on oﬀ-grid systems, reducing the
gaps between the energy generation and consumption [17]. However,

Combined Heat and Power systems (CHP) have a ﬂexible im-
plementation due to the use of conventional fuels (including coal,
natural gas, diesel and biofuels). Therefore, they do not depend on the
environmental and climatic conditions associated to the renewable re-
sources as wind, solar, geothermal or hydro systems [19]. The recent
advantages in shale gas exploitation have reduced the natural gas costs
[20]. Also, the use of bio-fueled CHP plants can stimulate the local
production of biofuels [21]. Environmental policies have developed
economic incentives for implementing technologies based on decen-
tralized energy systems, as CHP systems, for controlling the pollution
and to promote the economic development of disenfranchised com-
munities [22]. In addition, the design of small domestic CHP plants
with a low operating cost has been addressed in recent works for in-
terconnected systems [23] and for bio-fueled CHP systems [24].
For solving the electric storage problem, avoiding the battery costs,
in big centralized energy systems (as hydroelectric plants), pumped
water (obtained by using the surplus of energy) is stored as an upstream
for using later [25]. The fundamental principle of pumped hydroelectric
storage is to store electric energy in the form of hydraulic potential
energy [26]. However, the concept of polygeneration, as the use of
multiple fuels with simultaneous delivery of several utilities, leads to
the use of pumped water as domestic water and to consider it as a by-
product in small domestic CHP plants. This strategy can mitigate the
problems of water and energy supply inherent to low-income isolated
communities.
The design of polygeneration energy systems involves several issues,
including the optimal selection of prime movers [27], sizing of the
system [28], determining capital and operating costs [29], provide
energy demand proﬁles [30] and to account for the environmental
impact [31]. Recent works have focused on the water consumption in
centralized systems [32]. This problem, water-energy nexus, needs to
be considered on distributed energy systems as CHP units [33,34].

Diﬀerent technologies and operation proﬁles lead to diﬀerent levels of
water consumption in decentralized energy systems, considering the
remote location of water sources in isolated communities, where, in
addition, water is not an abundant resource, the water protection is an
important factor when the implementation of energy systems is con-
sidered [35]. In urban areas, the used land by distributed energy sys-
tems is a relevant issue due to the planning policies and available space
[36]. In isolated systems, out of urban areas, it depends of the geo-
graphic conditions, natural barriers as water bodies or mountainous
territory, or natural protected areas, national parks or reservations,
which do not have land available for using in infrastructure projects
[37]. In these cases, it is important to reduce the land used by the en-
ergy system reducing the environmental impact.
In this work, there is presented an optimization approach for de-
signing integrated oﬀ-grid CHP systems that provide water and energy
to small low-income communities. The water utilities include fresh and
hot water for domestic usage. The considered energy utilities are
electricity, fresh water and heat, associated to the hot water supply. For
showing the reduction in costs and the environmental impact, it is
presented a multi-objective mixed integer non-linear programming
model (MINLP), where there are considered aspects as the dynamics of
the thermal storage and the partial load operation of the CHP units.
Integer variables are used for deﬁning the prime mover of the system.
As options for selecting the prime movers, there are considered Internal
Combustion Engines (ICE), Fuel Cells (FC), Stirling Engines (SE) and
Microturbines (MT) fueled by natural gas. The model considers the
sizing of the CHP units, the thermal and fresh water storage units and
the pumping system. Due to the economic conditions of the considered
communities, there is considered as objective function the minimization
of the total annual cost (TAC). It includes the minimization of the size of
the system for reducing the space occupied by the system. Also, the
minimization of the used fresh water is considered as objective func-
tion, which allows to trade-oﬀthe water-energy nexus in decentralized
energy systems and reduce the environmental impact of the project. For
solving the proposed multi-objective problem, there is presented an
improved version of the weighting method based on the utopia point for
reaching the trade-oﬀbetween the multiple objectives considered. As
case study, real data from a small community in the mountainous zone
in the Paciﬁc Coast of Mexico is presented. The results show the fea-
sibility of the proposed system considering the trends in the operational
policy.
2. Proposed superstructure
The superstructure of the proposed conﬁguration is shown in Fig. 1.
The system provides electricity, fresh water and hot water to a set of
households located in an oﬀ-grid and isolated community with low
incomes (H). The selection of the central CHP units considers the four
more developed technologies: Internal Combustion Engine (ICE), Fuel
Cell (FC), Microturbine (MT) and Stirling Engine (SE) [38]. The local
extraction and transportation of water are performed through a
pumping system (P). The fresh water source is deﬁned by the nearest
available water body, which can be a lake, river or aquifer. The CHP
units are fed with natural gas from an external supply. The surplus of
electricity production is sent to the pumping system for extracting and
transporting water from local sources. The water obtained is stored in a
fresh water storage tank (FWST), which can be used for feeding the CHP
units, regulating the temperature of hot water supply or supplying fresh
water to the households. In this way, instead of using an electric energy
storage technology, as batteries, the water utility system is used for
storing a by-product of the electricity production. Besides, the heat
production is stored in a thermal storage tank (TST) that is used, in
combination with the FWST, for reducing the gaps between the energy
demands and the operation of the generation system and for storing the
surplus of energy production, which can be used in peak hours when
the capacity of the selected CHP unit is not enough for reaching the
utility demands.
The FWST combined with the pumping system allows the indirect
energy storage, but it includes additional purposes. The storage tank
can be used as the water treatment container for adding chlorine to the
water used in the community [39]. Diﬀerent diseases in low-income
communities in developing countries are associated to the lack of water

3.11. Multi-objective approach
Considering the circumstances of isolated communities in devel-
oping countries, the main objective function is to minimize the TAC.
This way, it is possible to reduce the negative economic impact in low-
income communities. The water-energy nexus is addressed through the
minimization of the water consumption of the system (SWT). Even
though the main objective of the system is to supply the fresh and hot
water to meet the household demands, it is possible to have a surplus in
the water used by the system. Despite that the natural water sources can
be recharged, the water surplus has a diﬀerent temperature when it is
returned to the water body. This way, the environmental impact of the
system, based on water exploitation, is reduced. Furthermore, in com-
munities located inside national parks, protected areas or ecological
reserves, it is important to reduce the used land. This condition can be
considered in the model through minimizing the occupied area for new
equipment (ST).
The simultaneous minimization of the objective functions is based
on the use of compromise solutions linked to the utopia point (UP).
First, all the objective solutions are individually minimized. The results
of this process deﬁne the lower bounds (LB) of the functions and, in
consequence, the utopia point (TACLB, SWT-LB, ST-LB). As the solutions
are in conﬂict, the highest values reached by the functions during the
individual optimization process deﬁne the upper bounds for the func-
tions and, similarly, the nadir solution (NS) (TACUB, SWT-UB, ST-UB).
These values are used for scaling the multi-objective function building

5. Results and discussion
The MINLP model was solved using the modelling language GAMS
[58] and the solver BARON [59]. The model consists of 19,611 con-
tinuous variables, 4 binary variables and 11,054 constraints. The so-
lutions were computed in an average CPU time of 2000 s for each case
with an average gap of 12%.
The results of the optimization process are showed in Table 3 ac-
cording with the preferences about the design objectives of the system.
These preferences have been designed according with three diﬀerent
criteria, the ﬁrst one is prioritizing a unique objective (Cases 1–3), the
second one is considering two criteria (Cases 4–6) and the last one is
where all the objectives are considered at the same level of importance
(Case 7). Cases 1–3 are used for conﬁguring the UP and the NS, which
are the reference for the rest of the results. Case 1 involves the mini-
mization of the economic objective (TAC), Case 2 the minimization of
the water consumption (SWT) and Case 3 the minimization of the land
used by the equipment (ST). Case 4 is the simultaneous minimization of
TAC and SWT without any importance on the value of the ST. Similarly,
Case 5 uses as objective functions the TAC and the ST and Case 6 ad-
dresses the minimization of SWT and ST.
The results obtained in Cases 1–3 show the conﬂict between the
considered objective functions. The minimization of fresh water con-
sumption (SWT), Case 2, leads to increments in the cost (TAC), which
increases 1.9% compared with Case 1, and land use (ST), which in-
creases 181% compared with Case 3. On the other hand, the mini-
mizations of TAC and ST represent increments of 41.3% and 358.6%
respectively in water consumption. It should be noticed the importance
of the sizing of the storage for avoiding the extra use of water and
energy. Case 2 shows how a big level of storage reduces the size of the
CHP units, and consequently the use of fuel, as well as the water con-
sumption.
Cases 4–7, where are used simultaneously diﬀerent design objec-
tives, shows an important ﬂexibility. The Pareto front, see Fig. 3, shows
Cases 5, 6 and 7 in the same region of the utopia point (UP). It means
that it is possible to ﬁnd the optimal trade-oﬀs between the objectives
considered in this approach. Case 7 presents a suitable trade-oﬀfor the
diﬀerent objective functions with a minimal diﬀerence with respect to
the UP. The used land, for this case, does not have diﬀerence. Incre-
ments in costs and water consumption are observed.
The energy-water nexus is a signiﬁcant aspect on the results. Most of
the water extracted from the local source is not used for the water
supply of the households, it is sent back to the water stream. It implies
an important surplus of power and water, which are not stored or used
by the system. In this work, it has been addressed just the domestic
consumption of energy utilities, future works can include the use of
these surplus of water and energy in local production as irrigation of
crops or cattle rising.
A common assumption is the use of ICE as the core of CHP systems,
where the focus of the design problem is the economic factor [60].
However, in the presented case the predominant technology is the MT.
Cases 3 and 4 use a combination of diﬀerent technologies, which causes
the diﬀerences in thermal and electric eﬃciencies and productions. The
presented case study shows a predominant thermal proﬁle, and con-
sidering the pumping needs plus the electric demand compared with
hot water demand, a technology with a predominant production of
thermal energy and ﬂexibility on partial load operation is selected over
the rest of the equipment [61]. Therefore, it is important the inclusion
of diﬀerent technologies in the optimal design of energy systems, for
considering the additional factors as energy loads, behavior of con-
sumption proﬁles and environmental conditions [29].
All the scenarios present a positive TAC. The added economic in-
dicators as the Net Present Value (NPV) and the Return of the
Investment (ROI) present a negative value. From a purely economic
point of view, it is not a desirable behavior. Despite that the costs are
reduced by the optimization process, especially in Cases 1, 5, 6 and 7.
For a common investor, the implementation of this system could be not
the best option. However, the government subsides applied to low-
carbon technologies incentives and policies for rural development can
be an alternative for reducing the economic impact of the proposed oﬀ-
grid CHP system [62]. Also, as was mentioned above, the surplus of
water can be used for irrigation producing extra incomes and beneﬁts.
The land used by the equipment, without considering extra planning
spaces, is reduced compared with similar alternatives as solar or wind
technologies, reducing the environmental impact and social conﬂicts
associated with the development of energy projects that use large tracts
of land [63].
Previous works have addressed the sensitivity of the performance of
CHP systems to the operational policy (including the scheduling op-
eration of the CHP unit, behavior of the storage and energy dispatch to
diﬀerent users) [21,64]. In this study, Cases 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 present a
similar selection of the CHP unit, considering the size and type of unit.
A remarkable condition is presented in Cases 5 and 7, they have the
same conﬁguration, but the diﬀerence is the operational policy. Case 5
is deﬁned by the preferences in economic and used land minimization,
meanwhile Case 7 takes in account all the diﬀerent objectives for
trading-oﬀthem. As a result, the cases are diﬀerent in the costs and
water consumption. Case 5 prefers to reduce the economic impact of the
system over the water consumption; then, the operation of the system
increases the level of energy and water sales. The increment in power
production increases the water used by the thermal load of the CHP unit
and the water pumped by the system.
Case 7 represents the equality in preferences with respect all the
considered objectives. As consequence, it is the nearest solution to the
utopia point and the suitable trade-oﬀbetween the multiple objectives.

6. Conclusions
An alternative approach for designing optimal water and energy
networks in low-income and oﬀ-grid communities is presented. The
approach allows the use of power surplus for extracting, transporting
and storing fresh water, through a pumping system, instead of the use of
electric storage. The advantages of the presented system are the addi-
tion of the water supply to the CHP system utilities.
The presented multi-objective MINLP model allows the selection of
prime movers as well as the size of the storage and pumping units. It
takes into account non-linear factors including the thermal storage
dynamics and the partial load conditions of the CHP units. Also, the
multi-objective approach, based on compromise solutions, deﬁnes the
design priorities and trade-oﬀs considering economic cost, water con-
sumption and land use of the water-energy system.
A case study from an oﬀ-grid and low income community from
Mexico was presented. The results show low costs and trade-oﬀregions
near to the utopia point with a surplus of water extraction, which can
be used on agricultural activities as irrigation and low land use. In
addition, the presented cases illustrate the impact of the operational
policy and the size of the storage for improving the economic perfor-
mance and water consumption of the proposed system.
