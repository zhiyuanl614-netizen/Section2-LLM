Optimal Design of Water Tank Size for Power System
Flexibility and Water Quality
Yiming Yao
, Chunyan Li
, Member, IEEE, Kaigui Xie
, Senior Member, IEEE,
Heng-Ming Tai
, Senior Member, IEEE, Bo Hu
, Member, IEEE, and Tao Niu
, Member, IEEE
Abstract—The increasing penetration of renewable energy, such
as wind power, has brought great challenges to the power system
operation due to its uncertainty. Flexibility, which measures the
ability of power system to deal with the uncertainties, is critical
for power system to adapt to the new era of renewable energy. The
rising electrical demand of the water distribution system (WDS)
creates opportunities for power system to leverage the ﬂexibility
provided by WDS. This paper investigates the water-energy re-
lationship between these two systems and makes full use of the
water pumps and tanks in the WDS to enhance the power system
ﬂexibility. Current WDS designs did not consider well the role
WDS plays in the power system. This hinders the WDS from being
fully used by power system to provide ﬂexibility. An optimization
model is proposed to determine the optimal tank size of WDS,
which may provide the maximum available ﬂexibility of power
system. The effect of tank size on water quality is also investigated
to ensure that the supplied water quality is not compromised.
Moreover, a Benders-based heuristic algorithm is proposed to ﬁnd
the optimization solution more efﬁciently and to protect the data
of each energy system. Results of case study highlight the merit of
the proposed optimization design and the advantage of using WDS
to provide ﬂexibility for power system.
Index Terms—Wind power, power system, water distribution
system, ﬂexibility, optimization.

I. INTRODUCTION
R
ENEWABLE energy is estimated to meet 80% of global
electricity demand growth during the next decade and is
expected to overtake the coal by 2025 as the primary means
of producing electricity [1]. Renewable energy such as wind
power, however, is uncertain and highly volatile compared with
the power generated by conventional thermal generating units.
With the increasing penetration of wind power, great challenges
on the imbalance of supply and demand have put forward high
requirements for the ﬂexibility of power system.
Flexibility is referred to as the capability of power system to
cope with the uncertainty that arises from variable renewable
generation [2]. Power system requires adequate ﬂexibility to
cope with the changing wind power. If not, events of wind power
curtailment or electrical demand curtailment will occur and
the power system may face huge economic losses. Inadequate
ﬂexibility is the main cause of the curtailment [3]. Thus, power
system ﬂexibility has become a global priority, as stated by
International Energy Agency in their 2018 report [4]. It is in
urgent need of ﬁnding effective means to ensure the ﬂexible
operation of power system.
WDS has been used to regulate the electricity consumption
curve and promote the accommodation of wind power by co-
ordinating the operation of water pumps and tanks [5]. WDS
accounts for 5% of the annual total electricity consumption in
China, and more than 15% in California, USA [6]. Electrical
demand associated with WDS is projected to increase. There
are a few pilot programs utilizing WDS to provide ﬂexibility for
power system [7]–[11]. WDS can provide frequency regulation
services, reduce the peak load, and balance the wind power
integration. In California, a water utility, which owns a WDS and
a wastewater treatment plant, can provide 5.3 MW interruptible
load capacity for up to 2 hours, resulting in more than $400000
annual revenues for the utility [9]. Thus, it is of great beneﬁt to
fully make use of the WDS to improve the ﬂexibility of power
system and to enhance the wind power accommodation.
Optimal operational strategies of integrating WDS into the
power system have been studied [12]–[16]. As an essential
component of the WDS, water tank plays an important role in
the optimal schedule of electricity consumption. The WDS can
pump water into the tank for storage when the wind is abundant
and release the stored water to meet the demand. Water tank can
be regarded as an auxiliary storage device for the power system.
The optimal tank design scheme may provide the maximum
available ﬂexibility of power system.
The water tank design in WDS has been widely studied. For
example, the pros and cons of various tank design models were
analyzed [17]. The network reduction approach, hydraulic simu-
lation, and mathematical optimization were combined to support
the planning process for tanks [18]. The size and location of the
watertankcouldbeoptimizedbyincorporatingtheEpanetsolver
into the decision process [19]. The optimization model of WDS
is formulated as a mixed-integer quadratically-constrained pro-
gramming (MIQCP) problem, which can be solved efﬁciently by
heuristic algorithms [20]. Multi-objective optimization methods
have been applied to the tank design considering factors such
as cost, greenhouse gas emissions and reliability [21]–[24]. The
above tank design schemes mainly consider the characteristics
of WDS, and the effect of WDS connected to power system has
not been taken into account.
To the best of our knowledge, not many researches on the
WDS tank design incorporating wind power into the power sys-
tem have been reported. Although larger tank size allows more
water to be stored and more wind power can be accommodated,
it may have adverse effect on water quality. Residual chlorine
concentration is one of the most important indicators to evaluate
the water quality. It should be maintained above the minimum
threshold at each water node. If not, harmful microorganisms
will breed and the water will not be safe anymore. Considering
the interaction between WDS and power system, the optimal
tank design scheme can be determined, which maximizes the
deliverability of WDS energy ﬂexibility. The upgraded WDS
is a promising solution to the power system in deﬁciency of
ﬂexibility.
This paper proposes an optimization model of water tank size
design for power system ﬂexibility and water quality. The design
model is a two-step process. The ﬁrst step generates the ﬂexibil-
ity requirements considering wind power and electrical demand
uncertainties. Then the optimal tank size and the operation of
WDS are determined according to the ﬂexibility requirements
and water quality criterion.
In this study, power system ﬂexibility refers to the ability
of a power system in response to the variations in electrical
demand and supply due to the uncertainty of variable renewable
energy. Inadequate ﬂexibility would force the power system to

The water quality properties of the WDS bring nonlinear par-

into a mixed-integer programming (MIP) problem, which can
be solved efﬁciently.
The main contributions of this paper are
a) Develop an electricity-water model to leverage the energy
ﬂexibility provided by WDS and facilitate effective incor-

b) Propose a method for optimal water tank design con-
sidering power system ﬂexibility and water quality. A
Benders-based heuristic algorithm is developed to solve
the optimization problem efﬁciently and to protect the data
privacy of each energy system.

model is described in Section II. Section III presents the solution
method using the Benders-based algorithm. Case studies are
given in Section IV to verify the advantages of the proposed
model. The paper is concluded in Section V.
II. THE PROPOSED OPTIMIZATION MODEL
This section presents the optimization model of water tank
size design in the WDS for power system ﬂexibility and water
quality. The ultimate goal is to minimize the annual investment
and operating costs. Prior to the detailed description of the
proposed optimization model, the structure of the optimization
model is outlined.
A. Optimization Structure
1) Two-Step Model for Tank Size Optimization: The WDS
optimization is a two-step model for water tank size optimiza-
tion. The process is depicted in Fig. 1. In the ﬁrst step, ﬂexibility
requirement scenarios are generated, which reﬂect the upward
and downward ﬂexibility requirements of power system.
Step 2 determines the optimal tank size of WDS under the
ﬂexibility requirement scenarios, water demand scenarios, and
the water quality requirement. The operation of pumps and tanks
are coordinated in the WDS to adjust the electricity consumption
so that the ﬂexibility requirements of power system can be met.
Fig. 2.
Typical daily value and uncertainty range.
Fig. 3.
Two-stage scenario-based optimization framework.
The tank size should not be too large to guarantee good water
quality.
2) Generation of Scenarios: The seasonal impact is consid-
ered by dividing the historical data of wind power, electrical
demand and water demand into four seasons with a 24-hour
proﬁle. Seasonal scenarios are then generated by using the
GAMS SCENRED tool based on Backward/Forward algorithm
[25]. The corresponding wind power, electrical demand and
water demand can be found in each seasonal scenario.
The variations of wind power and electrical demand in real-
time operation are limited by the uncertainty range, as shown
in Fig. 2. The study resolution is 15 minutes in this paper, and
there are 96 time steps in each scenario.
The volatility of water load is much lower than that of wind
power and electrical load. The variation of water demand in
real-time operation is neglected in many water tank planning
researches [18]–[24]. To simplify calculations, this paper does
not consider it either. In the future, research will be conducted
to model the water demand variations.
A two-stage robust optimization (TSRO) method is used to
ﬁnd the scenarios where power system is in urgent need of ﬂexi-
bility. The worst-case wind power output and electrical demand
in the real-time operation that jeopardize the operation cost of
power system are found by considering the typical seasonal
value and uncertainty range as shown in Fig. 2. In the worst-case
scenario, the ﬂexibility requirements during each time period
can be determined by calculating the amount of wind power
or electrical demand curtailments. Mathematical formulations
of the TSRO method and detailed process of generating the
ﬂexibility requirement scenarios are given in Section II-B below.
3) Two-Stage Scenario-Based Tank Size Optimization: The
optimal tank size is determined through a two-stage scenario-
based optimization method, as seen in Fig. 3. The investment

C. WDS Optimization
1) Overview of WDS: The structure of a WDS is shown in
Fig. 5. The reservoir serves as the source of water for WDS. The
water ﬂowis transportedfromonenodetoanother inthepipedue
to the head pressure difference between two nodes. The pump is
used when the pressure difference is not enough. Valves control
the pressure in a pipe or the amount of water ﬂowing through
a pipe. The water tank can store water or supply water into the
system.
The water pump in a WDS consumes electricity to increase
the pressure difference and transport the water ﬂow. More water
can be conveyed when the electricity consumption is increased.
The water can be stored in the tank to ensure the balance between
supply and demand. Thus, WDS can be regarded as an adjustable
electrical load from the perspective of power system. Optimal
control of the water pump and tank enables WDS to deal with
the ﬂexibility requirements of power system and to incorporate
more wind power.

III. SOLUTION METHODOLOGY
Many commercial solvers such as Gurobi are available for
power system solutions. But they are not applicable to the min-
max-min optimization problem of (1) and the nonlinear partial
differential equation (NLPDE) in the water distribution system
(WDS) model [32]. Thus, we apply the C&CG algorithm and
propose a Benders-based heuristic algorithm to transform the
min-max-min optimization problem of (1) and NLPDE into the
MIP problems so that the commercially available solvers can be
applied. Details are given below.

B. Larger-Scale Test System
A realistic power system in a province of Northwest China is
used to verify the superiority of the proposed tank size design.
The power system has 93 load buses and 179 transmission lines.
The installed wind power capacity is 3667.2 MW, accounting
for 12% of the total generating capacity. The “D-Town” WDS
with 443 pipes is used [39], which often serves as benchmarks
for water network design methods. Each load bus in the power
system is connected with a WDS. Four typical seasonal sce-
narios are generated using the GAMS SCENRED tool, and the
occurrence probability of each scenario equals to 0.25. The tank
size should be at least 3500 m3 according to the governmental
requirements. The water quality requirements, initial condi-
tions of residual chlorine, and the ﬂuctuation ranges of wind
power and electrical demand are the same as the test system in
Section A.

tions referred in Section A are considered again. Table VIII

be seen from Table VIII that both the upward and downward
ﬂexibilityrequirementscanbereducedgreatlywiththeproposed
tank size design scheme. The AS cost in each scenario is reduced
by about 80% to 100% after the ﬂexible operation of WDS with
the optimized tank size. This validates the economic merit of the
proposed model.

V. CONCLUSION
This paper has presented an optimization model of WDS
to enhance the power system ﬂexibility and incorporate more
wind power. The proposed model generates the ﬂexibility re-
quirements considering wind power and electrical demand un-
certainties, then ﬁnds the optimal size of water tank based on
the ﬂexibility and water quality requirements. A Benders-based
heuristic algorithm is developed to facilitate the solutions with
a master-slave framework. The master problem is solved in the
WDS setting and the slave problem via the power system. Since
only limited information needs to be shared, data privacy can be
protected.
Results from case studies have veriﬁed that the optimized tank
size can enhance the ﬂexibility of the power system. Both the
amount of wind power curtailment and that of the electrical
demand curtailment are reduced signiﬁcantly. Moreover, the
