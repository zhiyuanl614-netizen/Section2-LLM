Abstract—The growing penetration of distributed generation
(DG) and use of combined heat and power (CHP) techniques
have made electrical distribution systems and water distribu-
tion systems increasingly interdependent. This paper proposes
an integrated dispatch model that offers a generalized means
for
determining
the
potential
accommodated
DG
capacity
along with appropriate DG sitting for given urban electricity
and water system conﬁgurations. The AC optimal power ﬂow
(ACOPF)-based system model was developed for each phase of
a modiﬁed electrical distribution test feeder. A corresponding
water distribution system was then designed with its mathematical
model developed. A mathematical model of the CHP-based DG
system was also developed to couple the electricity and water
systems. Results indicate the optimal DG capacity and its optimal
sitting based on urban electricity and water infrastructures. The
present water network cannot support the complete recovery
of the exhaust heat. The mutual impacts on operational perfor-
mances of CHP-based DG units and urban energy infrastructures
were also investigated.
Index Terms—Combined heat and power (CHP), distributed
generation (DG), load ﬂow analysis, mathematical programming.

I. INTRODUCTION
E
NGINEERING resilient and sustainable urban energy
infrastructures is a prerequisite for continual urban
growth, made challenging due to the interdependency of the
conﬁning physical and social environment. Currently, rapidly
evolving environmental concerns and deregulated market
forces are driving distributed generation (DG) penetration into
distribution systems. This is changing the way that traditional
centralized electric power is supplied based on transmission
lines and substations. Additionally, DG technologies promise to
be efﬁcient, environmentally friendly, and dependable electric
power sources [1]–[3].
One important application of DG technologies is the com-
bined heat and power (CHP). To promote energy efﬁciency,
many gas-ﬁred DG units, e.g., microturbines, fuel cells, internal
combustion (IC) engines, etc., can provide thermal energy by re-
covering the exhaust heat during electricity generation, which
could be used to provide hot water, air conditioning, and dis-
trict heat supply. The CHP technique has been mature in today’s
market for industrial and commercial applications. Meanwhile,
it is also making inroads into residential areas, where the water
supply from water distribution networks explicitly affects the
captured DG thermal energy [4], [5].
A wide variety of mathematical optimization-based studies
have been conducted in the literature on network DG capacity
analysis, sitting, and sizing problems. Approaches based on AC
optimal power ﬂow (ACOPF) models, presented in [6]–[8], con-
sider intrinsic nonlinearities of DG locations and capacity anal-
ysis, in which DG connection points are ﬁxed and the maximum
DG power injections are evaluated. In [9] and [10], analytical
approaches are developed to solve DG sitting and sizing prob-
lems. Multiobjective-based optimization approaches are pro-
posed for generation capacity planning in [11] and [12]. In [13],
gradient and second-order methods are used to determine DG
sitting based on loss minimization.
In the urban environment, electricity and water infrastruc-
tures have developed independently. However, with growing
DG penetration and employment of CHP techniques, they are
becoming more interconnected. A number of publications have
investigated the connections between electricity and water dis-
tribution systems in areas of microgrid related optimization, dis-
trict heating and cooling, etc.. However, few studies have been
conducted to investigate the network capacity of CHP-based
DG in the context of urban electricity and water infrastructures.
In reference [14], the integrated load ﬂow method is used in a
simple case study to examine the interconnected electric power
and district heating networks. Speciﬁcally, a nine node micro-
grid feeder with only one location-ﬁxed CHP unit and an eleven
node district heating network are used in this study. In [9] and
[10], an approach for integrated optimal power ﬂow for energy
infrastructures, e.g., electric power, gas and district heating, is
presented and the energy hub concept is ﬁrst introduced as the
coupling point between different energy infrastructures. A three
bus system and a corresponding three node natural gas system
are also used to examine the optimal dispatch of multiple energy
carriers. Finally, a network ﬂow optimization model is devel-
oped in [15] for addressing the economic and physical integrity
of the national electric power system by integrating the electric
power, gas, coal, and water systems; this study, however, is fo-
cused on the transmission level rather than the distribution level.
Based on the above research, a clear need emerges for the
development of a model architecture to provide planning guid-
ance, which integrates electricity and water distribution systems
with the coupling of the CHP-based DG units. This is espe-
cially valuable in urban energy planning. To the best knowledge
of the authors of this paper, no general optimization technique
regarding the network capacity assessment of CHP-based DG
has been published based on relatively large distribution energy
systems. It is the objective of this paper to introduce a gener-
alized approach that investigates optimal DG capacity and sit-
ting, and analyzes mutual impacts on operational performances
of CHP-based DG units, and urban electricity and water infra-
structures.
II. ELECTRICAL SYSTEM MODEL DEVELOPMENT
A. Electrical System Selection and Modiﬁcation
The IEEE 123 node test feeder [16] was selected as the elec-
trical system for this study, which is a typical radial distribution
feeder, as shown in Fig. 1. Reasonable assumptions were made
to change it to a looped system with improved reliability and
increased DG capacity accommodation. By adding external AC
power sources (ExGens) and connecting nodes 51 and 300, the
original radial feeder is divided into several sections. Each sec-
tion is one subfeeder connecting two ExGens with a switch at
one side of the subfeeder. The modiﬁed looped system is still
not a complete three-phase system. To make the result eval-
uation more accurate, the ACOPF formulation was applied to
each phase. Consequently, the nodal DG output is represented
by summing up nodal DG electrical output and thermal output
of all three phases.
B. ACOPF Formulation
A group of CHP-based DG units were assumed to be placed
at each node with the capacity from zero to a set value, which
is multiple times of the base DG unit. The DG electric power
output and thermal output are treated as continuous variables
with both lower and upper capacity bounds. The lower capacity
bounds were defaulted as zero.
1) Objective Function: The objective of the integrated
electrical and water systems is to maximize the CHP-based

III. WATER SYSTEM DESIGN AND MODEL DEVELOPMENT
In this section, according to the electrical system, a general-
ized urban water distribution system was designed and corre-
sponding mathematical model was developed as well.
A. Water Network Design
In the water network, each node was assumed to represent a
cluster of houses. The water demand was estimated based on
the counted number of nodal households and the node loading
information given by the electrical system.
Optimization techniques are often used to obtain a design
of water distribution network. A genetic algorithm based opti-
mization tool (GANetXL) [17] in combination with a hydraulic
solver EPANET [18], is used to design a representative water
distribution network by minimizing costs to choose appropriate
pipe diameters. The ﬂowchart of this design process is shown
in Fig. 2.
The designed water distribution network corresponding to the
electrical system is shown in Fig. 3. Each node in the water
network corresponds to the same node in the electrical system. A
reservoir is located in node 118 to provide all the water demand
at each node. A pump is located between node 118 and node
250 to increase the water head up to the designed value (50 m)
in node 250. This is to ensure that for all existing nodal water
demand, the nodal water head is above the minimum required
value. Two pressure reducing valves (PRV) are also included
into this network to restrict the outﬂow.
B. Water System Model Development
To integrate the water system and electrical system, a mathe-
matical model of the water system was developed based on the
designed water distribution network.

IV. CHP-BASED DG MODEL
A. CHP-Based Microturbines
The CHP-based DG units are used as coupling points between
electrical system and water system. The energy efﬁciency of
microturbines can be boosted up from 30–40% to 85% with the
exhaust heat recovered [20].
The exhaust heat from microturbines travels through the heat
exchanger, normally the isolated or integrated part of microtur-
bines. The pressurized water by the pump ﬂows inside the heat
exchanger, used as a medium to carry away part of the exhaust
heat. The water ﬂow operates as a looped circulating process
and a certain amount of water is extracted from the water net-
work periodically or in peak thermal demand besides the nodal
existing water consumption. This additional water consumption
by DG units is usually only a one-time use.
The microturbine C65 [21] was selected as a reference for
CHP-based DG unit. The capacities of these series of products
are available in a wide range, which can be modularized and
show similar electrical and thermal characteristics as the se-
lected reference unit.

V. PROBLEM SOLVING, CASE STUDIES, AND DISCUSSION
A. Implementation
The integrated system model was coded and implemented as
a nonlinear mathematical programming (MP) problem in the
AIMMS optimization modeling environment [24]. The MP was
generated by AIMMS from the model structure and data, and
then passed to the selected efﬁcient commercial solver—the
CONOPT 3.14V generalized reduced gradient solver. In its de-
fault settings, CONOPT 3.14V has proven to be competitive
with the KNITRO interior point solver; it has shown reliability
and efﬁciency in convergence [25]. Normally it is not guar-
anteed for nonlinear solvers to ﬁnd the global optimum. The
returned solution most often is a local optimum, or possibly
no feasible solution can be found. The global solver such as
Baron is time-consuming and usually only works well on rela-
tively small NLP problems. In addition to the selected nonlinear
solver, the nonlinear presolver and the multistart procedure [26]
were also used to reduce problem size, search space, and in-
crease chances of ﬁnding the global optimum by comparing and
tracking all found feasible solutions.
The integrated system solving ﬂowchart is shown in Fig. 4.
The electrical system model was optimized ﬁrst for each single
phase to ﬁnd the maximum DG electrical output. The resultant
nodal DG electrical output is then used as the input parameters
of upper bounds for the thermal constraints, as shown in (19)
and (21). The optimal DG thermal output is achieved simulta-
neously when the electrical system model is optimized. How-
ever, as for the maximum DG recovered thermal output, it is
not only constrained by the electrical system model, but also by
the water system model and CHP-based DG system model. If
the minimum nodal water pressure is violated, then less water
will be extracted by DG units from water network to carry away
the thermal energy from exhaust heat. Subsequently, the DG re-
covered thermal output is reduced accordingly. This iteration
continues until no nodal water pressure violation occurs.

C. Discussion
The integrated mathematical model was solved as a nonlinear
optimization problem. A local returned optimum may not deﬁ-
nitely represent the global optimum. Additionally, it is also im-
practical and not guaranteed to ﬁnd a global optimum for any
large nonlinear planning problems. The algorithms of nonlinear
presolve and multistart were employed and only a local solu-
tion was found, which proved to be the best results generated
and are likely to be the global results. For this speciﬁc planning
problem, a technique with an achievable solution provides more
value than a global optimum which cannot be guaranteed for
other cases [6]. It should be noted that in a distribution system
without many meshed loops, such as that shown in Fig. 1, it is
highly possible that a single local optimum exists.
It has been demonstrated that for all the cases studied: extra
electrical output from DG units is available besides that used
to meet the system load; however, the CHP application cannot
be employed at every node and the existing water distribution
network cannot support the complete recovery of exhaust heat.
The performance of the integrated system depends on multiple
factors. For real deployment, other concerns, e.g., investment,
income, and location availability, should also be taken into ac-
count to size DG units. In reality, the PFs of most gas-ﬁred DG
units can be speciﬁed according to customer requirements. Fur-
thermore, the adjustable indoor or outdoor temperature is sug-
gested to provide an, ensuring that the gas-ﬁred DG units are
operated in optimal climatic conditions. Finally, the determina-
tion of water head limit during the planning period has to take
into account present water demand, additional demand and po-
tential thermal applications.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
The methodology developed for the integrated mathematical
model is novel as this is the ﬁrst instance of assessing the
CHP-based DG network capacity by coupling generalized
electrical distribution system, water distribution system and
gas-ﬁred CHP-based DG system. The problem of this DG
capacity maximization is different from cost minimization
problems as more congestion may appear not only in the
electrical network, but also in the water network. The proposed
method is generic and can also be used in a radial distribution
system, as shown in [5]. The CHP-based DG units are placed at
every node, rather than being ﬁxed into some nodes, and func-
tion as continuous decision variables. This solution approach
is new and being ﬁrst employed in the DG network capacity
assessment based on urban energy infrastructures.
